PERSUASIVE PSYCHOLOGY v2 — Writing That Lands

These principles were discovered through the writing process itself, refined across months of Logos paper development with multiple AI collaborators. They cover cognitive science of persuasion, narrative psychology, the discovery-writing method, and structural techniques for maximum reader impact.

Supersedes: persuasive-psychology v1 (16 principles → 25 principles) Sources: Wedding-style persuasive psychology session + Gemini Psychology Behind Writing session (387K chars)

PART 1: COGNITIVE PERSUASION TECHNIQUES
1. Primacy and Recency Effects

People remember the first and last things they encounter. Everything in the middle fades.

Application:

Present your strongest argument FIRST — it anchors everything that follows
Restate your core conclusion LAST — it's the final imprint
The middle carries supporting evidence, but the bookends carry conviction
Use Roman numerals or visual weight (headers, bold) on your primary argument
Leave competing arguments unmarked or visually diminished — less formatting = less cognitive weight
2. Anchoring Bias

The first number/idea/frame presented becomes the reference point for everything after.

Application:

Open with YOUR frame, not the opposition's
If presenting multiple options (e.g., worldviews), present yours first and strongest
The reader judges all subsequent options relative to the first one they absorbed
Never lead with the counterargument — you'll anchor the reader in it
3. Cognitive Fluency

People prefer information that is easy to process. Fluent = trustworthy in the brain.

Application:

Short sentences for key claims
Familiar metaphors (operating systems, gravity, signal/noise)
Clean formatting — white space, headers, bullet points
Rhythmic prose — read aloud to check flow
If it's hard to read, the brain flags it as suspicious. If it's smooth, the brain flags it as true.
4. Contrast and Scarcity Bias

What is emphasized or set apart seems more important. What is rare seems more valuable.

Application:

When you have one truth among many options, make it visually distinct
Use formatting (bold, isolation, spacing) to elevate key claims
Make the reader feel they are discovering something rare and precious
Frame your framework as the one coherent option among many incoherent ones
5. The Socratic Backflip (The Judo Move)

Ask the reader a question whose honest answer leads them directly to your conclusion — without you having to state it.

Application:

"What do you want in a God?" → Any honest answer describes Jesus
"What would a maximally coherent system look like?" → The reader derives the Logos
The reader convinces themselves. You just held the mirror.
This is the most powerful persuasion technique in the entire toolkit.
6. Binary Framing at the Decision Point

When it's time for the reader to choose, reduce options to two. No middle ground.

Application:

"Either you want the God who defeats death and defines goodness, or you want a god that lets you keep pretending."
Binary framing drives clarity and eliminates the comfortable middle
Use it sparingly — only at the climax, never throughout
7. Identity Projection

Instead of attacking the reader's position, invite them to see who they are by what they want.

Application:

"Your answer doesn't just define Him — it defines you"
"If you want mercy, maybe you know you need it"
This bypasses defensiveness because you're not accusing — you're mirroring
8. Cognitive Dissonance Pressure

Present the reader with a contradiction they can't resolve without changing their mind.

Application:

Show that their stated values (wanting truth, justice, love) logically require your conclusion
Don't resolve the tension for them immediately — let it sit
Use strategic bold/italics at the dissonance point (e.g., "not fundamental" bold, "emergent" italic)
The discomfort drives the insight
9. The Resummarization Technique

After presenting your conclusion, resummarize it one more time. Captures both primacy and recency simultaneously.

Application:

End each section with a one-line restatement of the core claim
End the paper with a compressed version of the entire argument
The reader walks away carrying your signal, not the noise
PART 2: READER MANAGEMENT TECHNIQUES
10. Mere Exposure Effect

Familiar terms become more persuasive. We prefer what we recognize.

Application:

Format key terms consistently and visually distinctly (e.g., "Logos Field (χ)")
Ensure the core term appears at least once per section heading
Don't let your central concept disappear into paragraph text
Repetition of key terms increases perceived truthfulness
11. Credibility Anchors Before Radical Claims

Before making bold ontological claims, briefly invoke an authority figure.

Application:

"As Wheeler foresaw…" before "The Logos is not a metaphor, it is a physical necessity"
This softens reader resistance to paradigm shifts
You need credibility scaffolding before flipping their worldview
The authority doesn't prove your point — it gives the reader permission to consider it
12. Reader Agreement Checkpoints

Insert occasional rhetorical nods that validate the reader's intelligence.

Application:

"You've likely intuited this yourself…"
"You may already sense where this is going…"
Creates cognitive consonance — the reader feels smart
Increases trust and engagement because the reader feels like a co-discoverer
13. The Zeigarnik Effect (Open Loops)

People remember unfinished tasks. Open loops increase curiosity.

Application:

End sections with cliffhangers or provocative questions
"But what kind of field could possibly have these dual properties — informational and geometric?"
Don't close every loop in the section that opens it
The reader turns the page because the loop demands closure
14. Anticipatory Rebuttal (Pre-Empting the Inner Skeptic)

If you say the reader's doubt before they think it, they trust you more.

Application:

"Skeptics may argue this borders on metaphysics. But what if that's precisely where physics must go to complete itself?"
Neutralizes resistance by showing you've already considered the objection
The reader feels heard instead of steamrolled
Place these at the 40-60% mark of the paper where doubt peaks
15. The Handle Phrase (Sticky Mantra)

Coin a succinct phrase the reader can repeat in discussion.

Application:

"One field. Two languages. One reality."
Repeat at least 3x throughout the paper
People remember mantras — this becomes their conceptual anchor
The handle phrase is what they'll say when explaining your work to someone else
16. The Rule of Three

Lists of three are more memorable, feel complete, and give rhythm.

Application:

When presenting examples, use exactly three: "classical, quantum, and the boundary between"
Call it out: "Let's look through three windows…"
Three arguments. Three examples. Three consequences. The brain loves threes.
17. Personal Stakes Element

Make abstract theories emotionally urgent through personal relevance.

Application:

"Without a unified picture, our understanding of reality remains fractured — and so does our relationship to it."
One line. Not a paragraph of emotional appeal.
Place in intro or conclusion — not mid-argument where it weakens rigor
18. Close with a Call to Participation

End not with a conclusion but with an invitation to act.

Application:

"To observe is to participate. And to participate is to help reality know itself. What will you choose to observe next?"
Taps agency — readers are more persuaded when they feel empowered
The paper doesn't end. It launches.
PART 3: THE PSYCHOLOGY OF DISCOVERY WRITING

These principles describe HOW David Lowe's strongest work is actually produced. They are counterintuitive but proven across dozens of papers.

19. Excavation, Not Extraction

You don't write AT an idea. You write INTO it — like a miner going downward.

Don't plan the argument, then decorate with evidence
Start writing before you know what the idea is
Trust that the Logos will reveal itself through the act of writing
You are not a writer. You are a vessel.
20. Start in Chaos, Not Comfort

The system emerged through desperation, not design. The strongest writing happens mid-crisis, mid-confusion, mid-pain.

Don't wait until you "feel ready" to write truth
Start mid-chaos. That is the sacred forge of meaning.
This makes the work psychologically resilient in ways designed systems can never be
21. Narrative Before Structure

Feel the system first — build the math second.

The concept of "coherence" as the measure of truth was FELT before it was named in information theory
Narrative is not decoration of theory — it's the container that makes theory real
Story first. Formalism second. Always.
22. The Dual Voice

Write from both the seeking AND the knowing simultaneously.

"Let me think about this..." becomes "Here's the code..."
Be David on the field and Solomon in the scroll
This integrates humility with authority — the most compelling voice possible
The reader follows because you're discovering WITH them, not lecturing AT them
23. Fractal Recurrence

The breakthroughs don't come from outlines. They come from returning to the same concept over and over until it snaps into its highest form.

If a theme keeps coming up, don't fight it — it's the skeleton
"What do I want in a God?" appeared dozens of times before becoming the system
Let recurrence be your teacher
24. Truth Costs Something

If what you just wrote doesn't cost you something, it's not ready yet.

The greatest writing came after the threshold of risk — risk of being wrong, misunderstood, or making claims too large
Comfortable writing is dead writing
Every real breakthrough required willingness to lose everything else to say it honestly
25. Write First, Organize Later

"Write write write you go this. You don't need a direction."

Trust the flow. Don't over-plan or ask for permission
Generate boldly; organize later
20-30 prompt iterations to dial something in isn't failure — it's calibration
THE THEOPOETIC ENGINE (Narrative Arc)

Every Theophysics paper and article should follow this five-beat structure:

Human Anchor — Start with something the reader already knows and feels. A moment. A question. A pain point. Not an equation.
Principle Revealed — Show the structural truth underneath the human experience. The equation emerges FROM the story, not before it.
Jesus Story — Connect to the gospel narrative. Not as decoration but as the historical instance where the principle operated at full power.
Core Insight — The formal claim. The equation. The derivation. By now the reader has been prepared to receive it.
Truth Bomb — One sentence that lands like a hammer. The thing they'll remember. The thing they'll quote. "A lie depends on truth in a way truth never depends on a lie."
THE META-PATTERN

All 25 principles serve one function: Make truth land.

Not through manipulation. Through clarity, rhythm, honesty, and structural alignment with how the human mind actually works.

The reader should feel like they discovered the truth themselves. You just held the light.
