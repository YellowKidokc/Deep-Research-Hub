# The Loop Method

**Why we are building this into OpenWriter**

The best writers do the same thing every paragraph. They do not repeat themselves — they repeat *moves* that work. A Kait Willett opening almost always does something concrete: a sentence that turns, a small fact with a huge implication, an asymmetry nobody noticed, a scene you can see. The magic is not one trick. It is that she has a menu of tricks and she picks the right one for the paragraph's job.

Most writers, including me, do not have that menu visible while we write. We remember the opening trick for five minutes, then drift back into explanation. The loop method fixes this. It makes the menu visible, checkable, and reusable.

---

## What a loop is

A loop is a paragraph scaffold with one specific job.

It is not a template in the boring sense. It does not tell you what to say. It tells you what the paragraph is doing:

- **Scene before thesis** — drop the reader into something concrete before you tell them what it means.
- **The map** — lay out the three or four places the argument could go.
- **Mirror** — take the objection that just hit your side and show it hitting the other side just as hard.
- **Hardest objection** — concede the strongest version of the other view before you answer it.
- **Home question** — ask where a claimed structure lives if the rival view is true.

Each loop has a fill-in-the-blank shape, but the content is always mine. The loop is the move. The words are mine.

---

## Why this matters

Writing is easy to start and hard to finish well because memory fails. You remember to open strong, then you forget to map. You remember to rebut, then you forget to concede. You remember to build evidence, then you forget to land the point.

A checklist helps, but checklists are passive. You look at them once and stop. A loop is active. It sits in the right rail of OpenWriter and keeps floating back up until you use it. Every time you finish a paragraph, the system checks which loops you have used in the document. The unused ones stay visible. They nag you constructively.

This is the difference between "remember to be fair" and having a button called **Hardest objection** that asks you to fill it in.

---

## How it works in OpenWriter

The right rail has a **Loops** panel. It lists every loop in the current library:

- **Intro loops** — Kait Willett-style opening moves.
- **Body loops** — paragraph moves for the middle of the piece.

When I click a loop, it drops its scaffold at the cursor. I fill in the blanks. The system marks that loop as used for this document.

When I finish a paragraph, I can run a quick check. The system looks at the document and tells me which loops are still unused. It does not force me to use them. It just keeps them in front of me. If a loop keeps showing up unused and I keep ignoring it, that is a decision. The system makes the decision visible.

---

## Loops are combinable

A single paragraph can carry more than one loop. An opening can be a **Scene** plus a **What I'm not doing**. A rebuttal can be a **Mirror** plus a **Small fact**. The loops are not mutually exclusive categories; they are ingredients.

The API we are building will eventually recommend combinations. It will read the draft and say: "For this paragraph, try Scene + Map. For this rebuttal, try Mirror + Hardest objection." I decide whether to take the suggestion. The AI proposes; I dispose.

---

## The deeper point

Good writing looks like inspiration. It is not. It is a set of repeatable moves applied with judgment. The loop method makes the moves explicit without making the prose mechanical.

Kait Willett's best work feels effortless because she has internalized the moves. The loop method is how someone who has not yet internalized them can still produce work at the same structural level. It is training wheels that do not stay on the bike — they teach you the shape of balance until you can do it without thinking.

The goal is not to write like Kait. The goal is to have her *machinery* available while I write in my own voice about my own claims.
