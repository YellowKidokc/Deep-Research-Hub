# The Predicates of Truth: Writing & Story Craft

If we boil down all the examples, quotes, and rules across these four transcripts to their bedrock axioms—the fundamental truths about how stories and sentences work on a human brain—here is what remains:

---

## I. "Show, Don't Tell" (Immersion & Evidence)

* **Emotion cannot be transferred by naming it; it must be recreated through evidence.** 
  Telling a reader a character is "scared" or "happy" only passes along a label. To make the reader actually *feel* the emotion, you have to hand them the raw sensory or behavioral evidence (the $2 + 2$) and let their own brain calculate the conclusion (the $4$).
* **Readers want to participate, not just observe.** 
  Humans are natural problem-solvers who are drawn to a "well-organized absence of information." When a writer leaves small gaps between an action and its emotional meaning, the reader is forced to step inside the story to bridge the gap.
* **"Showing" is about removing the author as the middleman.** 
  Whenever prose sounds like the author stepping onto the stage to explain, judge, or summarize for the *reader's* benefit, immersion breaks. When prose stays strictly inside what the *character* notices, thinks, or experiences in real time, immersion holds.
* **Specificity is the engine of belief.** 
  Abstract adjectives (*eerie, wondrous, huge, cold*) are hollow labels. Concrete, specific effects (*leaves crunching, a nose burning in frigid air, a kitchen big enough for a whole family to live in*) give the reader's internal camera something physical to film.
* **Real-time thought is stronger than cliché body language.** 
  Physical shorthand (clenched fists, pounding hearts, rolling stomachs) often feels melodramatic because it only shows a generic physical symptom. Walking the reader through the exact, unfiltered rhythm of a character's thoughts shows *why* the emotion is happening.
* **Perspective shapes reality.** 
  Objective descriptions are dead weight. True showing means filtering the setting and exposition through the bias, background, and current mood of the person looking at it (e.g., how a Navy SEAL sees rain on a window versus how a scared child sees it).
* **Showing and telling are ultimately just pacing controls.** 
  Telling isn't "bad" and showing isn't "good." Showing slows time down to dramatize what matters; telling speeds time up to summarize what doesn't. A story without telling would never move forward, and a story without showing would never matter.
* **A strong enough narrative voice can turn "telling" into "showing."** 
  When a narrator tells a story with a distinct, flavorful attitude or worldview, the *way* they summarize the world becomes an act of showing their own character.

## II. Story Architecture (Antonya Nelson's 9 Steps)

* **Emotional weight comes from lived truth, not factual accuracy.** 
  Real life is the best starter dough because it carries genuine emotional gravity, but factual history is just a launchpad. You must be willing to bend, exaggerate, or invent stakes to serve the dramatic truth over the literal truth.
* **A story belongs to whoever has the most to lose, not necessarily the person it happened to.** 
  You don't truly understand a narrative event until you rotate the camera around the room. The "right" point of view is almost always the character—or outsider—whose core identity, happiness, or survival is most threatened by the outcome.
* **Time is a pressure vessel.** 
  Without a boundary or a countdown (a "ticking clock"), a story is just a series of events leaking energy into the void. Giving the reader a visible horizon—a deadline, an approaching event, or the end of a season—compresses the narrative and creates automatic suspense.
* **Abstract emotions require physical anchors.** 
  In fiction, objects are never just scenery; they are totems. Planting an unusual, recurring physical prop in a story gives invisible things (grief, ambition, love) a tangible weight that characters can hold, trade, or fight over.
* **Stories only exist at the fault lines of change.** 
  A story cannot happen inside a static status quo; it requires an *"and then one day..."* crux where a character is forced from one mode of existence into another. In short fiction, this shift doesn't have to be an explosion—it can be a quiet, internal tectonic slip.
* **Shared reality builds reader trust.** 
  Dropping a fictional story into a recognizable real-world event, cultural moment, or shared human ritual (like Thanksgiving dinner or a public scandal) gives the reader an immediate foothold and lends automatic believability ("veracity") to the fiction.
* **Conflict is generated by the distance between opposites.** 
  Tension doesn't come from characters being bad; it comes from binary forces rubbing against each other (innocent vs. experienced, open-minded vs. bigoted, hunter vs. prey). The wider you stretch the gap between two characters' worldviews, the more natural friction the story generates.
* **Structure is a tool for emotional release, not a prison.** 
  Traditional arcs (rising action, climax, resolution) exist because they reliably trigger human emotional payoff, while formal constraints and experiments (like telling a story through a recipe or an unreliable narrator) exist to shock the writer—and the reader—out of autopilot.

## III. Sentence Mastery (The 6-Step Formula)

* **A sentence does not exist in a vacuum; its value is relative to its neighbors.** 
  Like notes in a melody, a sentence only works by echoing or contrasting with the sentence right before it. Monotony isn't caused by bad sentences; it's caused by identical sentence lengths, rhythms, and structures stacked in a row.
* **The verb is the center of gravity.** 
  Nouns andverbs are the real engines of prose, while adjectives and adverbs are often just decorative clutter. Choosing a verb by instinct gives you clichés; deliberately placing an ordinary, active verb into an unexpected context (*sunflowers weeping, fog slithering*) wakes up the reader's brain.
* **Surprise comes from friction between ordinary words, not from a thesaurus.** 
  A "power word" doesn't mean a fancy, ten-dollar dictionary word. It means placing a simple, familiar word right next to something it normally fights with (*a "naked" laugh, a "bitter" prayer, an "insomniac" rain*). Context creates brilliance, not complexity.
* **The best sentences are miniature stories.** 
  Rather than acting as a flat conveyor belt of information, a great sentence has its own internal arc—setting up an expectation in the first half and inverting, deepening, or subverting it by the period.
* **Repetition is musical; redundancy is laziness.** 
  Repeating a word by accident or using two words that mean the same thing (*"collaborated together"*) weakens prose. Repeating a phrase deliberately (anaphora, epistrophe, or structural mirroring) builds rhythm, emotional momentum, and hypnosis.
* **Clarity requires subtraction.** 
  Every extra prepositional phrase, weak modifier, or filler word dilutes the impact of the words that actually matter. Cutting the fat—and being brave enough to drop a blunt, three-word sentence into a paragraph—sharpens the blade.

## IV. Sentence Beginnings & Camera Angles (The 8 Tips)

* **The first word of a sentence controls the camera's focus.** 
  When every sentence starts with *"I"* or the character's name (*"He did this, she saw that"*), the camera stays locked on the observer, creating a repetitive barrier between the reader and the world.
* **Give agency to the world, not just the observer.** 
  Instead of filtering every sensation through the character (`I heard gunshots` / `I smelled cheap perfume` / `I threw a punch`), let the sensory detail or the action become the subject that acts upon the scene (`Gunshots boomed` / `Cheap perfume filled the air` / `My punch sailed wide`).
* **Front-loading context changes how the action lands.** 
  Pushing *when*, *how*, or *under what condition* an action happens to the very front of the sentence (using a time phrase, a participial action, or a dependent clause) breaks up mechanical "Subject-Verb-Object" rhythms and sets the stage before the main action hits.
* **Never sacrifice natural human speech for the sake of variety.** 
  Varying sentence structure is meant to make prose flow smoothly, not sound like a riddle. If moving a phrase to the front of a sentence makes you sound like Yoda (*"Over the hurdle, I jumped"*), the invisible spell of the prose breaks.
