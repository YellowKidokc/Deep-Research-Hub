Theorem that arithmetical truth cannot be defined in arithmetic

**Tarski's undefinability theorem** , stated and proved by [Alfred Tarski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Tarski "Alfred Tarski") in 1933, is an important limitative result in [mathematical logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_logic "Mathematical logic"), the [foundations of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_mathematics "Foundations of mathematics"), and in formal [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics "Semantics"). Informally, the theorem states that "arithmetical truth cannot be defined in arithmetic".[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski%27s_undefinability_theorem#cite_note-1)
The theorem applies more generally to any sufficiently strong [formal system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_system "Formal system"), showing that truth in the standard model of the system cannot be defined within the system. 
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tarski%27s_undefinability_theorem&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")]
In 1931, [Kurt Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_G%C3%B6del "Kurt Gödel") published the [incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems "Gödel's incompleteness theorems"), which he proved in part by showing how to represent the syntax of formal logic within [first-order arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_arithmetic "First-order arithmetic"). Each expression of the formal language of arithmetic is assigned a distinct number. This procedure is known variously as [Gödel numbering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del_numbering "Gödel numbering"), _coding_ and, more generally, as arithmetization. In particular, various _sets_ of expressions are coded as sets of numbers. For various syntactic properties (such as _being a formula_ , _being a sentence_ , etc.), these sets are [computable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computable_set "Computable set"). Moreover, any computable set of numbers can be defined by some arithmetical formula. For example, there are formulas in the language of arithmetic defining the set of codes for arithmetic sentences, and for provable arithmetic sentences (a [computably enumerable set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computably_enumerable_set "Computably enumerable set")). 
The undefinability theorem shows that this encoding cannot be done for [semantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic "Semantic") concepts such as truth. It shows that no sufficiently rich interpreted language can represent its own semantics. A corollary is that any [metalanguage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metalanguage "Metalanguage") capable of expressing the semantics of some object language (e.g. a predicate is definable in [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo%E2%80%93Fraenkel_set_theory "Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory") for whether formulae in the language of [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic "Peano arithmetic") are true in the standard [natural-number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number "Natural number") model of arithmetic[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarski%27s_undefinability_theorem#cite_note-2)) must have expressive power exceeding that of the object language. The metalanguage includes primitive notions, [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom "Axiom"), and [rules of inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_inference "Rules of inference") absent from the object language, so that there are theorems provable in the metalanguage not provable in the object language. 
The undefinability theorem is conventionally attributed to [Alfred Tarski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Tarski "Alfred Tarski"). Gödel also discovered the undefinability theorem in 1930, while proving his incompleteness theorems published in 1931, and well before the 1933 publication of Tarski's work (Murawski 1998). While Gödel never published anything bearing on his independent discovery of undefinability, he did describe it in a 1931 letter to [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann"). Tarski had obtained almost all results of his 1933 monograph _The Concept of Truth in the Languages of the Deductive Sciences_ between 1929 and 1931, and spoke about them to Polish audiences. However, as he emphasized in the paper, the undefinability theorem was the only result he did not obtain earlier. According to the footnote to the undefinability theorem (_Twierdzenie I_) of the 1933 monograph, the theorem and the sketch of the proof were added to the monograph only after the manuscript had been sent to the printer in 1931. Tarski reports there that, when he presented the content of his monograph to the Warsaw Academy of Science on March 21, 1931, he expressed at this place only some conjectures, based partly on his own investigations and partly on Gödel's short report on the incompleteness theorems "Einige metamathematische Resultate über Entscheidungsdefinitheit und Widerspruchsfreiheit" [Some metamathematical results on the definiteness of decision and consistency], [Austrian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austrian_Academy_of_Sciences "Austrian Academy of Sciences"), Vienna, 1930. 
## Simplified statement
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tarski%27s_undefinability_theorem&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Simplified statement")]
We will first state a simplified version of Tarski's theorem, then state and prove in the next section the theorem Tarski proved in 1933. 
Let  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8) be the language of [first-order arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_arithmetic "First-order arithmetic"). This is the theory of the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number "Natural number"), including their addition and multiplication, axiomatized by the [first-order Peano axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_axioms#First-order_theory_of_arithmetic "Peano axioms"). This is a "[first-order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_logic "First-order logic")" theory: the [quantifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantifier_\(logic\) "Quantifier \(logic\)") extend over natural numbers, but not over sets or functions of natural numbers. The theory is strong enough to describe [recursively defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion "Recursion") integer functions such as exponentiation, [factorials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorial "Factorial") or the [Fibonacci sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number "Fibonacci number"). 
Let  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed) be the standard [structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_\(mathematical_logic\) "Structure \(mathematical logic\)") for  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8), i.e.  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed) consists of the ordinary set of natural numbers and their addition and multiplication. Each sentence in  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8) can be interpreted in  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed) and then becomes either true or false. Thus  ( L , N ) {\displaystyle (L,\mathbb {N} )} ![{\\displaystyle \(L,\\mathbb {N} \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/803f75b0d8793afe685e34a66158c96a25c6be32) is the "interpreted first-order language of arithmetic". 
Each formula  φ {\displaystyle \varphi } ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/33ee699558d09cf9d653f6351f9fda0b2f4aaa3e) in  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8) has a [Gödel number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del_number "Gödel number") g ( φ ) {\displaystyle g(\varphi )} ![{\\displaystyle g\(\\varphi \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/95d3de6e92b84307e206a3e288720b902d8fbec6). This is a natural number that "encodes"  φ {\displaystyle \varphi } ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/33ee699558d09cf9d653f6351f9fda0b2f4aaa3e). In that way, the language  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8) can talk about formulas in  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8), not just about numbers. Let  T {\displaystyle T} ![{\\displaystyle T}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ec7200acd984a1d3a3d7dc455e262fbe54f7f6e0) denote the set of  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-sentences true in  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed), and  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855) the set of Gödel numbers of the sentences in  T {\displaystyle T} ![{\\displaystyle T}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ec7200acd984a1d3a3d7dc455e262fbe54f7f6e0). The following theorem answers the question: Can  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855) be defined by a formula of first-order arithmetic? 
_Tarski's undefinability theorem (simplified form)_ : There is no  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) that defines  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855). That is, there is no  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) such that for every  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-sentence  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3), the formula  T r u e ( g ( A ) ) ⟺ A {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (g(A))\iff A} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(g\(A\)\)\\iff A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1c394ac9afc330b429bfcfc33ab4302a2219663f) holds in  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed). 
Informally, the theorem says that the concept of truth of first-order arithmetic statements cannot be defined by a formula in first-order arithmetic. This implies a major limitation on the scope of "self-representation". By working in a stronger system (e.g., by adding a sort of subsets of  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed) as in [second-order arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-order_arithmetic "Second-order arithmetic")) it _is_ possible to define a formula  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) that holds on exactly the set  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855), but that does not define truth for the stronger system: this formula  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) only defines a truth predicate for formulas in the original language  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8) (e.g., because  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855) does not contain codes for sentences quantifying over subsets of  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed)). To define truth in this stronger system would require ascending to an even stronger system and so on. 
To prove the theorem, we proceed by [contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductio_ad_absurdum "Reductio ad absurdum") and assume that an  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) exists that is true for the natural number  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) in  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed) if and only if  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) is the Gödel number of a sentence in  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8) that is true in  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed). We could then use  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) to define a new  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula  S ( m ) {\displaystyle S(m)} ![{\\displaystyle S\(m\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/da5ab66b1231fb42c70285826dacad7d55b6bc48) that is true for the natural number  m {\displaystyle m} ![{\\displaystyle m}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0a07d98bb302f3856cbabc47b2b9016692e3f7bc) if and only if  m {\displaystyle m} ![{\\displaystyle m}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0a07d98bb302f3856cbabc47b2b9016692e3f7bc) is the Gödel number of a formula  φ ( x ) {\displaystyle \varphi (x)} ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi \(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4c4046f1f2de7df04bde418ba2bc4d3898ac2385) (with a free variable  x {\displaystyle x} ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4)) such that  φ ( m ) {\displaystyle \varphi (m)} ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi \(m\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5cb43fceeba71e55e9827d94cf51d8effdf46584) is false when interpreted in  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed) (i.e. the formula  φ ( x ) {\displaystyle \varphi (x)} ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi \(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4c4046f1f2de7df04bde418ba2bc4d3898ac2385), when applied to its own Gödel number, yields a false statement). If we now consider the Gödel number  g {\displaystyle g} ![{\\displaystyle g}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d3556280e66fe2c0d0140df20935a6f057381d77) of the formula  S ( m ) {\displaystyle S(m)} ![{\\displaystyle S\(m\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/da5ab66b1231fb42c70285826dacad7d55b6bc48), and ask whether the sentence  S ( g ) {\displaystyle S(g)} ![{\\displaystyle S\(g\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/57495cc61c3bf4708372b5aaaf84d38d71489b52) is true in  N {\displaystyle \mathbb {N} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {N} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdf9a96b565ea202d0f4322e9195613fb26a9bed), we obtain a contradiction. (This is known as a [diagonal argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_lemma "Diagonal lemma").) 
The theorem is a corollary of [Post's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post%27s_theorem "Post's theorem") about the [arithmetical hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetical_hierarchy "Arithmetical hierarchy"), proved some years after Tarski (1933). A semantic proof of Tarski's theorem from Post's theorem is obtained by contradiction as follows. Assuming  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855) is arithmetically definable, there is a natural number  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) such that  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855) is definable by a formula at level  Σ n 0 {\displaystyle \Sigma _{n}^{0}} ![{\\displaystyle \\Sigma _{n}^{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7a4ef07c43c84a667249d8d7400a79d53b0c1577) of the [arithmetical hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetical_hierarchy "Arithmetical hierarchy"). However,  T ∗ {\displaystyle T^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle T^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5426ed7abdea7d2ff995ad0e01bc4ca62a273855) is  Σ k 0 {\displaystyle \Sigma _{k}^{0}} ![{\\displaystyle \\Sigma _{k}^{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ec0f4d1692d9f0d788f0dd950f79d2ce342560fa)-hard for all  k {\displaystyle k} ![{\\displaystyle k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3c9a2c7b599b37105512c5d570edc034056dd40). Thus the arithmetical hierarchy collapses at level  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b), contradicting Post's theorem. 
## General form
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tarski%27s_undefinability_theorem&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: General form")]
Tarski proved a stronger theorem than the one stated above, using an entirely syntactical method. The resulting theorem applies to any formal language with [negation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negation "Negation"), and with sufficient capability for [self-reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-reference "Self-reference") that the [diagonal lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_lemma "Diagonal lemma") holds. First-order arithmetic satisfies these preconditions, but the theorem applies to much more general formal systems, such as [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZFC "ZFC"). 
_Tarski's undefinability theorem (general form)_ : Let  ( L , N ) {\displaystyle (L,{\mathcal {N}})} ![{\\displaystyle \(L,{\\mathcal {N}}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81df9c366f4012ad0e040487587f3d6b717d0981) be any interpreted formal language that includes negation and has a Gödel numbering  g ( φ ) {\displaystyle g(\varphi )} ![{\\displaystyle g\(\\varphi \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/95d3de6e92b84307e206a3e288720b902d8fbec6) satisfying the diagonal lemma, i.e. for every  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula  B ( x ) {\displaystyle B(x)} ![{\\displaystyle B\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b5dfb5ce580fb90ec93f88f878c55ea000650826) (with one free variable  x {\displaystyle x} ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4)) there is a sentence  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) such that  A ⟺ B ( g ( A ) ) {\displaystyle A\iff B(g(A))} ![{\\displaystyle A\\iff B\(g\(A\)\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d1143d7b5f009d70a36ee9484f4cd6774585f067) holds in  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd). Then there is no  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) with the following property: for every  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-sentence  A , {\displaystyle A,} ![{\\displaystyle A,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2746026864cc5896e3e52443a1c917be2df9d8ea) the formula  T r u e ( g ( A ) ) ⟺ A {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (g(A))\iff A} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(g\(A\)\)\\iff A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1c394ac9afc330b429bfcfc33ab4302a2219663f) is true in  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd). 
The proof of Tarski's undefinability theorem in this form is again by [reductio ad absurdum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductio_ad_absurdum "Reductio ad absurdum"). Suppose that an  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula  T r u e ( n ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (n)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45887a9f5cdaa1bfc29c5c68952a5f87fa7d0720) as above existed, i.e., if  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) is an  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-sentence, then  T r u e ( g ( A ) ) {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (g(A))} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(g\(A\)\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/349a93951100d7260c633a45f17ebf47447e1e5b) holds in  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd) if and only if  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) holds in  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd). Hence for all  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3), the formula  T r u e ( g ( A ) ) ⟺ A {\displaystyle \mathrm {True} (g(A))\iff A} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {True} \(g\(A\)\)\\iff A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1c394ac9afc330b429bfcfc33ab4302a2219663f) holds in  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd). But the diagonal lemma, with  B ( x ) {\displaystyle B(x)} ![{\\displaystyle B\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b5dfb5ce580fb90ec93f88f878c55ea000650826) instantiated to  ¬ T r u e ( x ) {\displaystyle \lnot \mathrm {True} (x)} ![{\\displaystyle \\lnot \\mathrm {True} \(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f6e1ea19d1c3c3a7d8fbc90ec38369af3f55e57e), yields a counterexample to this equivalence, by giving a "liar" formula  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2) such that  S ⟺ ¬ T r u e ( g ( S ) ) {\displaystyle S\iff \lnot \mathrm {True} (g(S))} ![{\\displaystyle S\\iff \\lnot \\mathrm {True} \(g\(S\)\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6769edfad52aaf597a9ea1847f3fcdd7dd9f84e6) holds in  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd). This is a contradiction. QED. 
## Discussion
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tarski%27s_undefinability_theorem&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Discussion")]
The formal machinery of the proof given above is wholly elementary except for the diagonalization that the diagonal lemma requires. The proof of the diagonal lemma is likewise surprisingly simple; for example, it does not invoke [recursive functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursion#Functional_recursion "Recursion") in any way. The proof does assume that every  L {\displaystyle L} ![{\\displaystyle L}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/103168b86f781fe6e9a4a87b8ea1cebe0ad4ede8)-formula has a [Gödel number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del_number "Gödel number"), but the specifics of a coding method are not required. Hence Tarski's theorem is much easier to motivate and prove than the more celebrated [theorems of Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems "Gödel's incompleteness theorems") about the metamathematical properties of first-order arithmetic. 
[Smullyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Smullyan "Raymond Smullyan") (1991, 2001) has argued forcefully that Tarski's undefinability theorem deserves much of the attention garnered by [Gödel's incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorem "Gödel's incompleteness theorem"). That the latter theorems have much to say about all of mathematics and more controversially, about a range of philosophical issues (e.g., [Lucas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lucas_\(philosopher\) "John Lucas \(philosopher\)") 1961) is less than evident. Tarski's theorem, on the other hand, is not directly about mathematics but about the inherent limitations of any formal language sufficiently expressive to be of real interest. Such languages are necessarily capable of enough [self-reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-reference "Self-reference") for the diagonal lemma to apply to them. The broader philosophical import of Tarski's theorem is more strikingly evident. 
An interpreted language is _strongly-semantically-self-representational_ exactly when the language contains [predicates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_\(grammar\) "Predicate \(grammar\)") and [function symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_symbol "Function symbol") defining all the [semantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic "Semantic") concepts specific to the language. Hence the required functions include the "semantic valuation function" mapping a formula  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) to its [truth value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_value "Truth value") | | A | | {\displaystyle ||A||} ![{\\displaystyle ||A||}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/da5ed1d3af60cef72f38deaf65b36e9923ff2e35), and the "semantic denotation function" mapping a term  t {\displaystyle t} ![{\\displaystyle t}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/65658b7b223af9e1acc877d848888ecdb4466560) to the object it denotes. Tarski's theorem then generalizes as follows: _No sufficiently powerful language is strongly-semantically-self-representational_. 
The undefinability theorem does not prevent truth in one theory from being defined in a stronger theory. For example, the set of (codes for) formulas of first-order [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic "Peano arithmetic") that are true in  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd) is definable by a formula in [second-order arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-order_arithmetic "Second-order arithmetic"). Similarly, the set of true formulas of the standard model of second-order arithmetic (or  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b)th-order arithmetic for any  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b)) can be defined by a formula in first-order [ZFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo%E2%80%93Fraenkel_set_theory "Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory").
