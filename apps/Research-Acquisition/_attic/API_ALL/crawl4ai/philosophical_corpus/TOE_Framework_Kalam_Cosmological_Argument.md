From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Philosophical argument for the existence of God
For the book on the topic by William Lane Craig, see [The Kalām Cosmological Argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Kal%C4%81m_Cosmological_Argument "The Kalām Cosmological Argument").
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/59/William_Lane_Craig.jpg/250px-William_Lane_Craig.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_Lane_Craig.jpg)[William Lane Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lane_Craig "William Lane Craig") (born 1949), who revived the Kalam cosmological argument during the 20th and 21st centuries
The **Kalam cosmological argument** is a modern formulation of the [cosmological argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_argument "Cosmological argument") for the [existence of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence_of_God "Existence of God"). It is named after the _[Kalam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam "Kalam")_ (medieval [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") [scholasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism")) from which many of its key ideas originated.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-craig7-1) [Philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher "Philosopher") and [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theologian "Theologian") [William Lane Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lane_Craig "William Lane Craig") was principally responsible for revitalising these ideas for modern academic discourse through his book _[The Kalām Cosmological Argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Kal%C4%81m_Cosmological_Argument "The Kalām Cosmological Argument")_ (1979), as well as other publications. 
The argument's central thesis is the [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics") impossibility of a [temporally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporality "Temporality") past-infinite [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe") and of [actual infinities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_infinity "Actual infinity") existing in the [real world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality "Reality"), traced by Craig to 11th-century Persian Muslim scholastic philosopher [Al-Ghazali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ghazali "Al-Ghazali"). This feature distinguishes it from other cosmological arguments, such as [Aquinas's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas") [Second Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Ways_\(Aquinas\)#Second_way:_The_Argument_of_the_First_Cause "Five Ways \(Aquinas\)"), which rests on the impossibility of a [causally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality "Causality") ordered [infinite regress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_regress "Infinite regress"), and those of [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz") and [Samuel Clarke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Clarke "Samuel Clarke"), which refer to the [principle of sufficient reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_sufficient_reason "Principle of sufficient reason").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-auto1-2)
Since Craig's original publication, the Kalam cosmological argument has elicited public debate between Craig and [Graham Oppy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Oppy "Graham Oppy"), [Adolf Grünbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Gr%C3%BCnbaum "Adolf Grünbaum"), [J. L. Mackie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._L._Mackie "J. L. Mackie") and [Quentin Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Smith "Quentin Smith"), and has been used in [Christian apologetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_apologetics "Christian apologetics").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-3)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-auto1-2) According to [Michael Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Martin_\(philosopher\) "Michael Martin \(philosopher\)"), the cosmological arguments presented by Craig, Bruce Reichenbach, and [Richard Swinburne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Swinburne "Richard Swinburne") are "among the most sophisticated and well-argued in [contemporary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_philosophy "Contemporary philosophy") [theological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology") philosophy".[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-4)

The most prominent form of the Kalam cosmological argument, as defended by William Lane Craig, is expressed in two parts, as an initial [syllogism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllogism "Syllogism") followed by further philosophical analysis.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-5)
### Initial syllogism
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Initial syllogism")]
The Kalam cosmological argument is a [deductive argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_argument "Deductive argument"). Therefore, if its [premises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premises "Premises") are true, the conclusion follows [necessarily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_consequence "Logical consequence"). 
  1. Everything that begins to exist has a cause.
  2. The universe began to exist.
  3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.


### Conceptual analysis of the cause
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Conceptual analysis of the cause")]
Craig argues that the cause of the universe [necessarily](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_entailment "Logical entailment") embodies specific properties, in being:[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-BCNTPA-6)
  * Uncaused, otherwise an [infinite regress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_regress "Infinite regress") of causes would arise.
  * Timeless (therefore changeless), spaceless, immaterial and enormously powerful, in creating [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime "Spacetime") and its contents _[ex nihilo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creatio_ex_nihilo "Creatio ex nihilo")_.
  * Personal, possessing [non-deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinism "Determinism") [agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent_causation "Agent causation"), in creating the universe from a timeless state (without prior determining conditions).
  * Singular, per [Occam's razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor "Occam's razor"), in the absence of good reasons to believe in the existence of more than one uncaused cause.


Based upon this analysis, he appends a further premise and conclusion:[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-BCNTPA-6)
  1. If the universe has a cause, then an uncaused, personal Creator of the universe exists who _[sans](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/sans#English "wikt:sans") (without)_ the universe[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CraigGT-7) is beginningless, changeless, immaterial, timeless, spaceless and enormously powerful.
  2. Therefore, an uncaused, personal Creator of the universe exists, who _sans_ the universe is beginningless, changeless, immaterial, timeless, spaceless and enormously powerful.


Craig appraises the [theological significance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#Conceptual_analysis_of_the_First_Cause) of the final conclusion:[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-auto-8)
> "... our whole universe was caused to exist by something beyond it and greater than it. For it is no secret that one of the most important conceptions of what theists mean by 'God' is Creator of heaven and earth."
## Historical background
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Historical background")]
The origins of the [cosmological argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_argument "Cosmological argument") can be traced to [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity"), rooted in the concept of the [prime mover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unmoved_mover "Unmoved mover"), introduced by [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle"). In the 6th century, [Syriac Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_Christianity "Syriac Christianity") theologian [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus "John Philoponus") (c. 490–c. 570) presented the first known version of the argument based on the impossibility of an [infinite temporal regress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_argument#The_infinite_regress "Cosmological argument"), postulating that time itself must have had a beginning.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-philo1-9)
Like other early Christian commentators, Philoponus disputed the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece") doctrine of the [eternity of matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternity_of_the_world "Eternity of the world"), noting that this was inconsistent with the [Judeo-Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judeo-Christian "Judeo-Christian") doctrine of _[creatio ex nihilo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creatio_ex_nihilo "Creatio ex nihilo")_. Furthermore, he examined the contradiction between Greek conceptions of past eternity and Aristotle's repudiation of the existence of [actual infinities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_infinities "Actual infinities").[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-duncan-10) In 529, he presented his critique, _On the Eternity of the World Against Proclus_ , categorising arguments for the [finitude of the past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_finitism "Temporal finitism"), which underpinned his arguments for the existence of God.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-philo1-9)
Philoponus's ideas would be developed substantially within the proceedings of [medieval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages "Middle Ages") [Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") [scholasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism")—or _[kalam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam "Kalam")_ —through the 9th and 12th centuries,[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-duncan-10) refined in the 11th century by [Al-Ghazali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Ghazali "Al-Ghazali") and in the 12th by [Ibn Rushd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Averroes "Averroes").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-11) In his landmark thesis, _[The Incoherence of the Philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Incoherence_of_the_Philosophers "The Incoherence of the Philosophers")_ , Persian Muslim theologian Al-Ghazali characterised the absurdity of a beginningless universe and of the existence of actual infinities, articulating a prototypical formulation of the modern Kalam cosmological argument:[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-Craig,_1994:_80-12)
> "Every being which begins has a cause for its beginning; now the world is a being which begins;      therefore, it possesses a cause for its beginning."
In the 13th century, the cosmological argument was introduced to medieval Christian theology, wherein it would be examined by [St. Bonaventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonaventure "Bonaventure") as well as [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas") in his _[Summa Theologica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica "Summa Theologica")_ (I, q.2, a.3) and _[Summa Contra Gentiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Contra_Gentiles "Summa Contra Gentiles")_ (I, 13).[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-13)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-auto2-14)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-15) Conceptions of [temporal finitism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_finitism "Temporal finitism") that had been substantiated in Philoponus's—and later, Al-Ghazali's—writings inspired energetic debate between Aquinas and Bonaventure, as well as further generations of scholars up until the 18th century. Craig writes:[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-16)
> "It finally sputtered to something of an inconclusive end in the thought of the great German philosopher [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant") in the 18th century. Kant held, ironically, that there are rationally compelling arguments for both [the finitude and infinitude of the past], so that the problem is insoluble and exposes the bankruptcy of reason itself."
## Contemporary discourse
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Contemporary discourse")]
According to [Quentin Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Smith "Quentin Smith"):[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-auto2-14)
> "A count of the articles in the philosophy journals shows that more articles have been published about Craig's defense of the Kalam argument than have been published about any other philosopher's contemporary formulation of an argument for God's existence."
The Kalam cosmological argument has received criticism from philosophers such as [J. L. Mackie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._L._Mackie "J. L. Mackie"), [Graham Oppy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Oppy "Graham Oppy"), [Adolf Grunbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Grunbaum "Adolf Grunbaum"), [Michael Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Martin_\(philosopher\) "Michael Martin \(philosopher\)"), [Quentin Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Smith "Quentin Smith"), Wes Morriston and Alex Malpass as well as physicists [Sean M. Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_M._Carroll "Sean M. Carroll"), [Lawrence Krauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Krauss "Lawrence Krauss") and [Victor Stenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Stenger "Victor Stenger").[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-17)
Modern discourse encompasses the fields of both philosophy and science (e.g. the fields of [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics "Quantum physics") and [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology "Cosmology")), which Bruce Reichenbach summarises as:[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-18)
> "... whether there needs to be a cause of the first natural existent, whether something like the universe can be finite and yet not have a beginning, and the nature of infinities and their connection with reality".
Since the temporal ordering of events is central, the Kalam argument also brings issues of the nature of time into the discussion.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-19)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Premise one: "Everything that begins to exist has a cause."")]
Craig and James Sinclair propose that the first premise is obviously true, at least more plausibly true than its negation.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-20)
They offer three reasons to support the premise:[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-21)
  1. [Rational intuition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism#Intuition/deduction_thesis "Rationalism"): The first premise is [self-evidently](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom "Axiom") true, originating in the [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysical "Metaphysical") intuition that "something cannot come into being from nothing" ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language"): _ex nihilo nihil fit_)—characterised in [Parmenidean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmenides "Parmenides") philosophy.
  2. [Reductio ad absurdum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductio_ad_absurdum "Reductio ad absurdum"): If false, it would be inexplicable why anything and everything does not randomly come into existence without a cause. To come into being without any cause is to come into being from nothing, which he says is surely absurd.
  3. [Inductive reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_reasoning "Inductive reasoning") from both common experience and scientific evidence, which constantly verifies and never falsifies its truth.


According to Reichenbach, "the Causal Principle has been the subject of extended criticism", which can be divided into philosophical and scientific criticisms.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-22)
### Philosophical objections
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Philosophical objections")]
[Graham Oppy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Oppy "Graham Oppy"), [J. L. Mackie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._L._Mackie "J. L. Mackie") and Wes Morriston have objected to the intuitiveness of the first premise.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-23)[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-24)[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-25) Oppy states: 
> "Mackie, [Adolf] Grunbaum, [Quentin] Smith and I—among many others—have taken issue with the first premise: why should it be supposed that absolutely everything which begins to exist has a cause for its beginning to exist?"
Mackie affirms that there is no good reason to assume _[a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori_and_a_posteriori "A priori and a posteriori")_ that an uncaused beginning of all things is impossible. Moreover, that the Causal Principle cannot be extrapolated to the universe from inductive experience. He appeals to [David Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hume "David Hume")'s thesis (_[An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Enquiry_Concerning_Human_Understanding "An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding")_) that effects without causes can be conceived in the mind, and that what is conceivable in the mind is possible in the real world.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-26) This argument has been criticised by Bruce Reichenbach and [G.E.M. Anscombe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G.E.M._Anscombe "G.E.M. Anscombe"), who point out the [phenomenological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_\(philosophy\) "Phenomenology \(philosophy\)") and logical problems in inferring factual possibility from conceivability.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-27)[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-28) Craig notes:[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-29)[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-30)
> "Hume himself clearly believed in the causal principle. He presupposes throughout the Enquiry that events have causes, and in 1754 he wrote to John Stewart, 'But allow me to tell you that I never asserted so absurd a Proposition as that anything might arise without a cause'."
Morriston asserts that causal laws are physical processes for which we have intuitive knowledge in the context of events within time and space, but that such intuitions do not hold true for the beginning of time itself. He states:[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-WM1-31)
> "We have no experience of the origin of worlds to tell us that worlds don't come into existence like that. We don't even have experience of the coming into being of anything remotely analogous to the '[initial singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_singularity "Initial singularity")' that figures in the [Big Bang theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang") of the origin of the universe."
Craig responds that causal laws are unrestricted [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics") truths that are "not [contingent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingency_\(philosophy\) "Contingency \(philosophy\)") upon the properties, causal powers, and [dispositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispositions "Dispositions") of the natural kinds of substances which happen to exist", remarking:[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-32)
> "The history of twentieth century astrophysical cosmology belies Morriston's claim that people have no strong intuitions about the need of a causal explanation of the origin of time and the universe."
### Scientific objections
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Scientific objections")]
#### Quantum indeterminacy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Quantum indeterminacy")]
[Paul Davies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Davies "Paul Davies") is unconvinced that the Causal Principle can be defined consistently in fundamental physics, proposing that the phenomenon of [quantum indeterminacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_indeterminacy "Quantum indeterminacy") contradicts a [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinism "Determinism") model of [cause and effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cause_and_effect "Cause and effect").[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-33) Craig concedes that quantum indeterminism contradicts the proposition that _every event_ has a cause. Nonetheless, he maintains it is consistent with the causal premise that "everything that begins to exist has a cause", encompassing the more modest view that objects cannot come into existence entirely devoid of causal conditions.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-crgOC-34) Even if quantum events arise without [sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient#Sufficiency "Necessary and sufficient") prior conditions, he affirms at least some [necessary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient#Necessity "Necessary and sufficient") conditions are present:[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-35)
> "The appearance of a particle in a quantum vacuum may thus be said to be spontaneous, but cannot properly be said to be absolutely uncaused, since it has many physically necessary conditions. To be uncaused in the relevant sense of an absolute beginning, an existent must lack any non-logical necessary or sufficient conditions whatsoever."
Wes Morriston inquires whether necessary conditions alone are adequate in defining causation, suggesting further substantiation is necessary when contemplating the causal origin of the universe.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-WM1-31) For Bruce Reichenbach, the strength of the first premise is grounded in the [ontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology "Ontology") of quantum indeterminacy. If indeterminism is a fundamental feature of the real world, rather than an [epistemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology") artefact of incomplete knowledge or [hidden variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden-variable_theory "Hidden-variable theory"), he suggests its implications for the Causal Principle would be substantially negative.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-36)
Craig stresses that the phenomenon of indeterminism is specific to the [Copenhagen interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_interpretation "Copenhagen interpretation") of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), remarking that this is only one of several interpretations, some of which are fully deterministic—mentioning [Bohmian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie%E2%80%93Bohm_theory "De Broglie–Bohm theory")[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-37)—and none of which are as yet known to be true. He concludes that subatomic physics is not a proven exception to the first premise.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-38)
#### The quantum vacuum
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: The quantum vacuum")]
[Quantum vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_vacuum_state "Quantum vacuum state") phenomena are often expounded to be uncaused in origin, referring to [quantum indeterminacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_indeterminacy "Quantum indeterminacy") governing the vacuum state and [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics "Semantics") that define the [zero-point field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-point_energy "Zero-point energy") as _[nothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horror_vacui_\(philosophy\) "Horror vacui \(philosophy\)")_ , in an ontological sense.[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-39) [Quentin Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quentin_Smith "Quentin Smith") illustrates the example of [virtual particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_particles "Virtual particles"), which appear and disappear through [fluctuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_fluctuation "Quantum fluctuation") in the quantum vacuum, as evidence of uncaused natural phenomena.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-40) In his publication, _[A Universe from Nothing: Why There is Something Rather Than Nothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Universe_from_Nothing:_Why_There_is_Something_Rather_Than_Nothing "A Universe from Nothing: Why There is Something Rather Than Nothing")_ , [Lawrence Krauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Krauss "Lawrence Krauss") proposes that quantum fluctuations can explain how [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space "Space"), [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time "Time") and [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter "Matter") can emerge from 'nothing' (referring to the vacuum state).[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-41) [Michael Martin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Lou_Martin "Michael Lou Martin") writes:[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-42)
> "Even if the universe has a beginning in time, in the light of recently proposed cosmological theories this beginning may be uncaused. Despite Craig's claim that theories postulating that the universe 'could pop into existence uncaused' are incapable of 'sincere affirmation,' such similar theories are in fact being taken seriously by scientists."
[David Albert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Albert "David Albert") deems the term 'nothing' to be misleading when describing the quantum vacuum. In a critique of Krauss's book, he remarks:[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-43)
> "[Relativistic-quantum-field-theoretical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory") vacuum states—no less than giraffes or refrigerators or solar systems—are particular arrangements of elementary physical stuff. The true relativistic-quantum-field-theoretical equivalent to there not being any physical stuff at all isn't this or that particular arrangement of the fields—what it is (obviously, and [ineluctably](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ineluctable#Adjective "wikt:ineluctable"), and on the contrary) is the simple _absence_ of the fields."
Craig defines the vacuum state as a [quantifiable, energetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_energy "Vacuum energy") medium that qualifies as _something_ rather than _[nothing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nothing "Nothing")_ , rejecting the view of quantum vacuum phenomena as uncaused. On quantum fluctuations, he writes:[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-44)
> "... virtual particles do not literally come into existence spontaneously out of nothing. Rather the energy locked up in a vacuum fluctuates spontaneously in such a way as to convert into [evanescent](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/evanescent#Adjective "wikt:evanescent") particles that return almost immediately to the vacuum."
[Alexander Vilenkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Vilenkin "Alexander Vilenkin") determines that, even in the "absence of space, time and matter", the laws of physics are still present and require explanation, though this state would be "as close to nothing as you can get". He continues:[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-45)
> "That makes you wonder, where are these laws? If the laws describe the creation of the universe, that suggests they existed prior to the universe. The question that nobody has any idea how to address is where these laws come from and why these laws in particular?"
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Premise two: "The universe began to exist."")]
Craig defends the second premise using both scientific and philosophical arguments for the [finitude of the past](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_finitism "Temporal finitism"). 
### Cosmology and physics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Cosmology and physics")]
For scientific confirmation, Craig refers to evidence in [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology "Cosmology") and [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"): 
  1. The [standard model of cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambda-CDM_model "Lambda-CDM model"), which characterises the origin of the universe in the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang").[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-46)
  2. The [second law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics "Second law of thermodynamics"), which stipulates that the universe must, in a finite duration, reach a state of equilibrium known as [heat death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_death_of_the_universe "Heat death of the universe"). If the universe has an infinite past, he argues, heat death would have already transpired.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-RFU-47)
  3. The [Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borde%E2%80%93Guth%E2%80%93Vilenkin_theorem "Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem"), according to which any universe that has, on average, been expanding throughout its history cannot have been expanding indefinitely but must have had a past boundary at which inflation began.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-48)


#### Boundary cosmology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Boundary cosmology")]
[Alexander Vilenkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Vilenkin "Alexander Vilenkin") describes the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem:[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-49)
> "A remarkable thing about this theorem is its sweeping generality. We made no assumptions about the material content of the universe. We did not even assume that gravity is described by [Einstein's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"). So, if Einstein's gravity requires some modification, our conclusion will still hold. The only assumption that we made was that the expansion rate of the universe never gets below some nonzero value, no matter how small."
According to Vilenkin and co-author [Alan Guth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Guth "Alan Guth"), the past boundary described by the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem does not necessarily represent a [cosmic beginning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogony "Cosmogony"), instead the beginning of [cosmic inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_\(cosmology\) "Inflation \(cosmology\)").[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-50) Though it "opens the door" for theories other than an absolute beginning, in a 2012 lecture, Vilenkin would discuss problems with alternative theories that would claim to avoid a cosmological beginning (including [eternal inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternal_inflation "Eternal inflation"), [cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_model "Cyclic model") and [emergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_Universe "Emergent Universe") models) concluding: "All the evidence we have says that the universe had a beginning".[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-51) In publications, he would conclude that the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem is sufficient evidence for a beginning of the universe.[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-52)[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-53)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-Vilenkin_2015-54)
Craig maintains that, if anything existed before the past boundary described by the theorem, it would be a [non-classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_spacetime "Quantum spacetime") region defined by an as-yet-undetermined theory of [quantum gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_gravity "Quantum gravity"). This quantum gravity region cannot avoid a beginning, he stresses, given that its inherent [instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metastability "Metastability") would render any quantum regime impossible to endure indefinitely or exist timelessly and changelessly:[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-55)[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-56)
> "If there is such a non-classical region, then it is not past eternal in the classical sense. But neither can it exist literally timelessly, akin to the way in which philosophers consider [abstract objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_objects "Abstract objects") to be timeless or theologians take God to be timeless. For this region is in a state of constant flux, which, given the [Indiscernibility of Identicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_of_indiscernibles#Indiscernibility_of_identicals "Identity of indiscernibles"), is sufficient for time. So even if time as defined in [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics "Classical physics") does not exist at such an era, some sort of time would."
He draws on arguments from [Anthony Aguirre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Aguirre "Anthony Aguirre") and John Kehayias, who write:[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-57)
> "It is very difficult to devise a system, especially a quantum one, that does nothing 'forever', then evolves. A truly stationary or periodic quantum state, which would last forever, would never evolve, whereas one with any instability will not endure for an indefinite time."
#### Exceptions to the theorem
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Exceptions to the theorem")]
Craig and James Sinclair identify 4 exception conditions to the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem:[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CrgCBU-58)
  1. Past eternal static state followed by cosmic expansion (Havg = 0) e.g. emergent models.
  2. Perpetual cycle of cosmic expansion then contraction (Havg = 0) e.g. cyclic models.
  3. Infinite cosmic contraction prior to the current expansion (Havg < 0) e.g. de Sitter cosmology.
  4. Reversal of time at the cosmological boundary, e.g. Aguirre-Gratton model.


They contend that these models contradict observational cosmology or confirm the cosmic beginning they were devised to disprove.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-59)
According to the **[emergent universe model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_Universe "Emergent Universe")** , the universe existed in a static phase for an infinite duration, then transitioned spontaneously via [quantum tunneling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunneling "Quantum tunneling") to a dynamic [expansion phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_expansion "Cosmic expansion"). The static phase is [metastable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metastability "Metastability") and, because it endures over infinite time, the universe overall has an average expansion rate of zero (Havg = 0) thereby avoiding the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem.[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CrgCBU-58)
_Metastability_ in this context refers to a [false vacuum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_vacuum "False vacuum"), which is stable according to [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics "Classical physics"), but unstable at the [quantum mechanical level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") in that it can transition spontaneously via quantum tunneling to a more stable [true vacuum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_vacuum "True vacuum"). This transition may trigger cosmic inflation, or conversely, collapse of the universe to a hyperdense state.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-60) Vilenkin and Audrey Mithani maintain that the metastable state cannot endure indefinitely. Though an event such as cosmic collapse is highly improbable, they argue that over an infinite duration it becomes a mathematical certainty. If the emergent universe cannot exist indefinitely, then it must have had a beginning.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-61)
Responding to Vilenkin and Mithani, [Rube Goldberg cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_Universe#"Rube_Goldberg_cosmology"_scenario "Emergent Universe") postulates an emergent model of [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime "Spacetime") that is fully classical, avoiding a quantum gravity era as well as the problems associated with quantum instability. However, the author notes that its composition comes at the cost of a "long chain of highly [ad hoc assumptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_hoc_hypothesis "Ad hoc hypothesis"), akin to a [Rube Goldberg machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rube_Goldberg_machine "Rube Goldberg machine")".[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-62)
**[Cyclic universes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_model "Cyclic model")** proceed through an infinite cycle of expansions and contractions. Though the earliest models proposed no net expansion or contraction (Havg = 0), they were noted to be in violation of the [second law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics "Second law of thermodynamics") given that the universe would eventually reach a state of [heat death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_death_of_the_universe "Heat death of the universe"), rendering an infinite cycle impossible.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-63) [Paul Steinhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Steinhardt "Paul Steinhardt") and [Neil Turok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Turok "Neil Turok") suggest that cosmic volume could increase with each cycle, causing _entropy dilution_ sufficient to avert heat death.[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-64) Vilenkin notes, however, that this would put the universe in a state of net expansion, rendering it subject to the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem, therefore finite into the past.[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-Vilenkin_2015-54)
**De Sitter Cosmology** describes a universe that contracts for infinite time prior to the [singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_singularity "Cosmological singularity"), followed by expansion. Though it avoids the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem by averaging net contraction (Havg < 0), [George Ellis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_F._R._Ellis "George F. R. Ellis") maintains that initial conditions must be inexplicably precise for contraction to [rebound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bounce "Big Bounce") into [uniform expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedmann%E2%80%93Lema%C3%AEtre%E2%80%93Robertson%E2%80%93Walker_metric "Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric") characteristic of the observed universe. This extraordinary fine-tuning must be acausal and assumed as [brute fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_fact "Brute fact"), he argues, given that it originates in an infinite past.[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-65) Vilenkin writes:[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CraigRFH-66)
> "You can evade the theorem by postulating that the universe was contracting prior to some time. ... This sounds as if there is nothing wrong with having contraction prior to expansion. But the problem is that a contracting universe is highly unstable. Small perturbations would cause it to develop all sorts of messy singularities, so it would never make it to the expanding phase."
The **[Aguirre–Gratton model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternal_inflation "Eternal inflation")** postulates a reversal of time at _t_ = 0, with the universe structured as two inflationary expansions extending either direction from the singularity.[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-67) Among its proponents are [Victor J. Stenger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_J._Stenger "Victor J. Stenger"),[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-68) as well as [Sean Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_M._Carroll "Sean M. Carroll"), who presents the model as "proof by construction" that a universe without a beginning is conceivable.[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-69) Vilenkin proposes that a reversal of time at _t_ = 0 "makes the moment _t_ = 0 rather special. I would say no less special than a true beginning of the universe."[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-70) He elaborates to Stenger:[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CraigRFH-66)
> "Models of this sort have been discussed by Aguirre & Gratton, and by Carroll & Chen. They had to assume though that the minimum of entropy was reached at the bounce and offered no mechanism to enforce this condition. It seems to me that it is essentially equivalent to a beginning."
### Philosophical arguments
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Philosophical arguments")]
For philosophical evidence of the finitude of the past, Craig refers to: 
  1. [Hilbert's paradox of the Grand Hotel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert%27s_paradox_of_the_Grand_Hotel "Hilbert's paradox of the Grand Hotel"), to illustrate the [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics") impossibility of [actual infinites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_infinities "Actual infinities") existing in reality.[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-71)
  2. [The Tristram Shandy Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Life_and_Opinions_of_Tristram_Shandy,_Gentleman#Mathematical_paradox "The Life and Opinions of Tristram Shandy, Gentleman"), to demonstrate the impossibility of forming an actual infinite by successive addition.[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-72)[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-73)


Correspondingly, he argues: 
  * An infinite series of past events is metaphysically impossible, given that it would represent an actual infinite existing in reality.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-craig7-1)
  * Past events cannot be extended to an infinite past, given that they consist of a series formed by successive addition.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-craig7-1)[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-74)


Variations of these arguments have been presented by philosopher [Andrew Loke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Loke "Andrew Loke"), who refers to a modified version of Hilbert's Hotel,[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-75) and Ben Waters, who has published the story of Methuselah's Diary as a refinement of the diary of Tristram Shandy.[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-76)
Craig maintains that, though it is metaphysically impossible for actual infinities to exist in the real world, they (and the absurdities that attend their existence in the real world) are [describable via mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_set "Infinite set"), therefore [logically possible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox#Veridical_paradox "Paradox").[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-77) He also distinguishes between [actual infinities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_infinities "Actual infinities") and [potential infinities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_infinities#Aristotle's_potential%E2%80%93actual_distinction "Actual infinities"), stating that it is fully possible for potential infinites to exist in the real world, in contrast to the former:[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-78)
> "A potential infinite is a series which has a beginning and is growing indefinitely; infinity serves merely as an ideal limit of the series which it never reaches ... That’s impossible, since for any [natural number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_number "Natural number") _n_ , _n_ +1 is always a finite number."
#### Actuality of the past
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Actuality of the past")]
[Edward Feser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Feser "Edward Feser") observes that the Kalam cosmological argument is based upon a [presentist theory of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_presentism "Philosophical presentism") in which past and future events do not exist, remarking that this would be incompatible with objections against an eternal past based upon Hilbert's Hotel:[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-79)
> "If the present alone is real, then how can an infinite series of events in time count as an actual infinite? Past moments of time are not actual; they no longer exist ... In the Hilbert’s hotel scenario [the rooms and guests] exist together all at once, at the same time."
Craig affirms that the simultaneous existence of the enumerated objects is irrelevant, so long as past events _have been_ real, thus instantiated in reality, they can be counted.[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-80)[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-actinf1-81) He refers to a narrative by [Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas") of a blacksmith who, from an eternal past, has used and broken successive hammers. He writes that the discarded hammers need no longer exist to be counted as an actual infinite.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-bcntb-82) Furthermore, that the absurdities attending the existence of actual infinites in reality still apply if the enumerated objects no longer exist:[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-83)
> "... in an infinite series of past events, the number of odd-numbered events is the same as the number of all the events, even though the latter collection includes all the odd-numbered events plus an infinite number of even-numbered events as well."
Feser contends that past events are more analogous to [abstract objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete "Abstract and concrete") than [concrete objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concrete_objects "Concrete objects"), such as rooms and guests relevant to Hilbert's Hotel, noting that it is fully tenable for mathematicians to deal with [infinite sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sets "Infinite sets") of abstract objects, such as [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers "Natural numbers"). For this, he refers to [Aristotle's postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_\(Aristotle\)#Book_IV_\(%CE%94;_208a%E2%80%93223b\) "Physics \(Aristotle\)") that denies the existence of time independent of _objects that change_ :[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-84)
> "To speak of time apart from change is a bit like speaking of a [universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_\(metaphysics\) "Universal \(metaphysics\)") like redness apart from actual red things—it is to engage in _[abstraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstraction "Abstraction")_ from the concrete conditions under which the thing in question (redness, or time) can actually exist."
#### The Symmetry Objection
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: The Symmetry Objection")]
Craig proposes that, though a beginningless past would qualify as an actually infinite series of past events, an endless future would qualify only as a potential infinite, given that future events, unlike past events, are yet to be actualised and merely potential.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-actinf1-81)
Wes Morriston and Alex Malpass argue that this asymmetry is arbitrary. They pronounce that future events that _will be_ actual satisfy the same [reality condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology#Realism_and_anti-realism "Ontology") as past events that are deemed actual in that they _have been_. Furthermore, they stipulate that [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers "Natural numbers") can be representative of moments in time. Since the set of natural numbers greater than any given value, _x_ , is always an actual infinite, so too, they argue, must future events beyond any given moment in time be actually infinite. They conclude that, if the Hilbert's Hotel paradox disproves an eternal past, so too must it negate an endless future, contradicting the [Judeo-Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judeo-Christian "Judeo-Christian") doctrine of [eternal life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternal_life_\(Christianity\) "Eternal life \(Christianity\)").[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-85)
Commenting in agreement that future events cannot be actually infinite, Craig responds that this objection [begs the question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Begging_the_question "Begging the question") and fails to address the [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological "Ontological") distinction between past and future qualified by the objectivity of _temporal becoming_ under [presentism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_presentism "Philosophical presentism").[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-86)[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CVM1-87) He writes: 
> "The series of future events can be finite but endless. In such a case the series is potentially infinite. This is a view that is so widespread and commonly accepted in the history of philosophy and science that the objector has a considerable [burden of proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burden_of_proof_\(philosophy\) "Burden of proof \(philosophy\)") to bear if he is to show that this view is untenable."
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Conclusion: "The universe has a cause."")]
In a critique of Craig's book _[The Kalām Cosmological Argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Kal%C4%81m_Cosmological_Argument "The Kalām Cosmological Argument")_ , published in 1979, Michael Martin writes:[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-88)
> "It should be obvious that Craig's conclusion that a single personal agent created the universe is a _[non sequitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_fallacy#Non_sequitur_in_everyday_speech "Formal fallacy")_. At most, this Kalam argument shows that some personal agent or agents created the universe. Craig cannot validly conclude that a single agent is the creator. On the contrary, for all he shows, there may have been trillions of personal agents involved in the creation."
Martin adds that Craig has not justified his claim of creation _[ex nihilo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creatio_ex_nihilo "Creatio ex nihilo")_ , remarking that the universe may have been created from pre-existing material in a timeless or eternal state. Moreover, that Craig takes his argument too far beyond what his premises allow in stating that the creator is greater than the universe. For this, he cites the example of a parent creating a child who eventually becomes greater than he or she.[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-89)
### Conceptual analysis of the First Cause
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Conceptual analysis of the First Cause")]
In the _Blackwell Companion to Natural Theology_ , published in 2009, Craig and James Sinclair present a philosophical analysis of the properties of the cause of the universe, indicating that they follow by [entailment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_consequence "Logical consequence") from the initial syllogism of the Kalam cosmological argument:[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-BCNTPA-6)
  1. The universe must originate _ex nihilo_ in being without [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural "Natural") cause, because no natural explanation can be causally prior to the very existence of the natural world. The cause of the universe is therefore outside of space and time (_timeless_ , therefore _changeless_ , and _spaceless_) as well as _immaterial_ and _enormously powerful_ , in bringing [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime "Spacetime") and its contents into existence.
  2. Even if positing a plurality of causes prior to the origin of the universe, the causal chain must terminate in a cause which is absolutely first and _uncaused_ , otherwise an [infinite regress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_regress "Infinite regress") of causes would arise, which Craig and Sinclair argue is impossible.
  3. [Occam's Razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_Razor "Occam's Razor") maintains that _[unicity](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/unicity "wikt:unicity")_ of the First Cause should be assumed in the absence of specific reasons to believe that there is more than one causeless cause.
  4. [Agent causation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent_causation "Agent causation"), or [volitional action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will "Free will"), is the only [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology "Ontology") condition in which an effect can arise in the absence of prior determining conditions. Therefore, only _personal, free agency_ can account for the origin of a first [temporal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporality "Temporality") effect from a changeless cause.
  5. There are two conceivable categories of objects with the potential to be _uncaused_ , _spaceless_ , _timeless_ and _immaterial_ : 
     * [Minds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind "Mind") (in some conceptions of [mind-body dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind-body_dualism#Substance_or_Cartesian_dualism "Mind-body dualism")) may be characterised as immaterial and spatially unextended, with the potential to be _unembodied_ , timeless, changeless and beginningless.[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-90)
     * [Abstract objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete "Abstract and concrete"), such as the set of [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers "Natural numbers"), may be described as non-spatial and non-temporal, but do not sit in causal relationships and are therefore causally ineffective.[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-91)


Based upon their conceptual analysis, Craig concludes:[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-BCNTPA-6)
> "... an uncaused, personal Creator of the universe exists, who _sans_ the universe[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CraigGT-7) is beginningless, changeless, immaterial, timeless, spaceless and enormously powerful."
He remarks upon the theological significance of this union of properties:[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-auto-8)
> "... our whole universe was caused to exist by something beyond it and greater than it. For it is no secret that one of the most important conceptions of what theists mean by 'God' is Creator of heaven and earth."
## Theories of time
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Theories of time")]
The Kalam cosmological argument is based on the [A-theory of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_series_and_B_series "A series and B series"), also known as the "tensed theory of time" or [presentism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_presentism "Philosophical presentism"), in which past and future events do not exist in reality (they _have existed_ , or _will exist_ , but do not exist now) and only the present exists.[[92]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-92) This is opposed to the [B-theory of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-theory_of_time "B-theory of time"), also known as the "tenseless theory of time" or [eternalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eternalism_\(philosophy_of_time\) "Eternalism \(philosophy of time\)"), in which past, present and future events co-exist and there is no privilege to the present other than as a [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference "Frame of reference").[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-93)
Under the A-theory, the passage of time and _temporal becoming_ are objective and [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_realism "Philosophical realism") features of the universe, with objects coming into being and going out of being. In the B-theory, temporal passage and becoming are illusions of [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness "Consciousness"). Craig explains:[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-crgOC-34)
> "On a B-Theory of time, the universe does not in fact come into being or become actual at the Big Bang; it just exists tenselessly as a four-dimensional space-time block that is finitely [extended](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_\(metaphysics\) "Extension \(metaphysics\)") in the earlier than direction. If time is tenseless, then the universe never really comes into being, and, therefore, the quest for a cause of its coming into being is misconceived."
### Neo-Lorentzian relativity
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Neo-Lorentzian relativity")]
Craig has defended the A-theory against objections from [J. M. E. McTaggart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._E._McTaggart "J. M. E. McTaggart") and [hybrid A–B theorists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Growing_block_universe "Growing block universe").[[94]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-94)[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-95) He refers to the [neo‐Lorentzian interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_ether_theory "Lorentz ether theory") of the [Special Theory of Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"), which he contends has become tenable in light of recent findings in quantum mechanics concerning [Bell's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem "Bell's theorem").[[96]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-96) Similar to Einstein's original interpretation, the Lorentzian view describes a 3-dimensional universe existing through time, with objects in motion demonstrating [time dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity#Time_dilation "Special relativity") and [length contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity#Length_contraction "Special relativity"). However, these relativistic distortions occur relative to a privileged rest frame (an absolute frame of reference for the universe) rather than relative to each observer.[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CrgLz-97)
He writes that the Lorentzian interpretation, in postulating an [absolute space and absolute time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_space_and_time "Absolute space and time"), resolves the fragmentation of reality that characterises Einsteinian relativity, in which observers in relative motion occupy different spaces and times. Importantly, [Lorentz's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz "Hendrik Lorentz") view of time as dynamic, and distinct from space, renders it compatible with the A-theory conception of a tensed universe.[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-RTMB-98)
Philosopher Yuri Balashov asserts that both consensus and evidence support [Minkowski's interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space "Minkowski space") of relativity, which posits a 4D geometric universe inhabited by objects extended in time as well as space.[[99]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-99) In spurning the notion of a 3D universe existing in time, Minkowskian relativity rejects the A-theory, correlating instead with the B-theory conception of a tenseless [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime "Spacetime"). Balashov remarks:[[100]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-100)
> "Despite the fact that presentism has the firm backing of [common sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_sense "Common sense") and eternalism revolts against it, eternalism is widely regarded as almost the default view in contemporary debates, and presentism as a highly problematic view."
Craig suggests that Balashov underestimates the challenge to Minkowskian relativity presented by recent findings.[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-CrgLz-97) He criticises Balashov for adopting a [verificationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verificationism "Verificationism") method that overlooks important metaphysical and theological foundations for the A-theory, declaring that correct interpretation of Special Relativity involves not merely scientific, but also metaphysical, inquiry.[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-RTMB-98)[[101]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-ttt-101)
### Compatibility with the B-theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kalam_cosmological_argument&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Compatibility with the B-theory")]
In a 2020 interview, Craig proposed that the Kalam cosmological argument could be adapted to the B-theory of time by:[[102]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-102)
  * Abandoning the metaphysical argument against forming an actual infinity by successive addition.
  * Modifying the causal premise (replacing its appeal to the concept of 'coming into being' with the concept of 'beginning to exist').


Under the B-theory, scientific evidence for the finitude of the past would still be valid and the argument as a whole would be tenable, though requiring reformulation to correct for the negation of temporal becoming. Craig articulates a B-theory version of its syllogism:[[103]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-103)
  1. If something exists at a time _t_ but it does not exist at any earlier point in time then it has a cause.
  2. The universe does exist at time _t_ at which there is no earlier time where the universe existed.
  3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.


Philosopher Ben Waters has also argued that the Kalam cosmological argument does not require a commitment to the A-theory.[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalam_cosmological_argument#cite_note-104)
