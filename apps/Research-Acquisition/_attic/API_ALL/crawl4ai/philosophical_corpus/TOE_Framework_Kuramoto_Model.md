From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Exactly solvable model of coupled oscillators
The **Kuramoto model** (or **Kuramoto–Daido model**), first proposed by [Yoshiki Kuramoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshiki_Kuramoto "Yoshiki Kuramoto") (蔵本 由紀, _Kuramoto Yoshiki_),[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-2) is a [mathematical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_model "Mathematical model") used in describing [synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronization "Synchronization"). More specifically, it is a model for the behavior of a large set of coupled [oscillators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillators "Oscillators").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-Strogatz-2000-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-4) Its formulation was motivated by the behavior of systems of [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical "Chemical") and [biological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_process "Biological process") oscillators, and it has found widespread applications in areas such as [neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_oscillation#Mathematical_description "Neural oscillation")[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-Understanding_the_dynamics_of_biolo-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-8) and oscillating flame dynamics.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-10) Kuramoto was quite surprised when the behavior of some physical systems, namely coupled arrays of [Josephson junctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josephson_junctions "Josephson junctions"), followed his model.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-11)
The model makes several assumptions, including that there is weak coupling, that the oscillators are identical or nearly identical, and that interactions depend sinusoidally on the phase difference between each pair of objects. 
## Definition
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Definition")]
[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:KuramotoModelPhaseLocking.ogv "Play video")Duration: 1 minute and 1 second.1:01[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:KuramotoModelPhaseLocking.ogv)Phase locking in the Kuramoto model
In the most popular version of the Kuramoto model, each of the oscillators is considered to have its own intrinsic [natural frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_frequency "Natural frequency") ω i {\displaystyle \omega _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\omega _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e174c191a5ba3889c66597461ef260811cce0481), and each is coupled equally to all other oscillators. Surprisingly, this fully [nonlinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_system "Nonlinear system") model can be solved exactly in the limit of infinite oscillators, _N_ → ∞;[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-Understanding_the_dynamics_of_biolo-5) alternatively, using self-consistency arguments, one may obtain steady-state solutions of the order parameter.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-Strogatz-2000-3) The most popular form of the model has the following governing equations:  d θ i d t = ω i + 1 N ∑ j = 1 N K i j sin ⁡ ( θ j − θ i ) , i = 1 … N , {\displaystyle {\frac {d\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\omega _{i}+{\frac {1}{N}}\sum _{j=1}^{N}K_{ij}\sin(\theta _{j}-\theta _{i}),\qquad i=1\ldots N,} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {d\\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\\omega _{i}+{\\frac {1}{N}}\\sum _{j=1}^{N}K_{ij}\\sin\(\\theta _{j}-\\theta _{i}\),\\qquad i=1\\ldots N,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/885876f0dd3b7add2547b75949c5f05a2538a1e1) where the system is composed of _N_ limit-cycle oscillators, with phases  θ i {\displaystyle \theta _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\theta _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/302b19204ed378e99ff4575341a67eebdbe5a555) and coupling constant _K_. 
Noise can be added to the system. In that case, the original equation is altered to  d θ i d t = ω i + ζ i + K N ∑ j = 1 N sin ⁡ ( θ j − θ i ) , {\displaystyle {\frac {d\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\omega _{i}+\zeta _{i}+{\dfrac {K}{N}}\sum _{j=1}^{N}\sin(\theta _{j}-\theta _{i}),} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {d\\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\\omega _{i}+\\zeta _{i}+{\\dfrac {K}{N}}\\sum _{j=1}^{N}\\sin\(\\theta _{j}-\\theta _{i}\),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2c7451dc10a7c231093f5a69b9baa7023dbcb898) where  ζ i {\displaystyle \zeta _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\zeta _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/aab3f49d01957ab74f9581cd34ba72cc7efc4bdc) is the fluctuation and a function of time. If the noise is considered to be white noise, then  ⟨ ζ i ( t ) ⟩ = 0 , {\displaystyle \langle \zeta _{i}(t)\rangle =0,} ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\zeta _{i}\(t\)\\rangle =0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b039285fc4528959039e9e8f36f2a3caf4fa01e8) ⟨ ζ i ( t ) ζ j ( t ′ ) ⟩ = 2 D δ i j δ ( t − t ′ ) , {\displaystyle \langle \zeta _{i}(t)\zeta _{j}(t')\rangle =2D\delta _{ij}\delta (t-t'),} ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\zeta _{i}\(t\)\\zeta _{j}\(t'\)\\rangle =2D\\delta _{ij}\\delta \(t-t'\),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2573eed64c4888d04f8526db8e8b31bdffe2dc1a) with  D {\displaystyle D} ![{\\displaystyle D}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f34a0c600395e5d4345287e21fb26efd386990e6) denoting the strength of noise. 
## Transformation
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Transformation")]
The transformation that allows this model to be solved exactly (at least in the _N_ → ∞ limit) is as follows: 
Define the "order" parameters _r_ and _ψ_ as       r e i ψ = 1 N ∑ j = 1 N e i θ j {\displaystyle re^{i\psi }={\frac {1}{N}}\sum _{j=1}^{N}e^{i\theta _{j}}} ![{\\displaystyle re^{i\\psi }={\\frac {1}{N}}\\sum _{j=1}^{N}e^{i\\theta _{j}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/85bfcddf1ef647fe126db5b6fd9f32fb579e52f4).
Here _r_ represents the phase-[coherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_\(physics\) "Coherence \(physics\)") of the population of oscillators and _ψ_ indicates the average phase. Substituting in the equation gives       d θ i d t = ω i + K r sin ⁡ ( ψ − θ i ) {\displaystyle {\frac {d\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\omega _{i}+Kr\sin(\psi -\theta _{i})} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {d\\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\\omega _{i}+Kr\\sin\(\\psi -\\theta _{i}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7ab8ade41fcc05e5d6fc806c1c149bf77417e709).
Thus the oscillators' equations are no longer explicitly coupled; instead the order parameters govern the behavior. A further transformation is usually done, to a rotating frame in which the statistical average of phases over all oscillators is zero (i.e.  ψ = 0 {\displaystyle \psi =0} ![{\\displaystyle \\psi =0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7eca9b06e607571768c9b79cb231a9997308c4e6)). Finally, the governing equation becomes       d θ i d t = ω i − K r sin ⁡ ( θ i ) {\displaystyle {\frac {d\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\omega _{i}-Kr\sin(\theta _{i})} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {d\\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\\omega _{i}-Kr\\sin\(\\theta _{i}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cf9b554002cbe1489d4a4a21112ec0d12a40ca11).
## Large _N_ limit
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Large N limit")]
Now consider the case as _N_ tends to infinity. Take the distribution of intrinsic natural frequencies as _g_(_ω_) (assumed [normalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalization_constant "Normalization constant")). Then assume that the density of oscillators at a given phase _θ_ , with given natural frequency _ω_ , at time _t_ is  ρ ( θ , ω , t ) {\displaystyle \rho (\theta ,\omega ,t)} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho \(\\theta ,\\omega ,t\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdcf0ddf09de2fb3e642cc97380982c4827036e3). Normalization requires that       ∫ − π π ρ ( θ , ω , t ) d θ = 1. {\displaystyle \int _{-\pi }^{\pi }\rho (\theta ,\omega ,t)\,d\theta =1.} ![{\\displaystyle \\int _{-\\pi }^{\\pi }\\rho \(\\theta ,\\omega ,t\)\\,d\\theta =1.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4a973251dff6c22379903a967b5780633f30223c)
The [continuity equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_equation "Continuity equation") for oscillator density will be       ∂ ρ ∂ t + ∂ ∂ θ [ ρ v ] = 0 , {\displaystyle {\frac {\partial \rho }{\partial t}}+{\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}[\rho v]=0,} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {\\partial \\rho }{\\partial t}}+{\\frac {\\partial }{\\partial \\theta }}\[\\rho v\]=0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dc6ebdd04b14aa243af7c3b0542a2f1baae16857)
where _v_ is the [drift velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drift_velocity "Drift velocity") of the oscillators given by taking the infinite-_N_ limit in the transformed governing equation, such that       ∂ ρ ∂ t + ∂ ∂ θ [ ρ ω + ρ K r sin ⁡ ( ψ − θ ) ] = 0. {\displaystyle {\frac {\partial \rho }{\partial t}}+{\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}[\rho \omega +\rho Kr\sin(\psi -\theta )]=0.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {\\partial \\rho }{\\partial t}}+{\\frac {\\partial }{\\partial \\theta }}\[\\rho \\omega +\\rho Kr\\sin\(\\psi -\\theta \)\]=0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7e7afb85917c47dada0e4f4677108dca735f2768)
Finally, the definition of the order parameters must be rewritten for the continuum (infinite _N_) limit.  θ i {\displaystyle \theta _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\theta _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/302b19204ed378e99ff4575341a67eebdbe5a555) must be replaced by its ensemble average (over all  ω {\displaystyle \omega } ![{\\displaystyle \\omega }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/48eff443f9de7a985bb94ca3bde20813ea737be8)) and the sum must be replaced by an integral, to give       r e i ψ = ∫ − π π e i θ ∫ − ∞ ∞ ρ ( θ , ω , t ) g ( ω ) d ω d θ . {\displaystyle re^{i\psi }=\int _{-\pi }^{\pi }e^{i\theta }\int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\rho (\theta ,\omega ,t)g(\omega )\,d\omega \,d\theta .} ![{\\displaystyle re^{i\\psi }=\\int _{-\\pi }^{\\pi }e^{i\\theta }\\int _{-\\infty }^{\\infty }\\rho \(\\theta ,\\omega ,t\)g\(\\omega \)\\,d\\omega \\,d\\theta .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/612c0d413eacbb69a042c32cb4cec40d19f2e125)
### Solutions for the large _N_ limit
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Solutions for the large N limit")]
The [incoherent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_\(physics\) "Coherence \(physics\)") state with all oscillators drifting randomly corresponds to the solution  ρ = 1 / ( 2 π ) {\displaystyle \rho =1/(2\pi )} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho =1/\(2\\pi \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ed45854666220b11a2e761d742c789b49072c5b2). In that case  r = 0 {\displaystyle r=0} ![{\\displaystyle r=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/894a83e863728b4ee2e12f3a999a09f5f2bf1c89), and there is no coherence among the oscillators. They are uniformly distributed across all possible phases, and the population is in a statistical [steady-state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady-state "Steady-state") (although individual oscillators continue to change phase in accordance with their intrinsic _ω_). 
When coupling _K_ is sufficiently strong, a fully synchronized solution is possible. In the fully synchronized state, all the oscillators share a common frequency, although their phases can be different. 
A solution for the case of partial synchronization yields a state in which only some oscillators (those near the ensemble's mean natural frequency) synchronize; other oscillators drift incoherently. Mathematically, the state has       ρ = δ ( θ − ψ − arcsin ⁡ ( ω K r ) ) {\displaystyle \rho =\delta \left(\theta -\psi -\arcsin \left({\frac {\omega }{Kr}}\right)\right)} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho =\\delta \\left\(\\theta -\\psi -\\arcsin \\left\({\\frac {\\omega }{Kr}}\\right\)\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b40e66fce8d10ae5fd83ff7f58acfcadd9479494)
for locked oscillators, and       ρ = n o r m a l i z a t i o n c o n s t a n t ( ω − K r sin ⁡ ( θ − ψ ) ) {\displaystyle \rho ={\frac {\rm {normalization\;constant}}{(\omega -Kr\sin(\theta -\psi ))}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho ={\\frac {\\rm {normalization\\;constant}}{\(\\omega -Kr\\sin\(\\theta -\\psi \)\)}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9349cb105cb7b75600a07e7aeb43940cbad7dd4a)
for drifting oscillators. The cutoff occurs when  | ω | < K r {\displaystyle |\omega |<Kr} ![{\\displaystyle |\\omega |<Kr}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/59b31c6184e3afcc763b25c28c1e2d20a272d414).

## Small _N_ cases
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Small N cases")]
When N is small, the solutions given above breaks down, as the continuum approximation cannot be used. 
The N=2 case is trivial. In the rotating frame  ω 1 = − ω 2 {\displaystyle \omega _{1}=-\omega _{2}} ![{\\displaystyle \\omega _{1}=-\\omega _{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/410b7bd92cb4add63c768c1ec668080066750e12), and so the system is described exactly by the angle between the two oscillators:  Δ θ = θ 1 − θ 2 {\displaystyle \Delta \theta =\theta _{1}-\theta _{2}} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta \\theta =\\theta _{1}-\\theta _{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5b82680e7e27e7fd46cecd60d28aa03610490d6b). When  K < K c = 2 | ω 1 | {\displaystyle K<K_{c}=2|\omega _{1}|} ![{\\displaystyle K<K_{c}=2|\\omega _{1}|}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1380cb0218eb9bf3c713e10f65422286c6d89a53), the angle cycles around the circle (that is, the fast oscillator keeps lapping around the slow oscillator). When  K > K c {\displaystyle K>K_{c}} ![{\\displaystyle K>K_{c}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9501841075f52d8cc1816d30bf8e4bac4d5b920f), the angle falls into a stable attractor (that is, the two oscillators lock in phase). Similarly, the state space of the N=3 case is a 2-dimensional torus, and so the system evolves as a flow on the 2-torus, which cannot be chaotic. 
Chaos first occurs when N=4. For some settings of  ω 1 , ω 2 , ω 3 , K {\displaystyle \omega _{1},\omega _{2},\omega _{3},K} ![{\\displaystyle \\omega _{1},\\omega _{2},\\omega _{3},K}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7b191081f64f009dac045ebe25fdc101282fb231), the system has a [strange attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractor "Strange attractor").[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-13)
A related case for N=2 is the [circle map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_map "Circle map") or [phase-locked loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase-locked_loop "Phase-locked loop"). In this model, one of the oscillators is driven at a fixed frequency (and thus no longer free to vary), while the other, weakly coupled to the driver, is free to spin arbitrarily.

## Variations of the models
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Variations of the models")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ea/Spatial_synchronization_patterns_in_variants_of_the_Kuramoto_Model.png/330px-Spatial_synchronization_patterns_in_variants_of_the_Kuramoto_Model.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Spatial_synchronization_patterns_in_variants_of_the_Kuramoto_Model.png)Distinct synchronization patterns in a two-dimensional array of Kuramoto-like oscillators with differing phase interaction functions and spatial coupling topologies. (A) Pinwheels. (B) Waves. (C) Chimeras. (D) Chimeras and waves combined. Color scale indicates oscillator phase.
There are a number of types of variations that can be applied to the original model presented above. Some models change the [topological structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space "Topological space"), others allow for heterogeneous weights, and other changes are more related to models that are inspired by the Kuramoto model but do not have the same functional form. 
### Variations of network topology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Variations of network topology")]
Beside the original model, which has an all-to-all topology, a sufficiently dense [complex network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_Network "Complex Network")-like topology is amenable to the mean-field treatment used in the solution of the original model[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-15) (see [Transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#Transformation) and [Large _N_ limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#Large_N_limit) above for more info). Network topologies such as rings and coupled populations support chimera states.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-16) One also may ask for the behavior of models in which there are intrinsically local, like one-dimensional topologies which the chain and the ring are prototypical examples. In such topologies, in which the coupling is not scalable according to 1/_N_ , it is not possible to apply the canonical mean-field approach, so one must rely upon case-by-case analysis, making use of symmetries whenever it is possible, which may give basis for abstraction of general principles of solutions. 
Uniform synchrony, waves and spirals can readily be observed in two-dimensional Kuramoto networks with diffusive local coupling. The stability of waves in these models can be determined analytically using the methods of Turing stability analysis.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-17) Uniform synchrony tends to be stable when the local coupling is everywhere positive whereas waves arise when the long-range connections are negative (inhibitory surround coupling). Waves and synchrony are connected by a topologically distinct branch of solutions known as ripple.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-18) These are low-amplitude spatially-periodic deviations that emerge from the uniform state (or the wave state) via a [Hopf bifurcation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopf_bifurcation "Hopf bifurcation").[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-19) The existence of ripple solutions was predicted (but not observed) by Wiley, Strogatz and [Girvan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_Girvan "Michelle Girvan"),[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-:0-20) who called them multi-twisted q-states. 
The topology on which the Kuramoto model is studied can be made adaptive[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-21) by use of [fitness model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_model_\(network_theory\) "Fitness model \(network theory\)") showing enhancement of synchronization and [percolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuropercolation "Neuropercolation") in a self-organised way. 
A graph with the minimal degree at least  d min ≥ 0.5 n {\displaystyle d_{\text{min}}\geq 0.5\,n} ![{\\displaystyle d_{\\text{min}}\\geq 0.5\\,n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f934aea9627244030843e40a16d8ceb2780efd1b) will be connected nevertheless for a graph to synchronize a little more it is required for such case it is known that there is critical connectivity threshold  μ c {\displaystyle \mu _{c}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mu _{c}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f1068445986eeca096bead2b6261b5c9f2221b61) such that any graph on  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) nodes with minimum degree  d min ≥ μ c ( n − 1 ) {\displaystyle d_{\text{min}}\geq \mu _{c}(n-1)} ![{\\displaystyle d_{\\text{min}}\\geq \\mu _{c}\(n-1\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d57a301d07f1975b0ddc6412d75021e68535a3c6) must globally synchronise.for  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) large enough. The minimum[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-:0-20)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-22) maximum[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-23) are known to lie between  0.6875 ≤ μ c ≤ 0.75 {\displaystyle 0.6875\leq \mu _{c}\leq 0.75} ![{\\displaystyle 0.6875\\leq \\mu _{c}\\leq 0.75}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0b707cbf29234447b48ec066268ce402c7b931f5). 
Similarly it is known that [Erdős-Rényi graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erd%C5%91s%E2%80%93R%C3%A9nyi_model "Erdős–Rényi model") with edge probability precisely  p = ( 1 + ϵ ) ln ⁡ ( n ) / n {\displaystyle p=(1+\epsilon )\ln(n)/n} ![{\\displaystyle p=\(1+\\epsilon \)\\ln\(n\)/n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d39e578edae6c72f74f9baee24370e86d0ba5c5b) as  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) goes to infinity will be connected and it has been conjectured[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-24) that this value is too the number at which these random graphs undergo synchronization which a 2022 preprint claims to have proved.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-25)[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-26)
### Variations of network topology and network weights: from vehicle coordination to brain synchronization
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Variations of network topology and network weights: from vehicle coordination to brain synchronization")]
[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wesphysdemo_-_Synchronized_Metronomes.webm "Play video")Duration: 2 minutes and 7 seconds.2:07[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wesphysdemo_-_Synchronized_Metronomes.webm)[Metronomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metronome "Metronome"), initially out of phase, synchronize through small motions of the base on which they are placed. This system has been shown to be equivalent to the Kuramoto model.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-27)
Some works in the control community have focused on the Kuramoto model on networks and with heterogeneous weights (i.e. the interconnection strength between any two oscillators can be arbitrary). The dynamics of this model reads as follows:       d θ i d t = ω i + ∑ j = 1 N a i j sin ⁡ ( θ j − θ i ) , i = 1 … N {\displaystyle {\frac {d\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\omega _{i}+\sum _{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}\sin(\theta _{j}-\theta _{i}),\qquad i=1\ldots N} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {d\\theta _{i}}{dt}}=\\omega _{i}+\\sum _{j=1}^{N}a_{ij}\\sin\(\\theta _{j}-\\theta _{i}\),\\qquad i=1\\ldots N}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/56fa6c5595fcc02c523177fdb91dcf3333cd128b)
where  a i j {\displaystyle a_{ij}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{ij}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebea6cd2813c330c798921a2894b358f7b643917) is a nonzero positive real number if oscillator  j {\displaystyle j} ![{\\displaystyle j}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2f461e54f5c093e92a55547b9764291390f0b5d0) is connected to oscillator  i {\displaystyle i} ![{\\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20). Such model allows for a more realistic study of, e.g., power grids,[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-28) flocking, schooling, and vehicle coordination.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-29) In the work from Dörfler and colleagues, several theorems provide rigorous conditions for phase and frequency synchronization of this model. Further studies, motivated by experimental observations in neuroscience, focus on deriving analytical conditions for cluster synchronization of heterogeneous Kuramoto oscillators on arbitrary network topologies.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-30)
Since the Kuramoto model seems to play a key role in assessing synchronization phenomena in the brain,[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-31) theoretical conditions that support empirical findings may pave the way for a deeper understanding of neuronal synchronization phenomena. 
### Variations of the phase interaction function
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Kuramoto_model&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Variations of the phase interaction function")]
Kuramoto approximated the phase interaction between any two oscillators by its first Fourier component, namely  Γ ( ϕ ) = sin ⁡ ( ϕ ) {\displaystyle \Gamma (\phi )=\sin(\phi )} ![{\\displaystyle \\Gamma \(\\phi \)=\\sin\(\\phi \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81859b87add6a37188aeb292b4b9c0d816a02c16), where  ϕ = θ j − θ i {\displaystyle \phi =\theta _{j}-\theta _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi =\\theta _{j}-\\theta _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/89dc44a3e05026d1700cac9f49dc48d5dff3b491). Better approximations can be obtained by including higher-order Fourier components,       Γ ( ϕ ) = sin ⁡ ( ϕ ) + a 1 sin ⁡ ( 2 ϕ + b 1 ) + . . . + a n sin ⁡ ( 2 n ϕ + b n ) {\displaystyle \Gamma (\phi )=\sin(\phi )+a_{1}\sin(2\phi +b_{1})+...+a_{n}\sin(2n\phi +b_{n})} ![{\\displaystyle \\Gamma \(\\phi \)=\\sin\(\\phi \)+a_{1}\\sin\(2\\phi +b_{1}\)+...+a_{n}\\sin\(2n\\phi +b_{n}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b0d55039c84b3661bfafaa0c06a9fd6ac91e9994),
where parameters  a i {\displaystyle a_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0bc77764b2e74e64a63341054fa90f3e07db275f) and  b i {\displaystyle b_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle b_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/40a8c2db2990a53c683e75961826167c5adac7c3) must be estimated. For example, synchronization among a network of weakly-coupled [Hodgkin–Huxley neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodgkin%E2%80%93Huxley_model "Hodgkin–Huxley model") can be replicated using coupled oscillators that retain the first four Fourier components of the interaction function.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-32) The introduction of higher-order phase interaction terms can also induce interesting dynamical phenomena such as partially synchronized states,[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-33) [heteroclinic cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heteroclinic_cycle "Heteroclinic cycle"),[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-34) and [chaotic dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaos_theory "Chaos theory").[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuramoto_model#cite_note-35)
