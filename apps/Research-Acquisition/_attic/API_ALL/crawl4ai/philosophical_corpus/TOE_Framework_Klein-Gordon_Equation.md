The **Klein–Gordon equation** (**Klein–Fock–Gordon equation** or sometimes **Klein–Gordon–Fock equation** , in earlier publications **Schrödinger–Gordon equation**) is a [relativistic wave equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_wave_equation "Relativistic wave equation"), related to the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger_equation "Schrödinger equation"). It is named after [Oskar Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Klein "Oskar Klein") and [Walter Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gordon_\(physicist\) "Walter Gordon \(physicist\)") (and after [Vladimir Fock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Fock "Vladimir Fock") in alternative names). It is second-order in space and time and manifestly [Lorentz-covariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_covariance "Lorentz covariance"). It is a [differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation "Differential equation") version of the relativistic [energy–momentum relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy%E2%80%93momentum_relation "Energy–momentum relation") E 2 = ( p c ) 2 + ( m 0 c 2 ) 2 {\displaystyle E^{2}=(pc)^{2}+\left(m_{0}c^{2}\right)^{2}\,} ![{\\displaystyle E^{2}=\(pc\)^{2}+\\left\(m_{0}c^{2}\\right\)^{2}\\,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4633e931262b0ed323e0093017d24caccc9df6db).

## Relevance
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Relevance")]
The equation is to be understood first as a classical continuous scalar field equation that can be quantized. The [quantization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_quantization "Second quantization") process introduces then a quantum field whose quanta are spinless particles. Its theoretical relevance is similar to that of the [Dirac equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_equation "Dirac equation").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-2) The equation solutions include a [scalar or pseudoscalar field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field "Scalar field")[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_]. In the realm of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics") electromagnetic interactions can be incorporated, forming the topic of [scalar electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_electrodynamics "Scalar electrodynamics"), the practical utility for particles like [pions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion") is limited.[[nb 1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-3)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-4) There is a second version of the equation for a complex scalar field that is theoretically important being the equation of the [Higgs Boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_Boson "Higgs Boson"). In the realm of [condensed matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter "Condensed matter") it can be used for many approximations of quasi-particles without spin.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-5)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-6)[[nb 2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-7)
The equation can be put into the form of a Schrödinger equation. In this form it is expressed as two coupled differential equations, each of first order in time.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-:0-8) The solutions have two components, reflecting the charge degree of freedom in relativity.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-:0-8)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-9) It admits a conserved quantity, but this is not positive definite. The wave function cannot therefore be interpreted as a [probability amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude "Probability amplitude"). The conserved quantity is instead interpreted as [electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge "Electric charge"), and the norm squared of the wave function is interpreted as a [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density "Charge density"). The equation describes all spinless particles with positive, negative, and zero charge. 
Any solution of the free Dirac equation is, for each of its four components, a solution of the free Klein–Gordon equation. Although historically it was invented as a single particle equation, the Klein–Gordon equation cannot form the basis of a consistent quantum relativistic _one-particle_ theory; any relativistic theory implies creation and annihilation of particles beyond a certain energy threshold.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-10)[[nb 3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-11)

## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: History")]
The equation was named after the physicists [Oskar Klein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oskar_Klein "Oskar Klein")[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-12) and [Walter Gordon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Gordon_\(physicist\) "Walter Gordon \(physicist\)"),[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-13) who in 1926 proposed that it describes relativistic electrons. [Vladimir Fock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Fock "Vladimir Fock") also discovered the equation independently in 1926 slightly after Klein's work,[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-14) in that Klein's paper was received on 28 April 1926, Fock's paper was received on 30 July 1926 and Gordon's paper on 29 September 1926. Other authors making similar claims in that same year include Johann Kudar, [Théophile de Donder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9ophile_de_Donder "Théophile de Donder") and [Frans-H. van den Dungen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frans-H._van_den_Dungen "Frans-H. van den Dungen"), and [Louis de Broglie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_de_Broglie "Louis de Broglie"). Although it turned out that modeling the electron's spin required the Dirac equation, the Klein–Gordon equation correctly describes the spinless relativistic [composite particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_particle "Composite particle"), like the [pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion"). On 4 July 2012, European Organization for Nuclear Research [CERN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CERN "CERN") announced the discovery of the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson "Higgs boson"). Since the Higgs boson is a spin-zero particle, it is the first observed ostensibly [elementary particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle "Elementary particle") to be described by the Klein–Gordon equation. Further experimentation and analysis is required to discern whether the Higgs boson observed is that of the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") or a more exotic, possibly composite, form. 
The Klein–Gordon equation was first considered as a quantum wave equation by [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schr%C3%B6dinger "Erwin Schrödinger") in his search for an equation describing [de Broglie waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie_wave "De Broglie wave"). The equation is found in his notebooks from late 1925, and he appears to have prepared a manuscript applying it to the hydrogen atom. Yet, because it fails to take into account the electron's spin, the equation predicts the hydrogen atom's fine structure incorrectly, including overestimating the overall magnitude of the splitting pattern by a factor of ⁠4 _n_ /2 _n_ − 1⁠ for the _n_ -th energy level. The Dirac equation relativistic spectrum is, however, easily recovered if the orbital-momentum quantum number l is replaced by total angular-momentum quantum number j.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-Itzykson&Zuber-15) In January 1926, Schrödinger submitted for publication instead _his_ equation, a non-relativistic approximation that predicts the Bohr energy levels of hydrogen without [fine structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine_structure "Fine structure"). 
In 1926, soon after the Schrödinger equation was introduced, [Vladimir Fock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Fock "Vladimir Fock") wrote an article about its generalization for the case of [magnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field "Magnetic field"), where [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force "Force") were dependent on [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity "Velocity"), and independently derived this equation. Both Klein and Fock used [Theodor Kaluza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_Kaluza "Theodor Kaluza") and Klein's method. Fock also determined the [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory "Gauge theory") for the [wave equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_equation "Wave equation"). The Klein–Gordon equation for a [free particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_particle "Free particle") has a simple [plane-wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane-wave "Plane-wave") solution. 
## Derivation
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Derivation")]
The non-relativistic equation for the energy of a free particle is       p 2 2 m = E . {\displaystyle {\frac {\mathbf {p} ^{2}}{2m}}=E.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {\\mathbf {p} ^{2}}{2m}}=E.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5ceee69f37ded361b2dac87b337532ea346bc467)
By quantizing this, we get the non-relativistic Schrödinger equation for a free particle:       p ^ 2 2 m ψ = E ^ ψ , {\displaystyle {\frac {\mathbf {\hat {p}} ^{2}}{2m}}\psi ={\hat {E}}\psi ,} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {\\mathbf {\\hat {p}} ^{2}}{2m}}\\psi ={\\hat {E}}\\psi ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b1678bf946036a069213686b9fe40584789cdab2)
where       p ^ = − i ℏ ∇ {\displaystyle \mathbf {\hat {p}} =-i\hbar \mathbf {\nabla } } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbf {\\hat {p}} =-i\\hbar \\mathbf {\\nabla } }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/84734d56a01b8fa67dbebcd19c02e8efd50c84b0)
is the [momentum operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum_operator "Momentum operator") (∇ being the [del operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Del "Del")), and       E ^ = i ℏ ∂ ∂ t {\displaystyle {\hat {E}}=i\hbar {\frac {\partial }{\partial t}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\hat {E}}=i\\hbar {\\frac {\\partial }{\\partial t}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7c7b09aea0927e2602cf2b856b3a6a042269c444)
is the [energy operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_operator "Energy operator"). 
The Schrödinger equation suffers from not being [relativistically invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_group "Poincaré group"), meaning that it is inconsistent with [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"). 
It is natural to try to use the identity from special relativity describing the energy:       p 2 c 2 + m 2 c 4 = E . {\displaystyle {\sqrt {\mathbf {p} ^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}}}=E.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\sqrt {\\mathbf {p} ^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}}}=E.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2bbeb7b2f0bf127303831428316a563a28e4b84e)
Then, just inserting the quantum-mechanical operators for momentum and energy yields the equation       ( − i ℏ ∇ ) 2 c 2 + m 2 c 4 ψ = i ℏ ∂ ∂ t ψ . {\displaystyle {\sqrt {(-i\hbar \mathbf {\nabla } )^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}}}\,\psi =i\hbar {\frac {\partial }{\partial t}}\psi .} ![{\\displaystyle {\\sqrt {\(-i\\hbar \\mathbf {\\nabla } \)^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}}}\\,\\psi =i\\hbar {\\frac {\\partial }{\\partial t}}\\psi .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8be0aea57908877df092739f936c2f4d7a6eae70)
The square root of a differential operator can be defined with the help of [Fourier transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform "Fourier transform"), but due to the asymmetry of space and time derivatives, Dirac found it impossible to include external electromagnetic fields in a relativistically invariant way. So he looked for another equation that can be modified in order to describe the action of electromagnetic forces. In addition, this equation, as it stands, is [nonlocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_at_a_distance "Action at a distance") (see also [Introduction to nonlocal equations](https://web.ma.utexas.edu/mediawiki/index.php/Introduction_to_nonlocal_equations)). 
Klein and Gordon instead began with the square of the above identity, i.e.       p 2 c 2 + m 2 c 4 = E 2 , {\displaystyle \mathbf {p} ^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}=E^{2},} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbf {p} ^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}=E^{2},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d8942e9c66f8c119a82a792450af0db8629e8de2)
which, when quantized, gives       ( ( − i ℏ ∇ ) 2 c 2 + m 2 c 4 ) ψ = ( i ℏ ∂ ∂ t ) 2 ψ , {\displaystyle \left((-i\hbar \mathbf {\nabla } )^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}\right)\psi =\left(i\hbar {\frac {\partial }{\partial t}}\right)^{2}\psi ,} ![{\\displaystyle \\left\(\(-i\\hbar \\mathbf {\\nabla } \)^{2}c^{2}+m^{2}c^{4}\\right\)\\psi =\\left\(i\\hbar {\\frac {\\partial }{\\partial t}}\\right\)^{2}\\psi ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/23e2c4f918bd966b023bea3841cf0c390fbff8a8)
which simplifies to       − ℏ 2 c 2 ∇ 2 ψ + m 2 c 4 ψ = − ℏ 2 ∂ 2 ∂ t 2 ψ . {\displaystyle -\hbar ^{2}c^{2}\mathbf {\nabla } ^{2}\psi +m^{2}c^{4}\psi =-\hbar ^{2}{\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}}\psi .} ![{\\displaystyle -\\hbar ^{2}c^{2}\\mathbf {\\nabla } ^{2}\\psi +m^{2}c^{4}\\psi =-\\hbar ^{2}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial t^{2}}}\\psi .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/350f48fa94dc16ef403f46e6b25bc831ad20b3d9)
Rearranging terms yields       1 c 2 ∂ 2 ∂ t 2 ψ − ∇ 2 ψ + m 2 c 2 ℏ 2 ψ = 0. {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}}\psi -\mathbf {\nabla } ^{2}\psi +{\frac {m^{2}c^{2}}{\hbar ^{2}}}\psi =0.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial t^{2}}}\\psi -\\mathbf {\\nabla } ^{2}\\psi +{\\frac {m^{2}c^{2}}{\\hbar ^{2}}}\\psi =0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/57fb5e431ce6fd37ec4d822184c1f3a30e91182f)
Since all reference to imaginary numbers has been eliminated from this equation, it can be applied to fields that are [real-valued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number "Real number"), as well as those that have [complex values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number "Complex number"). 
Rewriting the first two terms using the inverse of the [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric "Minkowski metric") diag(− _c_ 2, 1, 1, 1), and writing the Einstein summation convention explicitly we get       − η μ ν ∂ μ ∂ ν ψ ≡ ∑ μ = 0 3 ∑ ν = 0 3 − η μ ν ∂ μ ∂ ν ψ = 1 c 2 ∂ 0 2 ψ − ∑ ν = 1 3 ∂ ν ∂ ν ψ = 1 c 2 ∂ 2 ∂ t 2 ψ − ∇ 2 ψ . {\displaystyle -\eta ^{\mu \nu }\partial _{\mu }\,\partial _{\nu }\psi \equiv \sum _{\mu =0}^{3}\sum _{\nu =0}^{3}-\eta ^{\mu \nu }\partial _{\mu }\,\partial _{\nu }\psi ={\frac {1}{c^{2}}}\partial _{0}^{2}\psi -\sum _{\nu =1}^{3}\partial _{\nu }\,\partial _{\nu }\psi ={\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}}\psi -\mathbf {\nabla } ^{2}\psi .} ![{\\displaystyle -\\eta ^{\\mu \\nu }\\partial _{\\mu }\\,\\partial _{\\nu }\\psi \\equiv \\sum _{\\mu =0}^{3}\\sum _{\\nu =0}^{3}-\\eta ^{\\mu \\nu }\\partial _{\\mu }\\,\\partial _{\\nu }\\psi ={\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}\\partial _{0}^{2}\\psi -\\sum _{\\nu =1}^{3}\\partial _{\\nu }\\,\\partial _{\\nu }\\psi ={\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial t^{2}}}\\psi -\\mathbf {\\nabla } ^{2}\\psi .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/30fc5f6ac26b61349d135ad1ad03452c06ee0c15)
Thus the Klein–Gordon equation can be written in a covariant notation. This often means an abbreviation in the form of       ( ◻ + μ 2 ) ψ = 0 , {\displaystyle (\Box +\mu ^{2})\psi =0,} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\Box +\\mu ^{2}\)\\psi =0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dc15e77be20ed49dc0584aa006bfd5135ca95062)
where       μ = m c ℏ , {\displaystyle \mu ={\frac {mc}{\hbar }},} ![{\\displaystyle \\mu ={\\frac {mc}{\\hbar }},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6c2118e927ae2e88a243d76b2f95dd4253e1f7bd)
and       ◻ = 1 c 2 ∂ 2 ∂ t 2 − ∇ 2 . {\displaystyle \Box ={\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}}-\nabla ^{2}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\Box ={\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial t^{2}}}-\\nabla ^{2}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2946a2e54afe75f3e9aa29601cf4407db25ec390)
This operator is called the [wave operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_operator "Wave operator"). 
Today this form is interpreted as the relativistic [field equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_equation "Field equation") for [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_\(physics\) "Spin \(physics\)")-0 particles.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-:0-8) Furthermore, any _component_ of any solution to the free Dirac equation (for a [spin-1/2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin-1/2 "Spin-1/2") particle) is automatically a solution to the free Klein–Gordon equation. This generalizes to particles of any spin due to the [Bargmann–Wigner equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bargmann%E2%80%93Wigner_equations "Bargmann–Wigner equations"). Furthermore, in quantum field theory, every component of every quantum field must satisfy the free Klein–Gordon equation,[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_note-16) making the equation a generic expression of quantum fields.

#### Higgs sector
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Higgs sector")]
See also: [Higgs sector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_sector "Higgs sector")
The pure Higgs boson sector of the Standard model is modelled by a Klein–Gordon field with a potential, denoted  H {\displaystyle H} ![{\\displaystyle H}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/75a9edddcca2f782014371f75dca39d7e13a9c1b) for this section. The Standard model is a gauge theory and so while the field transforms trivially under the [Lorentz group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_group "Lorentz group"), it transforms as a  C 2 {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} ^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {C} ^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6f43d6ec8a1e1fe5a85aec0dd9bdcd45ae09b06b)-valued vector under the action of the  SU ( 2 ) {\displaystyle {\text{SU}}(2)} ![{\\displaystyle {\\text{SU}}\(2\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e1850368e362fa0be265cbb948aca0ff64a83342) part of the gauge group. Therefore, while it is a vector field  H : R 1 , 3 → C 2 {\displaystyle H:\mathbb {R} ^{1,3}\rightarrow \mathbb {C} ^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle H:\\mathbb {R} ^{1,3}\\rightarrow \\mathbb {C} ^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c9f9294f1fa7ff2ca9ab3409213770368e8a5964), it is still referred to as a scalar field, as scalar describes its transformation (formally, representation) under the Lorentz group. This is also discussed below in the scalar chromodynamics section. 
The Higgs field is modelled by a potential       V ( H ) = − m 2 H † H + λ ( H † H ) 2 {\displaystyle V(H)=-m^{2}H^{\dagger }H+\lambda (H^{\dagger }H)^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle V\(H\)=-m^{2}H^{\\dagger }H+\\lambda \(H^{\\dagger }H\)^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7318f0c77050a5a31617d1a0bc2aa4b4e92a1895),
which can be viewed as a generalization of the  ϕ 4 {\displaystyle \phi ^{4}} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi ^{4}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8e3f7052b477f76bbe009fc399bd9bf28b08510b) potential, but has an important difference: it has a circle of minima. This observation is an important one in the theory of [spontaneous symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_symmetry_breaking "Spontaneous symmetry breaking") in the Standard model.

### Quantum field
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Quantum field")]
The analogous limit of a quantum Klein–Gordon field is complicated by the non-commutativity of the field operator. In the limit _v_ ≪ _c_ , the [creation and annihilation operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_and_annihilation_operators "Creation and annihilation operators") decouple and behave as independent quantum [Schrödinger fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger_field "Schrödinger field"). 
## Scalar electrodynamics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Scalar electrodynamics")]
See also: [Scalar electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_electrodynamics "Scalar electrodynamics")
There is a way to make the complex Klein–Gordon field  ψ {\displaystyle \psi } ![{\\displaystyle \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45e5789e5d9c8f7c79744f43ecaaf8ba42a8553a) interact with electromagnetism in a [gauge-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory "Gauge theory") way. We can replace the (partial) derivative with the gauge-covariant derivative. Under a local  U ( 1 ) {\displaystyle {\text{U}}(1)} ![{\\displaystyle {\\text{U}}\(1\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/799f83909c3169d94893468b6aa2e83b40fee010) gauge transformation, the fields transform as       ψ ↦ ψ ′ = e i θ ( x ) ψ , {\displaystyle \psi \mapsto \psi '=e^{i\theta (x)}\psi ,} ![{\\displaystyle \\psi \\mapsto \\psi '=e^{i\\theta \(x\)}\\psi ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bf50ee6565721d9c6819e5410886a28afd150682)      ψ ¯ ↦ ψ ¯ ′ = e − i θ ( x ) ψ ¯ , {\displaystyle {\bar {\psi }}\mapsto {\bar {\psi }}'=e^{-i\theta (x)}{\bar {\psi }},} ![{\\displaystyle {\\bar {\\psi }}\\mapsto {\\bar {\\psi }}'=e^{-i\\theta \(x\)}{\\bar {\\psi }},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5b62c4c943ae78ec00632426c4d64abff2ebefc2)
where  θ ( x ) = θ ( t , x ) {\displaystyle \theta (x)=\theta (t,{\textbf {x}})} ![{\\displaystyle \\theta \(x\)=\\theta \(t,{\\textbf {x}}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/41d503f998c308eeede5345d6973fcae7d8708e7) is a function of spacetime, thus making it a local transformation, as opposed to a constant over all of spacetime, which would be a global  U ( 1 ) {\displaystyle {\text{U}}(1)} ![{\\displaystyle {\\text{U}}\(1\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/799f83909c3169d94893468b6aa2e83b40fee010) transformation. A subtle point is that global transformations can arise as local ones, when the function  θ ( x ) {\displaystyle \theta (x)} ![{\\displaystyle \\theta \(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/de0d0d32f86079d2585f75430af6125a48802d4f) is taken to be a constant function. 
A well-formulated theory should be invariant under such transformations. Precisely, this means that the equations of motion and action (see below) are invariant. To achieve this, ordinary derivatives  ∂ μ {\displaystyle \partial _{\mu }} ![{\\displaystyle \\partial _{\\mu }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1be3e5cf12940b60f8569a9927ad28823277177a) must be replaced by gauge-covariant derivatives  D μ {\displaystyle D_{\mu }} ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mu }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/267983ed233945e70f42935df28e623cfce15c12), defined as       D μ ψ = ( ∂ μ − i e A μ ) ψ {\displaystyle D_{\mu }\psi =(\partial _{\mu }-ieA_{\mu })\psi } ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mu }\\psi =\(\\partial _{\\mu }-ieA_{\\mu }\)\\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/93e463fbe126596f812627b23ba59f6a0c8a38e8)      D μ ψ ¯ = ( ∂ μ + i e A μ ) ψ ¯ {\displaystyle D_{\mu }{\bar {\psi }}=(\partial _{\mu }+ieA_{\mu }){\bar {\psi }}} ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mu }{\\bar {\\psi }}=\(\\partial _{\\mu }+ieA_{\\mu }\){\\bar {\\psi }}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/272d41878287809054b8ba458452359301aa7c34)
where the 4-potential or gauge field  A μ {\displaystyle A_{\mu }} ![{\\displaystyle A_{\\mu }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9277f5286335ab99c040c9c9151ab752d3bedc49) transforms under a gauge transformation  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af) as       A μ ↦ A μ ′ = A μ + 1 e ∂ μ θ {\displaystyle A_{\mu }\mapsto A'_{\mu }=A_{\mu }+{\frac {1}{e}}\partial _{\mu }\theta } ![{\\displaystyle A_{\\mu }\\mapsto A'_{\\mu }=A_{\\mu }+{\\frac {1}{e}}\\partial _{\\mu }\\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebbd350f9cf8916f3b5ab44b8062a3f1c61c60d9).
With these definitions, the covariant derivative transforms as       D μ ψ ↦ e i θ D μ ψ {\displaystyle D_{\mu }\psi \mapsto e^{i\theta }D_{\mu }\psi } ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mu }\\psi \\mapsto e^{i\\theta }D_{\\mu }\\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bfe65b580380b2c643c486603120b53b25f2f53d)
In natural units, the Klein–Gordon equation therefore becomes       D μ D μ ψ − M 2 ψ = 0. {\displaystyle D_{\mu }D^{\mu }\psi -M^{2}\psi =0.} ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mu }D^{\\mu }\\psi -M^{2}\\psi =0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f2beb6bf9873f0ab74f23737674e50eca77edfc9)
Since an _ungauged_ U ( 1 ) {\displaystyle {\text{U}}(1)} ![{\\displaystyle {\\text{U}}\(1\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/799f83909c3169d94893468b6aa2e83b40fee010) symmetry is only present in complex Klein–Gordon theory, this coupling and promotion to a _gauged_ U ( 1 ) {\displaystyle {\text{U}}(1)} ![{\\displaystyle {\\text{U}}\(1\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/799f83909c3169d94893468b6aa2e83b40fee010) symmetry is compatible only with complex Klein–Gordon theory and not real Klein–Gordon theory. 
In natural units and mostly minus signature we have 
**Scalar QED action**
S = ∫ d 4 x ( − 1 4 F μ ν F μ ν + D μ ψ D μ ψ ¯ − M 2 ψ ψ ¯ ) {\displaystyle S=\int d^{4}x\,\left(-{\frac {1}{4}}F^{\mu \nu }F_{\mu \nu }+D^{\mu }\psi D_{\mu }{\bar {\psi }}-M^{2}\psi {\bar {\psi }}\right)} ![{\\displaystyle S=\\int d^{4}x\\,\\left\(-{\\frac {1}{4}}F^{\\mu \\nu }F_{\\mu \\nu }+D^{\\mu }\\psi D_{\\mu }{\\bar {\\psi }}-M^{2}\\psi {\\bar {\\psi }}\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9b91997ca0eeb8dbe9a110e46e1adb4ed40cad53)
where  F μ ν = ∂ μ A ν − ∂ ν A μ {\displaystyle F_{\mu \nu }=\partial _{\mu }A_{\nu }-\partial _{\nu }A_{\mu }} ![{\\displaystyle F_{\\mu \\nu }=\\partial _{\\mu }A_{\\nu }-\\partial _{\\nu }A_{\\mu }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/77fbf0c39a13f9706357f83b041774749598ded1) is known as the Maxwell tensor, field strength or curvature depending on viewpoint. 
This theory is often known as scalar quantum electrodynamics or scalar QED, although all aspects we've discussed here are classical. 
### Scalar chromodynamics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Scalar chromodynamics")]
It is possible to extend this to a non-abelian gauge theory with a gauge group  G {\displaystyle G} ![{\\displaystyle G}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f5f3c8921a3b352de45446a6789b104458c9f90b), where we couple the scalar Klein–Gordon action to a [Yang–Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang%E2%80%93Mills "Yang–Mills") Lagrangian. Here, the field is actually vector-valued, but is still described as a scalar field: the scalar describes its transformation under [space-time transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_group "Poincaré group"), but not its transformation under the action of the gauge group. 
For concreteness we fix  G {\displaystyle G} ![{\\displaystyle G}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f5f3c8921a3b352de45446a6789b104458c9f90b) to be  SU ( N ) {\displaystyle {\text{SU}}(N)} ![{\\displaystyle {\\text{SU}}\(N\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d40b4f913e99b50055f5d7e7c4a8c9b9e05623cd), the [special unitary group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_unitary_group "Special unitary group") for some  N ≥ 2 {\displaystyle N\geq 2} ![{\\displaystyle N\\geq 2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4a366b1cf2a65d58e6be08fede1a447e0771926b). Under a gauge transformation  U ( x ) {\displaystyle U(x)} ![{\\displaystyle U\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6d626d3a1e65c94535c811c73fa83389cfb76683), which can be described as a function  U : R 1 , 3 → SU ( N ) , {\displaystyle U:\mathbb {R} ^{1,3}\rightarrow {\text{SU}}(N),} ![{\\displaystyle U:\\mathbb {R} ^{1,3}\\rightarrow {\\text{SU}}\(N\),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8b9ffe9941b895ff3f70af568a0614106d45ac8c) the scalar field  ψ {\displaystyle \psi } ![{\\displaystyle \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45e5789e5d9c8f7c79744f43ecaaf8ba42a8553a) transforms as a  C N {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} ^{N}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {C} ^{N}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f1a093c818bd6ea93f8a94d0aed556d907c4ba46) vector       ψ ( x ) ↦ U ( x ) ψ ( x ) {\displaystyle \psi (x)\mapsto U(x)\psi (x)} ![{\\displaystyle \\psi \(x\)\\mapsto U\(x\)\\psi \(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/55c68a1dbbabe7cc4855bd5f42a1719ab19e257b)      ψ † ( x ) ↦ ψ † ( x ) U † ( x ) {\displaystyle \psi ^{\dagger }(x)\mapsto \psi ^{\dagger }(x)U^{\dagger }(x)} ![{\\displaystyle \\psi ^{\\dagger }\(x\)\\mapsto \\psi ^{\\dagger }\(x\)U^{\\dagger }\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/104f2e5d2799f1938c256f1003e94c93ae3324e8).
The covariant derivative is       D μ ψ = ∂ μ ψ − i g A μ ψ {\displaystyle D_{\mu }\psi =\partial _{\mu }\psi -igA_{\mu }\psi } ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mu }\\psi =\\partial _{\\mu }\\psi -igA_{\\mu }\\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/309b5128474a543335d00cc862f80383fc0814fb)      D μ ψ † = ∂ μ ψ † + i g ψ † A μ † {\displaystyle D_{\mu }\psi ^{\dagger }=\partial _{\mu }\psi ^{\dagger }+ig\psi ^{\dagger }A_{\mu }^{\dagger }} ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mu }\\psi ^{\\dagger }=\\partial _{\\mu }\\psi ^{\\dagger }+ig\\psi ^{\\dagger }A_{\\mu }^{\\dagger }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ba47271bf81beb98eaf55b9beb9b6dc86fbdc7f3)
where the gauge field or connection transforms as       A μ ↦ U A μ U − 1 − i g ∂ μ U U − 1 . {\displaystyle A_{\mu }\mapsto UA_{\mu }U^{-1}-{\frac {i}{g}}\partial _{\mu }UU^{-1}.} ![{\\displaystyle A_{\\mu }\\mapsto UA_{\\mu }U^{-1}-{\\frac {i}{g}}\\partial _{\\mu }UU^{-1}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9623cea5cf211ea87c3bbe46924054b71e4f96a1)
This field can be seen as a matrix valued field which acts on the vector space  C N {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} ^{N}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {C} ^{N}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f1a093c818bd6ea93f8a94d0aed556d907c4ba46). 
Finally defining the chromomagnetic field strength or curvature,       F μ ν = ∂ μ A ν − ∂ ν A μ + g ( A μ A ν − A ν A μ ) , {\displaystyle F_{\mu \nu }=\partial _{\mu }A_{\nu }-\partial _{\nu }A_{\mu }+g(A_{\mu }A_{\nu }-A_{\nu }A_{\mu }),} ![{\\displaystyle F_{\\mu \\nu }=\\partial _{\\mu }A_{\\nu }-\\partial _{\\nu }A_{\\mu }+g\(A_{\\mu }A_{\\nu }-A_{\\nu }A_{\\mu }\),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/873f7ce7e1446ff5991825d2268d35bf15a78fc1)

  2. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_ref-7 "Jump up")** The [Sine-Gordon equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine-Gordon_equation "Sine-Gordon equation") is an important example of an [Integrable system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_system "Integrable system")
  3. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein%E2%80%93Gordon_equation#cite_ref-11 "Jump up")** To reconcile quantum mechanics with special relativity a multiple particle theory and therefore [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory") is needed, in which the Klein–Gordon equation reemerges as the equation obeyed by the components of all [_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] free quantum fields.   
[Steven Weinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Weinberg "Steven Weinberg") makes a point about this. He leaves out the treatment of relativistic wave mechanics altogether in his otherwise complete introduction to modern applications of quantum mechanics, explaining: "It seems to me that the way this is usually presented in books on quantum mechanics is profoundly misleading." (From the preface in _Lectures on Quantum Mechanics_ , referring to treatments of the Dirac equation in its original flavor.)   
Others, like [Walter Greiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Greiner "Walter Greiner") does in his series on theoretical physics, give a full account of the historical development and view of [relativistic quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_quantum_mechanics "Relativistic quantum mechanics") before they get to the modern interpretation, with the rationale that it is highly desirable or even necessary from a pedagogical point of view to take the long route. In quantum field theory, the solutions of the free (noninteracting) versions of the original equations still play a role. They are needed to build the Hilbert space ([Fock space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fock_space "Fock space")) and to express quantum fields by using complete sets (spanning sets of Hilbert space) of wave functions.
