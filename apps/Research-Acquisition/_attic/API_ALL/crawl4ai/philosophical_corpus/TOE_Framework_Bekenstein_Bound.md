Upper limit on entropy in physics
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Bekenstein-Hawking_entropy_of_a_black_hole.svg/250px-Bekenstein-Hawking_entropy_of_a_black_hole.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bekenstein-Hawking_entropy_of_a_black_hole.svg)According to the Bekenstein bound, the [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy") of a [black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole "Black hole") is proportional to the number of [Planck areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_units "Planck units") that it would take to cover the black hole's [event horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_horizon "Event horizon"). 
In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), the **Bekenstein bound** (named after [Jacob Bekenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Bekenstein "Jacob Bekenstein")) is an upper limit on the [thermodynamic entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_\(statistical_thermodynamics\) "Entropy \(statistical thermodynamics\)") _S_ , or [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_\(Information_theory\) "Entropy \(Information theory\)") _H_ , that can be contained within a given finite region of space which has a finite amount of energy—or equivalently, the maximum amount of information that is required to perfectly describe a given physical system down to the quantum level.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein1981-1-1) It implies that the information of a physical system, or the information necessary to perfectly describe that system, must be finite if the region of space and the energy are finite. 
## Equations
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bekenstein_bound&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Equations")]
The universal form of the bound was originally found by Jacob Bekenstein in 1981 as the [inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_\(mathematics\) "Inequality \(mathematics\)")[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein1981-1-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein2005-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein2008-3) S ≤ 2 π k R E ℏ c , {\displaystyle S\leq {\frac {2\pi kRE}{\hbar c}},} ![{\\displaystyle S\\leq {\\frac {2\\pi kRE}{\\hbar c}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9e924291bb44617ac2da9f3a1b584eddf5d70446) where _S_ is the [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy"), _k_ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant "Boltzmann constant"), _R_ is the [radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius "Radius") of a [sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphere "Sphere") that can enclose the given system, _E_ is the total [mass–energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence "Mass–energy equivalence") including any [rest masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_mass "Invariant mass"), _ħ_ is the [reduced Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant#Reduced_Planck_constant "Planck constant"), and _c_ is the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light "Speed of light"). Note that while gravity sometimes plays a significant role in its enforcement (e.g., in a black hole), the expression for the bound does not contain the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant "Gravitational constant")  _G_ , so the bound ought to apply to [quantum field theory in curved spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory_in_curved_spacetime "Quantum field theory in curved spacetime"). 
The [Bekenstein–Hawking boundary entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole_thermodynamics#Black_hole_entropy "Black hole thermodynamics") of three-dimensional [black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole "Black hole") exactly saturates the bound. The [Schwarzschild radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_radius "Schwarzschild radius") is given by  r s = 2 G M c 2 , {\displaystyle r_{\rm {s}}={\frac {2GM}{c^{2}}},} ![{\\displaystyle r_{\\rm {s}}={\\frac {2GM}{c^{2}}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/34628cf467a84c8d3146ef153209a3f64306b358) where _M_ is the mass of the stellar-sized object And so the two-dimensional area of the black hole's event horizon is  A = 4 π r s 2 = 16 π G 2 M 2 / c 4 , {\displaystyle A=4\pi r_{\rm {s}}^{2}={16\pi G^{2}M^{2}}/{c^{4}},} ![{\\displaystyle A=4\\pi r_{\\rm {s}}^{2}={16\\pi G^{2}M^{2}}/{c^{4}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8c9637054942cb11422742d14f18e71d2d5458f8) and using the [Planck length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_length "Planck length") l P 2 = ℏ G / c 3 , {\displaystyle l_{\rm {P}}^{2}=\hbar G/c^{3},} ![{\\displaystyle l_{\\rm {P}}^{2}=\\hbar G/c^{3},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/75668434045603e306235e28101a3a69f77548d4) the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy is  S = k A 4 l P 2 = 4 π k G M 2 ℏ c . {\displaystyle S={\frac {kA}{4\ l_{\rm {P}}^{2}}}={\frac {4\pi kGM^{2}}{\hbar c}}.} ![{\\displaystyle S={\\frac {kA}{4\\ l_{\\rm {P}}^{2}}}={\\frac {4\\pi kGM^{2}}{\\hbar c}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5875f6650897f719c2f250558c31be53a7a93716)
One interpretation of the bound makes use of the [microcanonical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcanonical_ensemble "Microcanonical ensemble") formula for entropy,  S = k log ⁡ Ω , {\displaystyle S=k\log \Omega ,} ![{\\displaystyle S=k\\log \\Omega ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f4a740e5c3bc55d81ccacccd31900ed1a3bdbe17) where  Ω {\displaystyle \Omega } ![{\\displaystyle \\Omega }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/24b0d5ca6f381068d756f6337c08e0af9d1eeb6f) is the number of energy [eigenstates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenstate "Eigenstate") accessible to the system. This is equivalent to saying that the dimension of the [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space") describing the system is[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-:0-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-5) dim ⁡ H = exp ⁡ ( 2 π R E ℏ c ) . {\displaystyle \dim {\mathcal {H}}=\exp \left({\frac {2\pi RE}{\hbar c}}\right).} ![{\\displaystyle \\dim {\\mathcal {H}}=\\exp \\left\({\\frac {2\\pi RE}{\\hbar c}}\\right\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6333f5f22a7ccdc62b948ad9280e45892bb2457f)
The bound is closely associated with [black hole thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole_thermodynamics "Black hole thermodynamics"), the [holographic principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holographic_principle "Holographic principle") and the [covariant entropy bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_entropy_bound "Covariant entropy bound") of quantum gravity, and can be derived from a conjectured strong form of the latter.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-:0-4)
## Origins
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bekenstein_bound&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Origins")]
Bekenstein derived the bound from heuristic arguments involving [black holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_hole "Black hole"). If a system exists that violates the bound, i.e., by having too much entropy, Bekenstein argued that it would be possible to violate the [second law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics "Second law of thermodynamics") by lowering it into a black hole. In 1995, [Ted Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Jacobson "Theodore Jacobson") demonstrated that the [Einstein field equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_field_equations "Einstein field equations") (i.e., [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity")) can be derived by assuming that the Bekenstein bound and the [laws of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_thermodynamics "Laws of thermodynamics") are true.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Jacobson1995-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Smolin2002-7) However, while a number of arguments were devised which show that some form of the bound must exist in order for the laws of thermodynamics and general relativity to be mutually consistent, the precise formulation of the bound was a matter of debate until Horacio Casini's work in 2008.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein2005-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein2008-3)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bousso1999-6-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bousso1999-7-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bousso2000-10)[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein2000-11)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bousso2002-12)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein2003-13)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-BoussoEtAl2003-14)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Bekenstein2004-15)[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-Tipler2005-16)
The following is a heuristic derivation that shows  S ≤ K k R E / ℏ c {\displaystyle S\leq K{kRE}/{\hbar c}} ![{\\displaystyle S\\leq K{kRE}/{\\hbar c}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/030298fc7204fd71d81a8fd07cd096ee20fc144f) for some constant ⁠ K {\displaystyle K} ![{\\displaystyle K}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2b76fce82a62ed5461908f0dc8f037de4e3686b0)⁠. Showing that  K = 2 π {\displaystyle K=2\pi } ![{\\displaystyle K=2\\pi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f3d25c8de31be61fdbeec654f570f059b021795a) requires a more technical analysis. 
Suppose we have a black hole of mass ⁠ M {\displaystyle M} ![{\\displaystyle M}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f82cade9898ced02fdd08712e5f0c0151758a0dd)⁠, then the [Schwarzschild radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_radius "Schwarzschild radius") of the black hole is ⁠ R bh ∼ G M / c 2 {\displaystyle R_{\text{bh}}\sim {GM}/{c^{2}}} ![{\\displaystyle R_{\\text{bh}}\\sim {GM}/{c^{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8723d62b62ad2ef51144225b09fae688f0a53b11)⁠, and the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy of the black hole is ⁠ ∼ k c 3 R bh 2 ℏ G ∼ k G M 2 / ℏ c {\displaystyle \sim {\frac {kc^{3}R_{\text{bh}}^{2}}{\hbar G}}\sim {kGM^{2}}/{\hbar c}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sim {\\frac {kc^{3}R_{\\text{bh}}^{2}}{\\hbar G}}\\sim {kGM^{2}}/{\\hbar c}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d9be293b6aa576b8d035288ef189ad23761fe0bc)⁠. 
Now take a box of energy ⁠ E {\displaystyle E} ![{\\displaystyle E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4232c9de2ee3eec0a9c0a19b15ab92daa6223f9b)⁠, entropy ⁠ S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2)⁠, and side length ⁠ R {\displaystyle R} ![{\\displaystyle R}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b0bfb3769bf24d80e15374dc37b0441e2616e33)⁠. If we throw the box into the black hole, the mass of the black hole goes up to ⁠ M + E / c 2 {\displaystyle M+{E}/{c^{2}}} ![{\\displaystyle M+{E}/{c^{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/611f80f44050ab404e7f8293666bf46c7656528e)⁠, and the entropy goes up by ⁠ k G M E / ℏ c 3 {\displaystyle {kGME}/{\hbar c^{3}}} ![{\\displaystyle {kGME}/{\\hbar c^{3}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c54386d14a69d9d53a39d14c756270afac468b22)⁠. Since entropy does not decrease, ⁠ k G M E / ℏ c 3 ≳ S {\displaystyle {kGME}/{\hbar c^{3}}\gtrsim S} ![{\\displaystyle {kGME}/{\\hbar c^{3}}\\gtrsim S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b6dafb0f0eb1b06ca961acffef02acd4486ff54b)⁠. 
In order for the box to fit inside the black hole, ⁠ R ≲ G M / c 2 {\displaystyle R\lesssim {GM}/{c^{2}}} ![{\\displaystyle R\\lesssim {GM}/{c^{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7006a8cca4d6ae43ffae5b330eec0d81c2059c06)⁠. If the two are comparable, ⁠ R ∼ G M / c 2 {\displaystyle R\sim {GM}/{c^{2}}} ![{\\displaystyle R\\sim {GM}/{c^{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f63083aa396857f63f4e27da69cc6cb4ea1e54dc)⁠, then we have derived the BH bound: ⁠ S ≲ k R E / ℏ c {\displaystyle S\lesssim {kRE}/{\hbar c}} ![{\\displaystyle S\\lesssim {kRE}/{\\hbar c}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bc0c70205fc3ee82bd68fa1f29677ba46a902fa3)⁠. 
## Proof in quantum field theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bekenstein_bound&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Proof in quantum field theory")]
A proof of the Bekenstein bound in the framework of [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory") was given in 2008 by Casini.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-17) One of the crucial insights of the proof was to find a proper interpretation of the quantities appearing on both sides of the bound. 
Naive definitions of entropy and energy density in Quantum Field Theory suffer from [ultraviolet divergences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet_divergence "Ultraviolet divergence"). In the case of the Bekenstein bound, ultraviolet divergences can be avoided by taking differences between quantities computed in an excited state and the same quantities computed in the [vacuum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_vacuum_state "Quantum vacuum state"). For example, given a spatial region ⁠ V {\displaystyle V} ![{\\displaystyle V}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/af0f6064540e84211d0ffe4dac72098adfa52845)⁠, Casini defines the entropy on the left-hand side of the Bekenstein bound as  S V = S ( ρ V ) − S ( ρ V 0 ) = − t r ( ρ V log ⁡ ρ V ) + t r ( ρ V 0 log ⁡ ρ V 0 ) {\displaystyle S_{V}=S(\rho _{V})-S(\rho _{V}^{0})=-\mathrm {tr} (\rho _{V}\log \rho _{V})+\mathrm {tr} (\rho _{V}^{0}\log \rho _{V}^{0})} ![{\\displaystyle S_{V}=S\(\\rho _{V}\)-S\(\\rho _{V}^{0}\)=-\\mathrm {tr} \(\\rho _{V}\\log \\rho _{V}\)+\\mathrm {tr} \(\\rho _{V}^{0}\\log \\rho _{V}^{0}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d9ab8a4b23da806fb5fffa3ee6ed176c574c3f48) where  S ( ρ V ) {\displaystyle S(\rho _{V})} ![{\\displaystyle S\(\\rho _{V}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/52035927380eb49b457262dd2e17d5e51dad30b9) is the [Von Neumann entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_entropy "Von Neumann entropy") of the [reduced density matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_matrix#Systems_and_subsystems "Density matrix") ρ V {\displaystyle \rho _{V}} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho _{V}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e292aadf5e174a93a038bc8198af545af7d938e9) associated with  V {\displaystyle V} ![{\\displaystyle V}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/af0f6064540e84211d0ffe4dac72098adfa52845) in the excited state ⁠ ρ {\displaystyle \rho } ![{\\displaystyle \\rho }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f7d439671d1289b6a816e6af7a304be40608d64)⁠, and  S ( ρ V 0 ) {\displaystyle S(\rho _{V}^{0})} ![{\\displaystyle S\(\\rho _{V}^{0}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fa27637c9014d66b21aa469ebbba5b5d92f41fb8) is the corresponding Von Neumann entropy for the vacuum state ⁠ ρ 0 {\displaystyle \rho ^{0}} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho ^{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/88c6f410d8a70adc3c47e685f75b00afa7981202)⁠. 
On the right-hand side of the Bekenstein bound, a difficult point is to give a rigorous interpretation of the quantity ⁠ 2 π R E {\displaystyle 2\pi RE} ![{\\displaystyle 2\\pi RE}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0998e1fafca0ec2f3376209cc2446140b1b0e1a4)⁠, where  R {\displaystyle R} ![{\\displaystyle R}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b0bfb3769bf24d80e15374dc37b0441e2616e33) is a characteristic length scale of the system and  E {\displaystyle E} ![{\\displaystyle E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4232c9de2ee3eec0a9c0a19b15ab92daa6223f9b) is a characteristic energy. This product has the same units as the generator of a [Lorentz boost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation "Lorentz transformation"), and the natural analog of a boost in this situation is the [modular Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Modular_Hamiltonian&action=edit&redlink=1 "Modular Hamiltonian \(page does not exist\)") of the vacuum state ⁠ K = − log ⁡ ρ V 0 {\displaystyle K=-\log \rho _{V}^{0}} ![{\\displaystyle K=-\\log \\rho _{V}^{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/350ff14cc57cbf46655df13790ccfd389f70a245)⁠. Casini defines the right-hand side of the Bekenstein bound as the difference between the expectation value of the modular Hamiltonian in the excited state and the vacuum state,  K V = t r ( K ρ V ) − t r ( K ρ V 0 ) . {\displaystyle K_{V}=\mathrm {tr} (K\rho _{V})-\mathrm {tr} (K\rho _{V}^{0}).} ![{\\displaystyle K_{V}=\\mathrm {tr} \(K\\rho _{V}\)-\\mathrm {tr} \(K\\rho _{V}^{0}\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1ed04d5215dfcf491840f6da3de5d87ba6ec5f00)
With these definitions, the bound reads  S V ≤ K V , {\displaystyle S_{V}\leq K_{V},} ![{\\displaystyle S_{V}\\leq K_{V},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cedd0da9101556963687144295c00bd0de0132f8) which can be rearranged to give  t r ( ρ V log ⁡ ρ V ) − t r ( ρ V log ⁡ ρ V 0 ) ≥ 0. {\displaystyle \mathrm {tr} (\rho _{V}\log \rho _{V})-\mathrm {tr} (\rho _{V}\log \rho _{V}^{0})\geq 0.} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {tr} \(\\rho _{V}\\log \\rho _{V}\)-\\mathrm {tr} \(\\rho _{V}\\log \\rho _{V}^{0}\)\\geq 0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b9a05347fde72ec44b21a084a5e5d3b7b490add)
This is simply the statement of positivity of [quantum relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_relative_entropy "Quantum relative entropy"), which proves the Bekenstein bound. 
However, the modular Hamiltonian can only be interpreted as a weighted form of energy for [conformal field theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_field_theory "Conformal field theory"), and when  V {\displaystyle V} ![{\\displaystyle V}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/af0f6064540e84211d0ffe4dac72098adfa52845) is a sphere. 
This construction allows us to make sense of the [Casimir effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimir_effect "Casimir effect")[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound#cite_note-:0-4) where the localized energy density is _lower_ than that of the vacuum, i.e. a _negative_ localized energy. The localized entropy of the vacuum is nonzero, and so, the Casimir effect is possible for states with a lower localized entropy than that of the vacuum. [Hawking radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawking_radiation "Hawking radiation") can be explained by dumping localized negative energy into a black hole.
