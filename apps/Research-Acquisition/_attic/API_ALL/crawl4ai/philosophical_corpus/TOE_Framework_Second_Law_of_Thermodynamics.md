The **second law of thermodynamics** is a [physical law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law "Physical law") based on [universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_\(metaphysics\) "Universal \(metaphysics\)") [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical "Empirical") [observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation "Observation") concerning [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat "Heat") and [energy interconversions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_transformation "Energy transformation"). A simple statement of the law is that heat always flows spontaneously from hotter to colder regions of matter (or 'downhill' in terms of the temperature gradient). Another statement is: "Not all heat can be converted into [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(thermodynamics\) "Work \(thermodynamics\)") in a [cyclic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_process "Cyclic process")."[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Rao-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Young&Freedman11th-3) These are informal definitions, however; more formal definitions appear below. 
The second law of thermodynamics establishes the concept of [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy") as a physical property of a [thermodynamic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_system "Thermodynamic system"). It predicts whether processes are forbidden despite obeying the requirement of [conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy "Conservation of energy") as expressed in the [first law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics") and provides necessary criteria for [spontaneous processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_process "Spontaneous process"). For example, the first law allows the process of a cup falling off a table and breaking on the floor, as well as allowing the reverse process of the cup fragments coming back together and 'jumping' back onto the table, while the second law allows the former and denies the latter. The second law may be formulated by the observation that the entropy of [isolated systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_system "Isolated system") left to spontaneous evolution cannot decrease, as they always tend toward a state of [thermodynamic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_equilibrium "Thermodynamic equilibrium") where the entropy is highest at the given internal energy.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-4) An increase in the combined entropy of system and surroundings accounts for the [irreversibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreversibility "Irreversibility") of natural processes, often referred to in the concept of the [arrow of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_of_time "Arrow of time").[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-6)
Historically, the second law was an [empirical finding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_evidence "Empirical evidence") that was accepted as an [axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom "Axiom") of [thermodynamic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics"). [Statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics") provides a microscopic explanation of the law in terms of [probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") of the states of large assemblies of [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom "Atom") or [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule"). The second law has been expressed in many ways. Its first formulation, which preceded the proper definition of entropy and was based on [caloric theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory "Caloric theory"), is [Carnot's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot%27s_theorem_\(thermodynamics\) "Carnot's theorem \(thermodynamics\)"), formulated by the French scientist [Sadi Carnot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_L%C3%A9onard_Sadi_Carnot "Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot"), who in 1824 showed that the efficiency of conversion of heat to work in a heat engine has an upper limit.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-8) The first rigorous definition of the second law based on the concept of entropy came from German scientist [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius "Rudolf Clausius") in the 1850s and included his statement that heat can never pass from a colder to a warmer body without some other change, connected therewith, occurring at the same time. 
The second law of thermodynamics allows the definition of the concept of [thermodynamic temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_temperature "Thermodynamic temperature"), but this has been formally delegated to the [zeroth law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeroth_law_of_thermodynamics "Zeroth law of thermodynamics"). 
## Introduction
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Introduction")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/96/Heat_flow_hot_to_cold.svg/250px-Heat_flow_hot_to_cold.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Heat_flow_hot_to_cold.svg)Heat flowing from hot water to cold water
The [first law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics") provides the definition of the [internal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_energy "Internal energy") of a [thermodynamic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_system "Thermodynamic system"), and expresses its change for a [closed system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_system "Closed system") in terms of [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(thermodynamics\) "Work \(thermodynamics\)") and [heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat "Heat").[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-9) It can be linked to the law of [conservation of energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy "Conservation of energy").[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-10) Conceptually, the first law describes the fundamental principle that systems do not consume or 'use up' energy, that energy is neither created nor destroyed, but is simply converted from one form to another. 
The second law is concerned with the direction of natural processes.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-11) It asserts that a natural process runs only in one sense, and is not reversible. That is, the state of a natural system itself can be reversed, but not without increasing the entropy of the system's surroundings, that is, both the state of the system plus the state of its surroundings cannot be together, fully reversed, without implying the destruction of entropy. 
For example, when a path for conduction or [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation "Radiation") is made available, heat always flows spontaneously from a hotter to a colder body. Such [phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon "Phenomenon") are accounted for in terms of [entropy change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy").[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-12)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-13) A heat pump can reverse this heat flow, but the reversal process and the original process, both cause entropy production, thereby increasing the entropy of the system's surroundings. If an isolated system containing distinct subsystems is held initially in internal thermodynamic equilibrium by internal partitioning by impermeable walls between the subsystems, and then some operation makes the walls more permeable, then the system spontaneously evolves to reach a final new internal [thermodynamic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_equilibrium "Thermodynamic equilibrium"), and its total entropy,  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2), increases. 
In a [reversible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversible_process_\(thermodynamics\) "Reversible process \(thermodynamics\)") or [quasi-static](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasistatic_process "Quasistatic process"), idealized process of transfer of energy as heat to a [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_system "Closed system") thermodynamic system of interest, (which allows the entry or exit of energy – but not transfer of matter), from an auxiliary thermodynamic system, an infinitesimal increment ( d S {\displaystyle \mathrm {d} S} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {d} S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72ba425d7d7a0f229457dea3c0be4a47ea303cc3)) in the entropy of the system of interest is defined to result from an infinitesimal [transfer of heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_of_heat "Transfer of heat") ( δ Q {\displaystyle \delta Q} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta Q}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bf715eece146c816847a8c5d56eae97798453d64)) to the system of interest, divided by the common [thermodynamic temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_temperature "Thermodynamic temperature") ( T ) {\displaystyle (T)} ![{\\displaystyle \(T\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c5f9a82bdcb95d511ed6965b0534a3eda98ceb62) of the system of interest and the auxiliary thermodynamic system:[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-14)      d S = δ Q T (closed system; idealized, reversible process) . {\displaystyle \mathrm {d} S={\frac {\delta Q}{T}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,{\text{(closed system; idealized, reversible process)}}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {d} S={\\frac {\\delta Q}{T}}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,{\\text{\(closed system; idealized, reversible process\)}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4422a72c7a3c220fba255df2cdc2466a34b1d62e)
Different notations are used for an [infinitesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimal "Infinitesimal") amount of heat  ( δ ) {\displaystyle (\delta )} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\delta \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ea20ade7c72d2d4722a03cbbfc3f54a03a7c2b53) and infinitesimal change of entropy  ( d ) {\displaystyle (\mathrm {d} )} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\mathrm {d} \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b16fdc1501515bc9d24841a920087022a08c9d9) because entropy is a [function of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_of_state "Function of state"), while heat, like work, is not. 
For an actually possible infinitesimal process without exchange of mass with the surroundings, the second law requires that the increment in system entropy fulfills the [inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clausius_Theorem "Clausius Theorem")[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-MortimerBook-15)[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FermiBook-16)      d S > δ Q T surr (closed system; actually possible, irreversible process). {\displaystyle \mathrm {d} S>{\frac {\delta Q}{T_{\text{surr}}}}\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,{\text{(closed system; actually possible, irreversible process).}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {d} S>{\\frac {\\delta Q}{T_{\\text{surr}}}}\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,\\,{\\text{\(closed system; actually possible, irreversible process\).}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ae63708f5ca5b7fdd07bcafe5d5d320053eb0b40)
This is because a general process for this case (no mass exchange between the system and its surroundings) may include work being done on the system by its surroundings, which can have frictional or viscous effects inside the system, because a chemical reaction may be in progress, or because heat transfer actually occurs only irreversibly, driven by a finite difference between the system temperature (_T_) and the temperature of the surroundings (_T_ surr).[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-:0-17)[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Munster_45-18)

which is the basis of the accurate determination of the absolute entropy of pure substances from measured heat capacity curves and entropy changes at phase transitions, i.e. by calorimetry.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Oxtoby8th-19)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-MortimerBook-15)
The [zeroth law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeroth_law_of_thermodynamics "Zeroth law of thermodynamics") in its usual short statement allows recognition that two bodies in a relation of thermal equilibrium have the same temperature, especially that a test body has the same temperature as a reference thermometric body.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-dugdale-20) For a body in thermal equilibrium with another, there are indefinitely many empirical temperature scales, in general respectively depending on the properties of a particular reference thermometric body. The second law allows[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] a distinguished temperature scale, which defines an absolute, [thermodynamic temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_temperature "Thermodynamic temperature"), independent of the properties of any particular reference thermometric body.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-22)
## Various statements of the law
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Various statements of the law")]
The second law of thermodynamics may be expressed in many specific ways,[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-MIT-23) the most prominent classical statements[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTELiebYngvason1999-24) being the statement by [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius "Rudolf Clausius") (1854), the statement by [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin "William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin") (1851), and the statement in [axiomatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic "Axiomatic") thermodynamics by [Constantin Carathéodory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantin_Carath%C3%A9odory "Constantin Carathéodory") (1909). These statements cast the law in general physical terms citing the impossibility of certain processes. The Clausius and the Kelvin statements have been shown to be equivalent.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTERao2004213-25)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Carnot's principle")]
The historical origin[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-26) of the second law of thermodynamics was in [Sadi Carnot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_L%C3%A9onard_Sadi_Carnot "Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot")'s theoretical analysis of the flow of heat in steam engines (1824). The centerpiece of that analysis, now known as a [Carnot engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot_engine "Carnot engine"), is an ideal [heat engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine "Heat engine") fictively operated in the limiting mode of extreme slowness known as quasi-static, so that the heat and work transfers are between subsystems that are always in their own internal states of [thermodynamic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_equilibrium "Thermodynamic equilibrium"). It represents the theoretical maximum efficiency of a heat engine operating between any two given thermal or heat reservoirs at different temperatures. Carnot's principle was recognized by Carnot at a time when the [caloric theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory "Caloric theory") represented the dominant understanding of the nature of heat, before the recognition of the [first law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics"), and before the mathematical expression of the concept of entropy. Interpreted in the light of the first law, Carnot's analysis is physically equivalent to the second law of thermodynamics, and remains valid today. Some samples from his book are: 
> ... wherever there exists a difference of temperature, motive power can be produced.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-27)
> The production of motive power is then due in [steam engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steam_engines "Steam engines") not to an actual consumption of caloric, but _to its transportation from a warm body to a cold body ..._[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-28)
> The motive power of heat is independent of the agents employed to realize it; its quantity is fixed solely by the temperatures of the bodies between which is effected, finally, the transfer of caloric.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-29)
In modern terms, Carnot's principle may be stated more precisely: 
> The efficiency of a quasi-static or reversible [Carnot cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot_cycle "Carnot cycle") depends only on the temperatures of the two heat reservoirs, and is the same, whatever the working substance. A Carnot engine operated in this way is the most efficient possible heat engine using those two temperatures.[_[attribution needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Attribution_needed "Wikipedia:Attribution needed")_][[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-30)[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-31)[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-32)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-33)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-34)[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-35)
### Clausius statement
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Clausius statement")]
In 1850, the German scientist [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius "Rudolf Clausius") laid the foundation for the second law of thermodynamics by examining the relation between heat transfer and work.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClausius1850-36) His formulation of the second law, which was published in German in 1854, is known as the _Clausius statement_ : 
> Heat can never pass from a colder to a warmer body without some other change, connected therewith, occurring at the same time.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClausius185486-37)
The statement by Clausius uses the concept of 'passage of heat'. As is usual in thermodynamic discussions, this means 'net transfer of energy as heat', and does not refer to contributory transfers one way and the other. 
Heat cannot spontaneously flow from cold regions to hot regions without external work being performed on the system, which is evident from ordinary experience of [refrigeration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigeration "Refrigeration"), for example. In a refrigerator, heat is transferred from cold to hot, but only when forced by an external agent, the refrigeration system. 
Understanding the Clausius statement requires prior definition of the concepts of 'hot versus cold' (temperature) and 'heat' (heat interaction). Within the Gyftopoulos-Beretta formulation of the general principles of thermodynamics, where temperature and heat are defined after the definitions of energy and entropy, the Clausius statement emerges as a rigorous consequence (theorem) of the first law and the Hatsopoulos-Keenan statement of the second law (see below).[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-38)[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-39)
### Kelvin statements
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Kelvin statements")]
[Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin "William Thomson, 1st Baron Kelvin") expressed the second law in several wordings. 
> It is impossible for a self-acting machine, unaided by any external agency, to convey heat from one body to another at a higher temperature.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
> It is impossible, by means of inanimate material agency, to derive mechanical effect from any portion of matter by cooling it below the temperature of the coldest of the surrounding objects.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTEThomson1851-40)
### Equivalence of the Clausius and the Kelvin statements
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Equivalence of the Clausius and the Kelvin statements")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/Deriving_Kelvin_Statement_from_Clausius_Statement.svg/250px-Deriving_Kelvin_Statement_from_Clausius_Statement.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Deriving_Kelvin_Statement_from_Clausius_Statement.svg)Derive Kelvin Statement from Clausius Statement
Suppose there is an engine violating the Kelvin statement: i.e., one that drains heat and converts it completely into work (the drained heat is fully converted to work) in a cyclic fashion without any other result. Now pair it with a reversed [Carnot engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot_engine "Carnot engine") as shown by the right figure. The [efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine#Efficiency "Heat engine") of a normal heat engine is _η_ and so the efficiency of the reversed heat engine is 1/_η_. The net and sole effect of the combined pair of engines is to transfer heat  Δ Q = Q ( 1 η − 1 ) {\textstyle \Delta Q=Q\left({\frac {1}{\eta }}-1\right)} ![{\\textstyle \\Delta Q=Q\\left\({\\frac {1}{\\eta }}-1\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/85a360321a75c2b741008172ac76a3d4836ddd7b) from the cooler reservoir to the hotter one, which violates the Clausius statement. This is a consequence of the [first law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics"), as for the total system's energy to remain the same;  Input + Output = 0 ⟹ ( Q + Q c ) − Q η = 0 {\textstyle {\text{Input}}+{\text{Output}}=0\implies (Q+Q_{c})-{\frac {Q}{\eta }}=0} ![{\\textstyle {\\text{Input}}+{\\text{Output}}=0\\implies \(Q+Q_{c}\)-{\\frac {Q}{\\eta }}=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/08875c9431acfc6eb4c7580bdacf002f34374de5), so therefore  Q c = Q ( 1 η − 1 ) {\textstyle Q_{c}=Q\left({\frac {1}{\eta }}-1\right)} ![{\\textstyle Q_{c}=Q\\left\({\\frac {1}{\\eta }}-1\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/03702804374f47cb9ddb39fffcd69daa65a64366), where (1) the sign convention of heat is used in which heat entering into (leaving from) an engine is positive (negative) and (2)  Q η {\displaystyle {\frac {Q}{\eta }}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {Q}{\\eta }}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fdda20674365c00854ab1eb7607f880ca2969af) is obtained by [the definition of efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_engine#Efficiency "Heat engine") of the engine when the engine operation is not reversed. Thus a violation of the Kelvin statement implies a violation of the Clausius statement, i.e. the Clausius statement implies the Kelvin statement. We can prove in a similar manner that the Kelvin statement implies the Clausius statement, and hence the two are equivalent. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Planck's proposition")]
Planck offered the following proposition as derived directly from experience. This is sometimes regarded as his statement of the second law, but he regarded it as a starting point for the derivation of the second law. 
> It is impossible to construct an engine which will work in a complete cycle, and produce no effect except the production of work and cooling of a heat reservoir.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-41)[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-42)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Relation between Kelvin's statement and Planck's proposition")]
It is almost customary in textbooks to speak of the "Kelvin–Planck statement" of the law, as for example in the text by [ter Haar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirk_ter_Haar "Dirk ter Haar") and [Wergeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Wergeland "Harald Wergeland").[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-43) This version, also known as the **heat engine statement** , of the second law states that 
> It is impossible to devise a [cyclically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_cycle "Thermodynamic cycle") operating device, the sole effect of which is to absorb energy in the form of heat from a single [thermal reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_reservoir "Heat reservoir") and to deliver an equivalent amount of [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_\(physics\) "Work \(physics\)").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Rao-2)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Planck's statement")]
[Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck "Max Planck") stated the second law as follows. 
> Every process occurring in nature proceeds in the sense in which the sum of the entropies of all bodies taking part in the process is increased. In the limit, i.e. for reversible processes, the sum of the entropies remains unchanged.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Planck_100-44)[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Planck_463-45)[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Roberts_&_Miller_382-46)
Rather like Planck's statement is that of [George Uhlenbeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Uhlenbeck "George Uhlenbeck") and G. W. Ford for _irreversible phenomena_. 
> ... in an irreversible or spontaneous change from one equilibrium state to another (as for example the equalization of temperature of two bodies A and B, when brought in contact) the entropy always increases.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-47)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Principle of Carathéodory")]
[Constantin Carathéodory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantin_Carath%C3%A9odory "Constantin Carathéodory") formulated thermodynamics on a purely mathematical axiomatic foundation. His statement of the second law is known as the Principle of Carathéodory, which may be formulated as follows:[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-48)
> In every neighborhood of any state S of an adiabatically enclosed system there are states inaccessible from S.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-49)
With this formulation, he described the concept of [adiabatic accessibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_accessibility "Adiabatic accessibility") for the first time and provided the foundation for a new subfield of classical thermodynamics, often called [geometrical thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruppeiner_geometry "Ruppeiner geometry"). It follows from Carathéodory's principle that quantity of energy quasi-statically transferred as heat is a holonomic [process function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_function "Process function"), in other words,  δ Q = T d S {\displaystyle \delta Q=TdS} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta Q=TdS}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e8142742fd8644554e5a89912f03dcac94097b70).[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Sychev1991-50)
Though it is almost customary in textbooks to say that Carathéodory's principle expresses the second law and to treat it as equivalent to the Clausius or to the Kelvin-Planck statements, such is not the case. To get all the content of the second law, Carathéodory's principle needs to be supplemented by Planck's principle, that isochoric work always increases the internal energy of a closed system that was initially in its own internal thermodynamic equilibrium.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Munster_45-18)[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTELiebYngvason199949-51)[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Planck_1926-52)[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-53)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-ZanchiniBeretta2014-54)[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_]
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Planck's principle")]
In 1926, Max Planck wrote an important paper on the basics of thermodynamics.[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Planck_1926-52)[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-55) He indicated the principle 
> The internal energy of a closed system is increased by an adiabatic process, throughout the duration of which, the volume of the system remains constant.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Munster_45-18)[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTELiebYngvason199949-51)
This formulation does not mention heat and does not mention temperature, nor even entropy, and does not necessarily implicitly rely on those concepts, but it implies the content of the second law. A closely related statement is that "Frictional pressure never does positive work."[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-56) Planck wrote: "The production of heat by friction is irreversible."[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-57)[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-58)
Not mentioning entropy, this principle of Planck is stated in physical terms. It is very closely related to the Kelvin statement given just above.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-59) It is relevant that for a system at constant volume and [mole numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_\(unit\) "Mole \(unit\)"), the entropy is a monotonic function of the internal energy. Nevertheless, this principle of Planck is not actually Planck's preferred statement of the second law, which is quoted above, in a previous sub-section of the present section of this present article, and relies on the concept of entropy. 
A statement that in a sense is complementary to Planck's principle is made by Claus Borgnakke and Richard E. Sonntag. They do not offer it as a full statement of the second law: 
> ... there is only one way in which the entropy of a [closed] system can be decreased, and that is to transfer heat from the system.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-60)
Differing from Planck's just foregoing principle, this one is explicitly in terms of entropy change. Removal of matter from a system can also decrease its entropy. 
### Hatsopoulos-Keenan statement
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Hatsopoulos-Keenan statement")]
In 1965, [G. N. Hatsopoulos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_N._Hatsopoulos "George N. Hatsopoulos") and [Joseph Keenan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Henry_Keenan "Joseph Henry Keenan") published their textbook _Principles of General Thermodynamics_ , which restates the second law of thermodynamics in terms of the existence of stable equilibrium states.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-61) Their formulation of the second law of thermodynamics states that: 
> When an isolated system performs a process after the removal of a series of internal constraints, it will reach a unique state of stable equilibrium: this state of equilibrium is independent of the order in which the constraints are removed.
This shows that the second law of thermodynamics can be stated in terms of the existence of stable equilibrium states. The Hatsopoulos-Keenan statement of the Second Law [entails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entailment "Entailment") the Clausius, Kelvin-Planck, and Carathéodory statements of the Second Law,[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-62) and has provided a basis to extend the traditional definition of [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy") to the non-equilibrium domain. Hatsopoulus and Keenan maintained that it is unlikely to identify a satisfactory definition of heat without a prior statement of the second law.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-63)[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-64)[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-65)
The Hatsopoulos–Keenan statement of the second law has been developed into a rigorous formulation of the general axiomatic foundations of equilibrium and nonequilibrium, classical, statistical, and quantum thermodynamics by [G. N. Hatsopoulos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_N._Hatsopoulos "George N. Hatsopoulos"), [E. P. Gyftopoulos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elias_Gyftopoulos "Elias Gyftopoulos"), E. Zanchini, and G. P. Beretta.[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-66)[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-67)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-ZanchiniBeretta2014-54)[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-68)[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-69)
### Relating the second law to the definition of temperature
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Relating the second law to the definition of temperature")]
The second law has been shown to be equivalent to the [internal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_energy "Internal energy") _U_ defined as a [convex function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_function "Convex function") of the other extensive properties of the system.[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-70) That is, when a system is described by stating its [internal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_energy "Internal energy") _U_ , an extensive variable, as a function of its [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy") _S_ , volume _V_ , and mol number _N_ , i.e. _U_ = _U_ (_S_ , _V_ , _N_), then the temperature is equal to the [partial derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivative "Partial derivative") of the internal energy with respect to the entropy[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Callen_146%E2%80%93148-71) (essentially equivalent to the first _TdS_ equation for _V_ and _N_ held constant): 
T = ( ∂ U ∂ S ) V , N {\displaystyle T=\left({\frac {\partial U}{\partial S}}\right)_{V,N}} ![{\\displaystyle T=\\left\({\\frac {\\partial U}{\\partial S}}\\right\)_{V,N}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/818d012b1eb2878592bb811562c29772d322ca9f)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Second law statements, such as the Clausius inequality, involving radiative fluxes")]
The Clausius inequality, as well as some other statements of the second law, must be re-stated to have general applicability for all forms of heat transfer, i.e. scenarios involving radiative fluxes. For example, the integrand (đ _Q_ /_T_) of the Clausius expression applies to heat conduction and convection, and the case of ideal infinitesimal blackbody radiation (BR) transfer, but does not apply to most radiative transfer scenarios and in some cases has no physical meaning whatsoever. Consequently, the Clausius inequality was re-stated[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-72) so that it is applicable to cycles with processes involving any form of heat transfer. The entropy transfer with radiative fluxes ( δ S NetRad \delta S_{\text{NetRad}} ![\\delta S_{\\text{NetRad}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3b0fc3cd3f47c2dbfa232ae4655bfb54617fcce1)) is taken separately from that due to heat transfer by conduction and convection ( δ Q C C \delta Q_{CC} ![\\delta Q_{CC}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b5cdb90175f1dd1778f623a3fe2ceb8a4b6c13a8)), where the temperature is evaluated at the system boundary where the heat transfer occurs. The modified Clausius inequality, for all heat transfer scenarios, can then be expressed as 
∫ cycle ( δ Q C C T b + δ S NetRad ) ≤ 0 {\displaystyle \int _{\text{cycle}}\left({\frac {\delta Q_{CC}}{T_{b}}}+\delta S_{\text{NetRad}}\right)\leq 0} ![{\\displaystyle \\int _{\\text{cycle}}\\left\({\\frac {\\delta Q_{CC}}{T_{b}}}+\\delta S_{\\text{NetRad}}\\right\)\\leq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/de0cad3dff2686472b588c698200a5c822575488)
In a nutshell, the Clausius inequality is saying that when a cycle is completed, the change in the state property S will be zero, so the entropy that was produced during the cycle must have transferred out of the system by heat transfer. The  δ \delta ![\\delta ](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3086070a07409fa7e760fea6e7c932b2a590b1ce) (or đ) indicates a path dependent integration. 
Due to the inherent emission of radiation from all matter, most entropy flux calculations involve incident, reflected and emitted radiative fluxes. The energy and entropy of unpolarized blackbody thermal radiation, is calculated using the spectral energy and entropy radiance expressions derived by Max Planck[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-73) using equilibrium statistical mechanics:

In deriving this blackbody spectral entropy radiance, with the goal of deriving the blackbody energy formula, Planck postulated that the energy of a photon was quantized (partly to simplify the mathematics), thereby starting quantum theory. 
A non-equilibrium statistical mechanics approach has also been used to obtain the same result as Planck, indicating it has wider significance and represents a non-equilibrium entropy.[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-74) A plot of _K v_ versus frequency (v) for various values of temperature (T) gives a family of blackbody radiation energy spectra, and likewise for the entropy spectra. For non-blackbody radiation (NBR) emission fluxes, the spectral entropy radiance _L v_ is found by substituting _K v_ spectral energy radiance data into the _L v_ expression (noting that emitted and reflected entropy fluxes are, in general, not independent). For the emission of NBR, including graybody radiation (GR), the resultant emitted entropy flux, or radiance L, has a higher ratio of entropy-to-energy (_L_ /_K_), than that of BR. That is, the entropy flux of NBR emission is farther removed from the conduction and convection _q_ /_T_ result, than that for BR emission.[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-75) This observation is consistent with Max Planck's blackbody radiation energy and entropy formulas and is consistent with the fact that blackbody radiation emission represents the maximum emission of entropy for all materials with the same temperature, as well as the maximum entropy emission for all radiation with the same energy radiance. 
### Generalized conceptual statement of the second law principle
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Generalized conceptual statement of the second law principle")]
Second law analysis is valuable in scientific and engineering analysis in that it provides a number of benefits over energy analysis alone, including the basis for determining energy quality (exergy content[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-76)[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-77)[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-78)), understanding fundamental physical phenomena, and improving performance evaluation and optimization. As a result, a conceptual statement of the principle is very useful in engineering analysis. Thermodynamic systems can be categorized by the four combinations of either entropy (S) up or down, and uniformity (Y) – between system and its environment – up or down. This 'special' category of processes, category IV, is characterized by movement in the direction of low disorder and low uniformity, counteracting the second law tendency towards uniformity and disorder.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Wright_12%E2%80%9318-79)
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e8/Exergysun1.png/250px-Exergysun1.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Exergysun1.png)Four categories of processes given entropy up or down and uniformity up or down
The second law can be conceptually stated[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Wright_12%E2%80%9318-79) as follows: Matter and energy have the tendency to reach a state of uniformity or internal and external equilibrium, a state of maximum disorder (entropy). Real non-equilibrium processes always produce entropy, causing increased disorder in the universe, while idealized reversible processes produce no entropy and no process is known to exist that destroys entropy. The tendency of a system to approach uniformity may be counteracted, and the system may become more ordered or complex, by the combination of two things, a work or exergy source and some form of instruction or intelligence. Where 'exergy' is the thermal, mechanical, electric or chemical work potential of an energy source or flow, and 'instruction or intelligence', although subjective, is in the context of the set of category IV processes. 
Consider a category IV example of robotic manufacturing and assembly of vehicles in a factory. The robotic machinery requires electrical work input and instructions, but when completed, the manufactured products have less uniformity with their surroundings, or more complexity (higher order) relative to the raw materials they were made from. Thus, system entropy or disorder decreases while the tendency towards uniformity between the system and its environment is counteracted. In this example, the instructions, as well as the source of work may be internal or external to the system, and they may or may not cross the system boundary. To illustrate, the instructions may be pre-coded and the electrical work may be stored in an energy storage system on-site. Alternatively, the control of the machinery may be by remote operation over a communications network, while the electric work is supplied to the factory from the local electric grid. In addition, humans may directly play, in whole or in part, the role that the robotic machinery plays in manufacturing. In this case, instructions may be involved, but intelligence is either directly responsible, or indirectly responsible, for the direction or application of work in such a way as to counteract the tendency towards disorder and uniformity. 
There are also situations where the entropy spontaneously decreases by means of energy and entropy transfer. When thermodynamic constraints are not present, spontaneously energy or mass, as well as accompanying entropy, may be transferred out of a system in a progress to reach external equilibrium or uniformity in intensive properties of the system with its surroundings. This occurs spontaneously because the energy or mass transferred from the system to its surroundings results in a higher entropy in the surroundings, that is, it results in higher overall entropy of the system plus its surroundings. Note that this transfer of entropy requires dis-equilibrium in properties, such as a temperature difference. One example of this is the cooling crystallization of water that can occur when the system's surroundings are below freezing temperatures. Unconstrained heat transfer can spontaneously occur, leading to water molecules freezing into a crystallized structure of reduced disorder (sticking together in a certain order due to molecular attraction). The entropy of the system decreases, but the system approaches uniformity with its surroundings (category III). 
On the other hand, consider the refrigeration of water in a warm environment. Due to refrigeration, as heat is extracted from the water, the temperature and entropy of the water decreases, as the system moves further away from uniformity with its warm surroundings or environment (category IV). The main point, take-away, is that refrigeration not only requires a source of work, it requires designed equipment, as well as pre-coded or direct operational intelligence or instructions to achieve the desired refrigeration effect. 
## Corollaries
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Corollaries")]
### Perpetual motion of the second kind
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Perpetual motion of the second kind")]
Main article: [Perpetual motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perpetual_motion "Perpetual motion")
Before the establishment of the second law, many people who were interested in inventing a perpetual motion machine had tried to circumvent the restrictions of [first law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics") by extracting the massive internal energy of the environment as the power of the machine. Such a machine is called a "perpetual motion machine of the second kind". The second law declared the impossibility of such machines. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Carnot's theorem")]
[Carnot's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot%27s_theorem_\(thermodynamics\) "Carnot's theorem \(thermodynamics\)") (1824) is a principle that limits the maximum efficiency for any possible engine. The efficiency solely depends on the temperature difference between the hot and cold thermal reservoirs. Carnot's theorem states: 
  * All irreversible heat engines between two heat reservoirs are less efficient than a [Carnot engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot_engine "Carnot engine") operating between the same reservoirs.
  * All reversible heat engines between two heat reservoirs are equally efficient with a Carnot engine operating between the same reservoirs.


In his ideal model, the heat of caloric converted into work could be reinstated by reversing the motion of the cycle, a concept subsequently known as [thermodynamic reversibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_reversibility "Thermodynamic reversibility"). Carnot, however, further postulated that some caloric is lost, not being converted to mechanical work. Hence, no real heat engine could realize the [Carnot cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot_cycle "Carnot cycle")'s reversibility and was condemned to be less efficient. 
Though formulated in terms of caloric (see the obsolete [caloric theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory "Caloric theory")), rather than [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy"), this was an early insight into the second law. 
### Clausius inequality
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Clausius inequality")]
The [Clausius theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clausius_theorem "Clausius theorem") (1854) states that in a cyclic process 
∮ δ Q T surr ≤ 0. {\displaystyle \oint {\frac {\delta Q}{T_{\text{surr}}}}\leq 0.} ![{\\displaystyle \\oint {\\frac {\\delta Q}{T_{\\text{surr}}}}\\leq 0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/17daae51489d5c2e13b9e241d27f51c811b2f6ca)
The equality holds in the reversible case[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-80) and the strict inequality holds in the irreversible case, with _T_ surr as the temperature of the heat bath (surroundings) here. The reversible case is used to introduce the state function [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy "Entropy"). This is because in cyclic processes the variation of a state function is zero from state functionality.

### Entropy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Entropy")]
Main article: [Entropy (classical thermodynamics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_\(classical_thermodynamics\) "Entropy \(classical thermodynamics\)")
According to the [Clausius equality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clausius_theorem "Clausius theorem"), for a _reversible process_
∮ δ Q T = 0 {\displaystyle \oint {\frac {\delta Q}{T}}=0} ![{\\displaystyle \\oint {\\frac {\\delta Q}{T}}=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c592a64d03a3decd4011e6c1454e62a40a6c9f71)
That means the line integral  ∫ L δ Q T {\displaystyle \int _{L}{\frac {\delta Q}{T}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\int _{L}{\\frac {\\delta Q}{T}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cb2dd542ebc69408366001d232890632960f7197) is path independent for reversible processes. 
So we can define a state function _S_ called entropy, which for a reversible process or for pure heat transfer satisfies 
d S = δ Q T {\displaystyle dS={\frac {\delta Q}{T}}} ![{\\displaystyle dS={\\frac {\\delta Q}{T}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4a490ba698a8c5716f119d0932af9a11ed483b13)
With this we can only obtain the difference of entropy by integrating the above formula. To obtain the absolute value, we need the [third law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_law_of_thermodynamics "Third law of thermodynamics"), which states that _S_ = 0 at [absolute zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_zero "Absolute zero") for perfect crystals. 
For any irreversible process, since entropy is a state function, we can always connect the initial and terminal states with an imaginary reversible process and integrating on that path to calculate the difference in entropy. 
Now reverse the reversible process and combine it with the said irreversible process. Applying the [Clausius inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clausius_inequality "Clausius inequality") on this loop, with _T_ surr as the temperature of the surroundings, 
− Δ S + ∫ δ Q T surr = ∮ δ Q T surr ≤ 0 {\displaystyle -\Delta S+\int {\frac {\delta Q}{T_{\text{surr}}}}=\oint {\frac {\delta Q}{T_{\text{surr}}}}\leq 0} ![{\\displaystyle -\\Delta S+\\int {\\frac {\\delta Q}{T_{\\text{surr}}}}=\\oint {\\frac {\\delta Q}{T_{\\text{surr}}}}\\leq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/556bdefc4ff8c38dc8c9db40e45d7b03a9c53e38)
Thus, 
Δ S ≥ ∫ δ Q T surr {\displaystyle \Delta S\geq \int {\frac {\delta Q}{T_{\text{surr}}}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta S\\geq \\int {\\frac {\\delta Q}{T_{\\text{surr}}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d80a8a4cac34747ec74534ab04869a96374fcd16)
where the equality holds if the transformation is reversible. If the process is an [adiabatic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_process "Adiabatic process"), then  δ Q = 0 {\displaystyle \delta Q=0} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta Q=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/90687641a4ec52e9db4cf73695d57f8b49899f85), so  Δ S ≥ 0 {\displaystyle \Delta S\geq 0} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta S\\geq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fa331aee4875f49fa0f9bc35b807212533254270). 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Energy, available useful work")]
See also: [Exergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exergy "Exergy")
An important and revealing idealized special case is to consider applying the second law to the scenario of an isolated system (called the total system or universe), made up of two parts: a sub-system of interest, and the sub-system's surroundings. These surroundings are imagined to be so large that they can be considered as an _unlimited_ heat reservoir at temperature _T_ R and pressure _P_ R – so that no matter how much heat is transferred to (or from) the sub-system, the temperature of the surroundings will remain _T_ R; and no matter how much the volume of the sub-system expands (or contracts), the pressure of the surroundings will remain _P_ R. 
Whatever changes to _dS_ and _dS_ R occur in the entropies of the sub-system and the surroundings individually, the entropy _S_ tot of the isolated total system must not decrease according to the second law of thermodynamics: 
d S t o t = d S + d S R ≥ 0 {\displaystyle dS_{\mathrm {tot} }=dS+dS_{\text{R}}\geq 0} ![{\\displaystyle dS_{\\mathrm {tot} }=dS+dS_{\\text{R}}\\geq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d1ee0cb66a20406514aa651942169ce5da79de16)
According to the [first law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_law_of_thermodynamics "First law of thermodynamics"), the change _dU_ in the internal energy of the sub-system is the sum of the heat _δq_ added to the sub-system, _minus_ any work _δw_ done _by_ the sub-system, _plus_ any net chemical energy entering the sub-system _d_ Σ _μ iRNi_, so that: 
d U = δ q − δ w + d ( ∑ μ i R N i ) {\displaystyle dU=\delta q-\delta w+d\left(\sum \mu _{iR}N_{i}\right)} ![{\\displaystyle dU=\\delta q-\\delta w+d\\left\(\\sum \\mu _{iR}N_{i}\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f590f84d5e98aa9aa7845e92f6d554846acf5192)
where _μ_ _iR_ are the [chemical potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_potential "Chemical potential") of chemical species in the external surroundings. 
Now the heat leaving the reservoir and entering the sub-system is 
δ q = T R ( − d S R ) ≤ T R d S {\displaystyle \delta q=T_{\text{R}}(-dS_{\text{R}})\leq T_{\text{R}}dS} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta q=T_{\\text{R}}\(-dS_{\\text{R}}\)\\leq T_{\\text{R}}dS}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d5e1e8bbcbeda8267224a93444f2313b2c280074)
where we have first used the definition of entropy in classical thermodynamics (alternatively, in statistical thermodynamics, the relation between entropy change, temperature and absorbed heat can be derived); and then the second law inequality from above. 
It therefore follows that any net work _δw_ done by the sub-system must obey 
δ w ≤ − d U + T R d S + ∑ μ i R d N i {\displaystyle \delta w\leq -dU+T_{\text{R}}dS+\sum \mu _{iR}dN_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta w\\leq -dU+T_{\\text{R}}dS+\\sum \\mu _{iR}dN_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9b8f7943e892e4c9ea8e98ca9921ff904c995db6)
It is useful to separate the work _δw_ done by the subsystem into the _useful_ work _δw u_ that can be done _by_ the sub-system, over and beyond the work _p R dV_ done merely by the sub-system expanding against the surrounding external pressure, giving the following relation for the useful work (exergy) that can be done: 
δ w u ≤ − d ( U − T R S + p R V − ∑ μ i R N i ) {\displaystyle \delta w_{u}\leq -d\left(U-T_{\text{R}}S+p_{\text{R}}V-\sum \mu _{iR}N_{i}\right)} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta w_{u}\\leq -d\\left\(U-T_{\\text{R}}S+p_{\\text{R}}V-\\sum \\mu _{iR}N_{i}\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c93c927d9430f9e7172367c34c6e7b6d08b5c58d)
It is convenient to define the right-hand-side as the exact derivative of a thermodynamic potential, called the _availability_ or _[exergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exergy "Exergy")_ _E_ of the subsystem, 
E = U − T R S + p R V − ∑ μ i R N i {\displaystyle E=U-T_{\text{R}}S+p_{\text{R}}V-\sum \mu _{iR}N_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle E=U-T_{\\text{R}}S+p_{\\text{R}}V-\\sum \\mu _{iR}N_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/eb948b0c889a01e8aa04e170ffce992a1641b2ad)
The second law therefore implies that for any process which can be considered as divided simply into a subsystem, and an unlimited temperature and pressure reservoir with which it is in contact, 
d E + δ w u ≤ 0 {\displaystyle dE+\delta w_{u}\leq 0} ![{\\displaystyle dE+\\delta w_{u}\\leq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/263c3052f2f3f8c38885c3c83a2d9188b932976a)
i.e. the change in the subsystem's exergy plus the useful work done _by_ the subsystem (or, the change in the subsystem's exergy less any work, additional to that done by the pressure reservoir, done _on_ the system) must be less than or equal to zero. 
In sum, if a proper _infinite-reservoir-like_ reference state is chosen as the system surroundings in the real world, then the second law predicts a decrease in _E_ for an irreversible process and no change for a reversible process. 
d S tot ≥ 0 {\displaystyle dS_{\text{tot}}\geq 0} ![{\\displaystyle dS_{\\text{tot}}\\geq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/667b194847be239ef7a0b33078b4018496476625) is equivalent to  d E + δ w u ≤ 0 {\displaystyle dE+\delta w_{u}\leq 0} ![{\\displaystyle dE+\\delta w_{u}\\leq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/263c3052f2f3f8c38885c3c83a2d9188b932976a)
This expression together with the associated reference state permits a [design engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_engineer "Design engineer") working at the macroscopic scale (above the [thermodynamic limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_limit "Thermodynamic limit")) to utilize the second law without directly measuring or considering entropy change in a total isolated system (see also _[Process engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_engineer "Process engineer")_). Those changes have already been considered by the assumption that the system under consideration can reach equilibrium with the reference state without altering the reference state. An efficiency for a process or collection of processes that compares it to the reversible ideal may also be found (see _[Exergy efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exergy_efficiency "Exergy efficiency")_). 
This approach to the second law is widely utilized in [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering "Engineering") practice, [environmental accounting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_accounting "Environmental accounting"), [systems ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systems_ecology "Systems ecology"), and other disciplines. 
## Direction of spontaneous processes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Direction of spontaneous processes")]
The second law determines whether a proposed physical or chemical process is forbidden or may occur spontaneously. For [isolated systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_system "Isolated system"), no energy is provided by the surroundings and the second law requires that the entropy of the system alone cannot decrease: Δ _S_ ≥ 0. Examples of spontaneous physical processes in isolated systems include the following: 
  1. [Heat can be transferred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_transfer "Heat transfer") from a region of higher temperature to a lower temperature (but not the reverse).
  2. Mechanical energy can be converted to thermal energy (but not the reverse).
  3. A solute can move from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration (but not the reverse).


However, for some non-isolated systems which can exchange energy with their surroundings, the surroundings exchange enough heat with the system, or do sufficient work on the system, so that the processes occur in the opposite direction. In such a case, the reverse process can occur because it is _coupled_ to a simultaneous process that _increases_ the entropy of the surroundings. The coupled process will go forward provided that the total entropy change of the system and surroundings combined is nonnegative as required by the second law: Δ _S_ tot = Δ _S_ + Δ _S_ R ≥ 0. For the three examples given above: 
  1. Heat can be transferred from a region of lower temperature to a higher temperature by a [refrigerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigerator "Refrigerator") or [heat pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_pump "Heat pump"), provided that the device delivers sufficient mechanical work to the system and converts it to thermal energy inside the system.
  2. Thermal energy can be converted by a heat engine to mechanical work within a system at a single temperature, provided that the heat engine transfers a sufficient amount of heat from the system to a lower-temperature region in the surroundings.
  3. A solute can travel from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration in the biochemical process of [active transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_transport "Active transport"), if sufficient work is provided by a concentration gradient of a chemical such as [ATP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adenosine_triphosphate "Adenosine triphosphate") or by an [electrochemical gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_gradient "Electrochemical gradient").


### Second law in chemical thermodynamics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Second law in chemical thermodynamics")]
For a [spontaneous chemical process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_process "Spontaneous process") in a closed system at constant temperature and pressure without non-_PV_ work, the Clausius inequality Δ _S_ > _Q/T_ surr transforms into a condition for the change in [Gibbs free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_free_energy "Gibbs free energy")
Δ G < 0 {\displaystyle \Delta G<0} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta G<0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/41ad1c1ad25e7df4a1e249f0955f799538de5208)
or d _G_ < 0. For a similar process at constant temperature and volume, the change in [Helmholtz free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helmholtz_free_energy "Helmholtz free energy") must be negative,  Δ A < 0 {\displaystyle \Delta A<0} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta A<0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/80ac4e13b480ec981508056714912f9aebcdb931). Thus, a negative value of the change in free energy (_G_ or _A_) is a necessary condition for a process to be spontaneous. This is the most useful form of the second law of thermodynamics in chemistry, where free-energy changes can be calculated from tabulated enthalpies of formation and standard molar entropies of reactants and products.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Oxtoby8th-19)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-MortimerBook-15) The chemical equilibrium condition at constant _T_ and _p_ without electrical work is d _G_ = 0. 
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: History")]
See also: [History of entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_entropy "History of entropy")
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Sadi_Carnot.jpeg/250px-Sadi_Carnot.jpeg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Sadi_Carnot.jpeg)Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot in the traditional uniform of a student of the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_Polytechnique "École Polytechnique")
The first theory of the conversion of heat into mechanical work is due to [Sadi Carnot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_L%C3%A9onard_Sadi_Carnot "Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot") in 1824. He was the first to realize correctly that the efficiency of this conversion depends on the difference of temperature between an engine and its surroundings. 
Recognizing the significance of [James Prescott Joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Prescott_Joule "James Prescott Joule")'s work on the conservation of energy, [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius "Rudolf Clausius") was the first to formulate the second law during 1850, in this form: heat does not flow _spontaneously_ from cold to hot bodies. While common knowledge now, this was contrary to the [caloric theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory "Caloric theory") of heat popular at the time, which considered heat as a fluid. From there he was able to infer the principle of Sadi Carnot and the definition of entropy (1865). 
Established during the 19th century, the [Kelvin–Planck statement of the second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin-Planck_statement "Kelvin-Planck statement") says, "It is impossible for any device that operates on a [cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_process "Cyclic process") to receive heat from a single [reservoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_reservoir "Heat reservoir") and produce a net amount of work." This statement was shown to be equivalent to the statement of Clausius. 
The [ergodic hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_hypothesis "Ergodic hypothesis") is also important for [Ludwig Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Boltzmann "Ludwig Boltzmann")'s approach. It says that, over long periods of time, the time spent in some region of the phase space of microstates with the same energy is proportional to the volume of this region, i.e. that all accessible microstates are equally probable over a long period of time. Equivalently, it says that time average and average over the statistical ensemble are the same. 
There is a traditional doctrine, starting with Clausius, that entropy can be understood in terms of molecular 'disorder' within a [macroscopic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic_bodies "Macroscopic bodies"). This doctrine is obsolescent.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-82)[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-83)[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Lambert-84)
### Account given by Clausius
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Account given by Clausius")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/34/Clausius-1.jpg/250px-Clausius-1.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Clausius-1.jpg)Rudolf Clausius
In 1865, the German physicist [Rudolf Clausius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Clausius "Rudolf Clausius") stated what he called the "second fundamental theorem in the [mechanical theory of heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_theory_of_heat "Mechanical theory of heat")" in the following form:[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-FOOTNOTEClausius1867-85)
∫ δ Q T = − N {\displaystyle \int {\frac {\delta Q}{T}}=-N} ![{\\displaystyle \\int {\\frac {\\delta Q}{T}}=-N}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/788c11865876c1029258d9f6c21a942a0909650f)
where _Q_ is heat, _T_ is temperature and _N_ is the "equivalence-value" of all uncompensated transformations involved in a cyclical process. Later, in 1865, Clausius would come to define "equivalence-value" as entropy. On the heels of this definition, that same year, the most famous version of the second law was read in a presentation at the Philosophical Society of Zurich on April 24, in which, in the end of his presentation, Clausius concludes: 
> The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum.
This statement is the best-known phrasing of the second law. Because of the looseness of its language, e.g. [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe"), as well as lack of specific conditions, e.g. open, closed, or isolated, many people take this simple statement to mean that the second law of thermodynamics applies virtually to every subject imaginable. This is not true; this statement is only a simplified version of a more extended and precise description. 
In terms of time variation, the mathematical statement of the second law for an [isolated system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isolated_system "Isolated system") undergoing an arbitrary transformation is: 
d S d t ≥ 0 {\displaystyle {\frac {dS}{dt}}\geq 0} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {dS}{dt}}\\geq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c43dbbd79e66bed761e987211afc239aa28a4c21)
where 
  * _S_ is the entropy of the system;
  * _t_ is [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time "Time").


The equality sign applies after equilibration. An alternative way of formulating of the second law for isolated systems is: 
d S d t = S ˙ i {\displaystyle {\frac {dS}{dt}}={\dot {S}}_{\text{i}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {dS}{dt}}={\\dot {S}}_{\\text{i}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9cb29f3ca9895dedba07fa26fa94cde318dc85a6)
with  S ˙ i ≥ 0 {\displaystyle {\dot {S}}_{\text{i}}\geq 0} ![{\\displaystyle {\\dot {S}}_{\\text{i}}\\geq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c178cbfb1ca94089d12f36d625731fd1a00e227d), the sum of the rate of [entropy production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_production "Entropy production") by all processes inside the system. The advantage of this formulation is that it shows the effect of the entropy production. The rate of entropy production is a very important concept since it determines (limits) the efficiency of thermal machines. Multiplied with ambient temperature  T a {\displaystyle T_{\text{a}}} ![{\\displaystyle T_{\\text{a}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1e2e4686f46533e2d4a091bfde3783858ddb1d7e) it gives the so-called dissipated energy  P diss = T a S ˙ i {\displaystyle P_{\text{diss}}=T_{\text{a}}{\dot {S}}_{\text{i}}} ![{\\displaystyle P_{\\text{diss}}=T_{\\text{a}}{\\dot {S}}_{\\text{i}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d953867441df5a6e55431170b46861d126340d89). 
The expression of the second law for closed systems (so, allowing heat exchange and moving boundaries, but not exchange of matter) is: 
d S d t = Q ˙ T + S ˙ i {\displaystyle {\frac {dS}{dt}}={\frac {\dot {Q}}{T}}+{\dot {S}}_{\text{i}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {dS}{dt}}={\\frac {\\dot {Q}}{T}}+{\\dot {S}}_{\\text{i}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fa7fd070768bac72ee61142bb627daac7fa647f4)
with  S ˙ i ≥ 0 {\displaystyle {\dot {S}}_{\text{i}}\geq 0} ![{\\displaystyle {\\dot {S}}_{\\text{i}}\\geq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c178cbfb1ca94089d12f36d625731fd1a00e227d). Here, 
  * Q ˙ {\displaystyle {\dot {Q}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\dot {Q}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/14ef647ca15bb236a9473fcbe17f16fe87c95ab4) is the heat flow into the system
  * T {\displaystyle T} ![{\\displaystyle T}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ec7200acd984a1d3a3d7dc455e262fbe54f7f6e0) is the temperature at the point where the heat enters the system.


The equality sign holds in the case that only reversible processes take place inside the system. If irreversible processes take place (which is the case in real systems in operation) the >-sign holds. If heat is supplied to the system at several places we have to take the algebraic sum of the corresponding terms. 
For open systems (also allowing exchange of matter): 
d S d t = Q ˙ T + S ˙ + S ˙ i {\displaystyle {\frac {dS}{dt}}={\frac {\dot {Q}}{T}}+{\dot {S}}+{\dot {S}}_{\text{i}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {dS}{dt}}={\\frac {\\dot {Q}}{T}}+{\\dot {S}}+{\\dot {S}}_{\\text{i}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/65b9febd48a49541bd3b1ace200e6d9a5a3bb183)
with  S ˙ i ≥ 0 {\displaystyle {\dot {S}}_{\text{i}}\geq 0} ![{\\displaystyle {\\dot {S}}_{\\text{i}}\\geq 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c178cbfb1ca94089d12f36d625731fd1a00e227d). Here,  S ˙ {\displaystyle {\dot {S}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\dot {S}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/151eef166d15fbc6d73b76167f158c45b7670427) is the flow of entropy into the system associated with the flow of matter entering the system. It should not be confused with the time derivative of the entropy. If matter is supplied at several places we have to take the algebraic sum of these contributions.

[Statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics") gives an explanation for the second law by postulating that a material is composed of atoms and molecules which are in constant motion.[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-86) A particular set of positions and velocities for each particle in the system is called a [microstate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstate_\(statistical_mechanics\) "Microstate \(statistical mechanics\)") of the system and because of the constant motion, the system is constantly changing its microstate.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Statistical mechanics postulates that, in equilibrium, each microstate that the system might be in is equally likely to occur, and when this assumption is made, it leads directly to the conclusion that the second law must hold in a statistical sense.[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-87)[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-88)[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-89) That is, the second law will hold on average, with a statistical variation on the order of 1/√ _N_ where _N_ is the number of particles in the system.[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-90) For everyday (macroscopic) situations, the probability that the second law will be violated is practically zero. However, for systems with a small number of particles, thermodynamic parameters, including the entropy, may show significant statistical deviations from that predicted by the second law.[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-91) Classical thermodynamic theory does not deal with these statistical variations.[[92]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-92)
## Derivation from statistical mechanics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: Derivation from statistical mechanics")]
Further information: [H-theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-theorem "H-theorem")
The first mechanical argument of the [kinetic theory of gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_theory_of_gases "Kinetic theory of gases") that molecular collisions entail an equalization of temperatures and hence a tendency towards equilibrium was due to [James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell "James Clerk Maxwell") in 1860;[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-93) [Ludwig Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Boltzmann "Ludwig Boltzmann") with his [H-theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-theorem "H-theorem") of 1872 also argued that due to collisions gases should over time tend toward the [Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%E2%80%93Boltzmann_distribution "Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution"). 
Due to [Loschmidt's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loschmidt%27s_paradox "Loschmidt's paradox"), derivations of the second law have to make an assumption regarding the past, namely that the system is [uncorrelated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_and_dependence "Correlation and dependence") at some time in the past; this allows for simple probabilistic treatment. This assumption is usually thought as a [boundary condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_condition "Boundary condition"), and thus the second law is ultimately a consequence of the initial conditions somewhere in the past, probably at the beginning of the universe (the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang")), though [other scenarios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_brain "Boltzmann brain") have also been suggested.[[94]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Hawking_AOT-94)[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-95)[[96]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Lebowitz-96)
Given these assumptions, in statistical mechanics, the second law is not a postulate, rather it is a consequence of the [fundamental postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics#Fundamental_postulate "Statistical mechanics"), also known as the equal prior probability postulate, so long as one is clear that simple probability arguments are applied only to the future, while for the past there are auxiliary sources of information which tell us that it was low entropy.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] The first part of the second law, which states that the entropy of a thermally isolated system can only increase, is a trivial consequence of the equal prior probability postulate, if we restrict the notion of the entropy to systems in thermal equilibrium. The entropy of an isolated system in thermal equilibrium containing an amount of energy of  E {\displaystyle E} ![{\\displaystyle E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4232c9de2ee3eec0a9c0a19b15ab92daa6223f9b) is: 
S = k B ln ⁡ [ Ω ( E ) ] {\displaystyle S=k_{\mathrm {B} }\ln \left[\Omega \left(E\right)\right]} ![{\\displaystyle S=k_{\\mathrm {B} }\\ln \\left\[\\Omega \\left\(E\\right\)\\right\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/98aaf7e3918c01dcd3623db44cf34dd698594eda)
where  Ω ( E ) {\displaystyle \Omega \left(E\right)} ![{\\displaystyle \\Omega \\left\(E\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ab95b1220d150818ca655e3491155e0468ce2923) is the number of quantum states in a small interval between  E {\displaystyle E} ![{\\displaystyle E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4232c9de2ee3eec0a9c0a19b15ab92daa6223f9b) and  E + δ E {\displaystyle E+\delta E} ![{\\displaystyle E+\\delta E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4083aab9a05dd601cd79d43fdb9006c40e9baa29). Here  δ E {\displaystyle \delta E} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/14bd788b183deb3082d2f76b8e4101d950292bb0) is a macroscopically small energy interval that is kept fixed. Strictly speaking this means that the entropy depends on the choice of  δ E {\displaystyle \delta E} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/14bd788b183deb3082d2f76b8e4101d950292bb0). However, in the thermodynamic limit (i.e. in the limit of infinitely large system size), the specific entropy (entropy per unit volume or per unit mass) does not depend on  δ E {\displaystyle \delta E} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/14bd788b183deb3082d2f76b8e4101d950292bb0). 
Suppose we have an isolated system whose macroscopic state is specified by a number of variables. These macroscopic variables can, e.g., refer to the total volume, the positions of pistons in the system, etc. Then  Ω {\displaystyle \Omega } ![{\\displaystyle \\Omega }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/24b0d5ca6f381068d756f6337c08e0af9d1eeb6f) will depend on the values of these variables. If a variable is not fixed, (e.g. we do not clamp a piston in a certain position), then because all the accessible states are equally likely in equilibrium, the free variable in equilibrium will be such that  Ω {\displaystyle \Omega } ![{\\displaystyle \\Omega }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/24b0d5ca6f381068d756f6337c08e0af9d1eeb6f) is maximized at the given energy of the isolated system[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Young&FreedmanIS-97) as that is the most probable situation in equilibrium. 
If the variable was initially fixed to some value then upon release and when the new equilibrium has been reached, the fact the variable will adjust itself so that  Ω {\displaystyle \Omega } ![{\\displaystyle \\Omega }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/24b0d5ca6f381068d756f6337c08e0af9d1eeb6f) is maximized, implies that the entropy will have increased or it will have stayed the same (if the value at which the variable was fixed happened to be the equilibrium value). Suppose we start from an equilibrium situation and we suddenly remove a constraint on a variable. Then right after we do this, there are a number  Ω {\displaystyle \Omega } ![{\\displaystyle \\Omega }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/24b0d5ca6f381068d756f6337c08e0af9d1eeb6f) of accessible microstates, but equilibrium has not yet been reached, so the actual probabilities of the system being in some accessible state are not yet equal to the prior probability of  1 / Ω {\displaystyle 1/\Omega } ![{\\displaystyle 1/\\Omega }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8c69a7440bc1d3fa40206529c31c03122d2ad15e). We have already seen that in the final equilibrium state, the entropy will have increased or have stayed the same relative to the previous equilibrium state. Boltzmann's [H-theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-theorem "H-theorem"), however, proves that the quantity _H_ increases monotonically as a function of time during the intermediate out of equilibrium state. 
### Derivation of the entropy change for reversible processes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: Derivation of the entropy change for reversible processes")]
The second part of the second law states that the entropy change of a system undergoing a reversible process is given by: 
d S = δ Q T {\displaystyle dS={\frac {\delta Q}{T}}} ![{\\displaystyle dS={\\frac {\\delta Q}{T}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4a490ba698a8c5716f119d0932af9a11ed483b13)
where the temperature is defined as: 
1 k B T ≡ β ≡ d ln ⁡ [ Ω ( E ) ] d E {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{k_{\mathrm {B} }T}}\equiv \beta \equiv {\frac {d\ln \left[\Omega \left(E\right)\right]}{dE}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {1}{k_{\\mathrm {B} }T}}\\equiv \\beta \\equiv {\\frac {d\\ln \\left\[\\Omega \\left\(E\\right\)\\right\]}{dE}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e720432da60639a2f9cdcefb4ac56845da4f36b0)
See _[Microcanonical ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcanonical_ensemble "Microcanonical ensemble")_ for the justification for this definition. Suppose that the system has some external parameter, _x_ , that can be changed. In general, the energy eigenstates of the system will depend on _x_. According to the [adiabatic theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adiabatic_theorem "Adiabatic theorem") of quantum mechanics, in the limit of an infinitely slow change of the system's Hamiltonian, the system will stay in the same energy eigenstate and thus change its energy according to the change in energy of the energy eigenstate it is in. 
The generalized force, _X_ , corresponding to the external variable _x_ is defined such that  X d x {\displaystyle Xdx} ![{\\displaystyle Xdx}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6331a545837b58e5cc6261e85c7318af63f42a20) is the work performed by the system if _x_ is increased by an amount _dx_. For example, if _x_ is the volume, then _X_ is the pressure. The generalized force for a system known to be in energy eigenstate  E r {\displaystyle E_{r}} ![{\\displaystyle E_{r}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/293328c22d5276d48118b81fdd39033c93c100e1) is given by: 
X = − d E r d x {\displaystyle X=-{\frac {dE_{r}}{dx}}} ![{\\displaystyle X=-{\\frac {dE_{r}}{dx}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ef59b97cf23d098fd2c99ac242f58d488c090efb)
Since the system can be in any energy eigenstate within an interval of  δ E {\displaystyle \delta E} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/14bd788b183deb3082d2f76b8e4101d950292bb0), we define the generalized force for the system as the expectation value of the above expression: 
X = − ⟨ d E r d x ⟩ {\displaystyle X=-\left\langle {\frac {dE_{r}}{dx}}\right\rangle \,} ![{\\displaystyle X=-\\left\\langle {\\frac {dE_{r}}{dx}}\\right\\rangle \\,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/92d3cc55dd6b04f150999033a1d7e431f79b8e57)

### Initial conditions at the Big Bang
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=31 "Edit section: Initial conditions at the Big Bang")]
Further information: [Past hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Past_hypothesis "Past hypothesis")
As elaborated above, it is thought that the second law of thermodynamics is a result of the very low-entropy initial conditions at the [Big Bang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Bang "Big Bang"). From a statistical point of view, these were very special conditions. On the other hand, they were quite simple, as the universe – or at least the part thereof from which the [observable universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe "Observable universe") developed – seems to have been extremely uniform.[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-98)
This may seem somewhat paradoxical, since in many physical systems uniform conditions (e.g. mixed rather than separated gases) have high entropy. The paradox is solved once realizing that gravitational systems have [negative heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_capacity#Negative_heat_capacity "Heat capacity"), so that when gravity is important, uniform conditions (e.g. gas of uniform density) in fact have lower entropy compared to non-uniform ones (e.g. black holes in empty space).[[99]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-99) Yet another approach is that the universe had high (or even maximal) entropy given its size, but as the universe grew it rapidly came out of thermodynamic equilibrium, its entropy only slightly increased compared to the increase in maximal possible entropy, and thus it has arrived at a very low entropy when compared to the much larger possible maximum given its later size.[[100]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-100)
As for the reason why initial conditions were such, one suggestion is that [cosmological inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_inflation "Cosmological inflation") was enough to wipe off non-smoothness, while another is that the universe was [created spontaneously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartle%E2%80%93Hawking_state "Hartle–Hawking state") where the mechanism of creation implies low-entropy initial conditions.[[101]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-101)
## Living organisms
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=32 "Edit section: Living organisms")]
There are two principal ways of formulating thermodynamics, (a) through passages from one state of thermodynamic equilibrium to another, and (b) through cyclic processes, by which the system is left unchanged, while the total entropy of the surroundings is increased. These two ways help to understand the processes of life. The thermodynamics of living organisms has been considered by many authors, including [Erwin Schrödinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Schr%C3%B6dinger "Erwin Schrödinger") (in his book _[What is Life?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What_is_Life%3F "What is Life?")_) and [Léon Brillouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Brillouin "Léon Brillouin").[[102]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Brillouin_2013_p.-102)
To a fair approximation, living organisms may be considered as examples of (b). Approximately, an animal's physical state cycles by the day, leaving the animal nearly unchanged. Animals take in food, water, and oxygen, and, as a result of [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism "Metabolism"), give out breakdown products and heat. Plants [take in radiative energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosynthesis "Photosynthesis") from the sun, which may be regarded as heat, and carbon dioxide and water. They give out oxygen. In this way they grow. Eventually they die, and their remains rot away, turning mostly back into carbon dioxide and water. This can be regarded as a cyclic process. Overall, the sunlight is from a high temperature source, the sun, and its energy is passed to a lower temperature sink, i.e. radiated into space. This is an increase of entropy of the surroundings of the plant. Thus animals and plants obey the second law of thermodynamics, considered in terms of cyclic processes. 
Furthermore, the ability of living organisms to grow and increase in complexity, as well as to form correlations with their environment in the form of adaption and memory, is not opposed to the second law – rather, it is akin to general results following from it: Under some definitions, an increase in entropy also results in an increase in complexity,[[103]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Ladyman_Lambert_Wiesner_pp._33%E2%80%9367-103) and for a finite system interacting with finite reservoirs, an increase in entropy is equivalent to an increase in correlations between the system and the reservoirs.[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-104)
Living organisms may be considered as open systems, because matter passes into and out from them. Thermodynamics of open systems is currently often considered in terms of passages from one state of thermodynamic equilibrium to another, or in terms of flows in the approximation of local thermodynamic equilibrium. The problem for living organisms may be further simplified by the approximation of assuming a steady state with unchanging flows. General principles of entropy production for such approximations are a subject of [ongoing research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-equilibrium_thermodynamics "Non-equilibrium thermodynamics"). 
## Gravitational systems
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=33 "Edit section: Gravitational systems")]
Commonly, systems for which gravity is not important have a positive [heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_capacity "Heat capacity"), meaning that their temperature rises with their internal energy. Therefore, when energy flows from a high-temperature object to a low-temperature object, the source temperature decreases while the sink temperature is increased; hence temperature differences tend to diminish over time. 
This is not always the case for systems in which the gravitational force is important: systems that are bound by their own gravity, such as stars, can have negative heat capacities. As they contract, both their total energy and their entropy decrease[[105]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-105) but [their internal temperature may increase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelvin-Helmholtz_mechanism "Kelvin-Helmholtz mechanism"). This can be significant for [protostars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protostars "Protostars") and even gas giant planets such as [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter "Jupiter"). When the entropy of the [black-body radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-body_radiation "Black-body radiation") emitted by the bodies is included, however, the total entropy of the system can be shown to increase even as the entropy of the planet or star decreases.[[106]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-106)
## Non-equilibrium states
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=34 "Edit section: Non-equilibrium states")]
Main article: [Non-equilibrium thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-equilibrium_thermodynamics "Non-equilibrium thermodynamics")
The theory of classical or [equilibrium thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_equilibrium "Thermodynamic equilibrium") is idealized. A main postulate or assumption, often not even explicitly stated, is the existence of systems in their own internal states of thermodynamic equilibrium. In general, a region of space containing a physical system at a given time, that may be found in nature, is not in thermodynamic equilibrium, read in the most stringent terms. In looser terms, nothing in the entire universe is or has ever been truly in exact thermodynamic equilibrium.[[107]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-Grandy_151-107)[[108]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-108)
For purposes of physical analysis, it is often enough convenient to make an assumption of [thermodynamic equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_equilibrium "Thermodynamic equilibrium"). Such an assumption may rely on trial and error for its justification. If the assumption is justified, it can often be very valuable and useful because it makes available the theory of thermodynamics. Elements of the equilibrium assumption are that a system is observed to be unchanging over an indefinitely long time, and that there are so many particles in a system, that its particulate nature can be entirely ignored. Under such an equilibrium assumption, in general, there are no macroscopically detectable [fluctuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_fluctuations "Thermal fluctuations"). There is an exception, the case of [critical states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_point_\(thermodynamics\) "Critical point \(thermodynamics\)"), which exhibit to the naked eye the phenomenon of [critical opalescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_opalescence "Critical opalescence"). For laboratory studies of critical states, exceptionally long observation times are needed. 
In all cases, the assumption of thermodynamic equilibrium, once made, implies as a consequence that no putative candidate "fluctuation" alters the entropy of the system. 
It can easily happen that a physical system exhibits internal macroscopic changes that are fast enough to invalidate the assumption of the constancy of the entropy. Or that a physical system has so few particles that the particulate nature is manifest in observable fluctuations. Then the assumption of thermodynamic equilibrium is to be abandoned. There is no unqualified general definition of entropy for non-equilibrium states.[[109]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-109)
There are intermediate cases, in which the assumption of local thermodynamic equilibrium is a very good approximation,[[110]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-110)[[111]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-111)[[112]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-112)[[113]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-113) but strictly speaking it is still an approximation, not theoretically ideal. 
For non-equilibrium situations in general, it may be useful to consider statistical mechanical definitions of other quantities that may be conveniently called 'entropy', but they should not be confused or conflated with thermodynamic entropy properly defined for the second law. These other quantities indeed belong to statistical mechanics, not to thermodynamics, the primary realm of the second law. 
The physics of macroscopically observable fluctuations is beyond the scope of this article. 
## Arrow of time
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=35 "Edit section: Arrow of time")]
See also: [Arrow of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_of_time "Arrow of time") and [Entropy (arrow of time)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_\(arrow_of_time\) "Entropy \(arrow of time\)")
The second law of thermodynamics is a physical law that is not symmetric to reversal of the time direction. This does not conflict with symmetries observed in the fundamental laws of physics (particularly [CPT symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CPT_symmetry "CPT symmetry")) since the second law applies statistically on time-asymmetric boundary conditions.[[114]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-114) The second law has been related to the difference between moving forwards and backwards in time, or to the principle that cause precedes effect ([the causal arrow of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow_of_time#Causal_arrow_of_time "Arrow of time"), or [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality "Causality")).[[115]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-115)
## Irreversibility
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=36 "Edit section: Irreversibility")]
Irreversibility in [thermodynamic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_process "Thermodynamic process") is a consequence of the asymmetric character of thermodynamic operations, and not of any internally irreversible microscopic properties of the bodies. Thermodynamic operations are macroscopic external interventions imposed on the participating bodies, not derived from their internal properties. There are reputed "paradoxes" that arise from failure to recognize this. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=37 "Edit section: Loschmidt's paradox")]
Main article: [Loschmidt's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loschmidt%27s_paradox "Loschmidt's paradox")
[Loschmidt's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loschmidt%27s_paradox "Loschmidt's paradox"), also known as the reversibility paradox, is the objection that it should not be possible to deduce an irreversible process from the time-symmetric dynamics that describe the microscopic evolution of a macroscopic system. 
In the opinion of Schrödinger, "It is now quite obvious in what manner you have to reformulate the law of entropy – or for that matter, all other irreversible statements – so that they be capable of being derived from reversible models. You must not speak of one isolated system but at least of two, which you may for the moment consider isolated from the rest of the world, but not always from each other."[[116]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-116) The two systems are isolated from each other by the wall, until it is removed by the thermodynamic operation, as envisaged by the law. The thermodynamic operation is externally imposed, not subject to the reversible microscopic dynamical laws that govern the constituents of the systems. It is the cause of the irreversibility. The statement of the law in this present article complies with Schrödinger's advice. The cause–effect relation is logically prior to the second law, not derived from it. This reaffirms Albert Einstein's postulates that cornerstone Special and General Relativity - that the flow of time is irreversible, however it is relative. Cause must precede effect, but only within the constraints as defined explicitly within [General Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Relativity "General Relativity") (or [Special Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_Relativity "Special Relativity"), depending on the local spacetime conditions). Good examples of this are the [Ladder Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladder_paradox "Ladder paradox"), [time dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_dilation "Time dilation") and [length contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_contraction "Length contraction") exhibited by objects approaching the velocity of light or within proximity of a super-dense region of mass/energy - e.g. black holes, neutron stars, magnetars and quasars. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=38 "Edit section: Poincaré recurrence theorem")]
Main article: [Poincaré recurrence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_recurrence_theorem "Poincaré recurrence theorem")
The [Poincaré recurrence theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincar%C3%A9_recurrence_theorem "Poincaré recurrence theorem") considers a theoretical microscopic description of an isolated physical system. This may be considered as a model of a thermodynamic system after a thermodynamic operation has removed an internal wall. The system will, after a sufficiently long time, return to a microscopically defined state very close to the initial one. The Poincaré recurrence time is the length of time elapsed until the return. It is exceedingly long, likely longer than the life of the universe, and depends sensitively on the geometry of the wall that was removed by the thermodynamic operation. The recurrence theorem may be perceived as apparently contradicting the second law of thermodynamics. More obviously, however, it is simply a microscopic model of thermodynamic equilibrium in an isolated system formed by removal of a wall between two systems. For a typical thermodynamical system, the recurrence time is so large (many many times longer than the lifetime of the universe) that, for all practical purposes, one cannot observe the recurrence. One might wish, nevertheless, to imagine that one could wait for the Poincaré recurrence, and then re-insert the wall that was removed by the thermodynamic operation. It is then evident that the appearance of irreversibility is due to the utter unpredictability of the Poincaré recurrence given only that the initial state was one of thermodynamic equilibrium, as is the case in macroscopic thermodynamics. Even if one could wait for it, one has no practical possibility of picking the right instant at which to re-insert the wall. The Poincaré recurrence theorem provides a solution to Loschmidt's paradox. If an isolated thermodynamic system could be monitored over increasingly many multiples of the average Poincaré recurrence time, the thermodynamic behavior of the system would become invariant under time reversal. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Second_law_of_thermodynamics&action=edit&section=39 "Edit section: Maxwell's demon")]
Main article: [Maxwell's demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%27s_demon "Maxwell's demon")
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6f/James-clerk-maxwell3.jpg/250px-James-clerk-maxwell3.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:James-clerk-maxwell3.jpg)James Clerk Maxwell
[James Clerk Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell "James Clerk Maxwell") imagined one container divided into two parts, _A_ and _B_. Both parts are filled with the same [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas "Gas") at equal temperatures and placed next to each other, separated by a wall. Observing the [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") on both sides, an imaginary [demon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demon "Demon") guards a microscopic trapdoor in the wall. When a faster-than-average molecule from _A_ flies towards the trapdoor, the demon opens it, and the molecule will fly from _A_ to _B_. The average [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed "Speed") of the molecules in _B_ will have increased while in _A_ they will have slowed down on average. Since average molecular speed corresponds to temperature, the temperature decreases in _A_ and increases in _B_ , contrary to the second law of thermodynamics.[[117]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-:1-117)
One response to this question was suggested in 1929 by [Leó Szilárd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le%C3%B3_Szil%C3%A1rd "Leó Szilárd") and later by [Léon Brillouin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Brillouin "Léon Brillouin"). Szilárd pointed out that a real-life Maxwell's demon would need to have some means of measuring molecular speed, and that the act of acquiring information would require an expenditure of energy.[[118]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-:2-118) Likewise, Brillouin demonstrated that the decrease in entropy caused by the demon would be less than the entropy produced by choosing molecules based on their speed.[[117]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-:1-117)
Maxwell's 'demon' repeatedly alters the permeability of the wall between _A_ and _B_. It is therefore performing [thermodynamic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_operation "Thermodynamic operation") on a microscopic scale, not just observing ordinary spontaneous or natural macroscopic thermodynamic processes.[[118]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics#cite_note-:2-118)
