From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Perceptual phenomenon
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/51/Optical_Illustion-Ambiguous_Patterns.svg/120px-Optical_Illustion-Ambiguous_Patterns.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Optical_Illustion-Ambiguous_Patterns.svg)Examples of visually ambiguous patterns. From top to bottom: [Necker cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necker_cube "Necker cube"), [Schroeder stairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schroeder_stairs "Schroeder stairs") and a figure that can be interpreted as black or white arrows.
**Multistable perception** (or **bistable perception**) is a [perceptual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perceptual "Perceptual") phenomenon in which an observer experiences an unpredictable sequence of spontaneous subjective changes. While usually associated with [visual perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_perception "Visual perception") (a form of [optical illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_illusion "Optical illusion")), multistable perception can also be experienced with [auditory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound "Sound") and [olfactory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfactory "Olfactory") percepts. 
## Classification
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Multistable_perception&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Classification")]
Perceptual [multistability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistability "Multistability") can be evoked by visual patterns that are too [ambiguous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambiguous "Ambiguous") for the human [visual system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_system "Visual system") to definitively and uniquely interpret. Familiar examples include the [Necker cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necker_cube "Necker cube"), [Schroeder staircase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schroeder_staircase "Schroeder staircase"), [structure from motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_from_motion "Structure from motion"), [monocular rivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monocular_rivalry "Monocular rivalry"), and [binocular rivalry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binocular_rivalry "Binocular rivalry"), but many more visually [ambiguous patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambiguous_image "Ambiguous image") are known. Because most of these images lead to an alternation between two mutually exclusive perceptual states, they are sometimes also referred to as bistable perception.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-1)
[Auditory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_auditory_perception "Multistable auditory perception") and [olfactory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfactory "Olfactory") examples can occur when there are conflicting, and so rival, inputs into the two [ears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ear "Ear")[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-2) or two [nostrils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nostril "Nostril").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-3)
## Characterization
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Multistable_perception&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Characterization")]
The transition from one percept to its alternative is called a perceptual reversal ([Paradigm shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm_shift "Paradigm shift")). They are spontaneous and [stochastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic "Stochastic") events that cannot be eliminated by intentional efforts, although some control over the alternation process is learnable. Reversal rates vary drastically between stimuli and observers. They are slower for people with [bipolar disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipolar_disorder "Bipolar disorder").[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-5)
## Cultural history
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Multistable_perception&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Cultural history")]
Human interest in these phenomena can be traced back to [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history "Ancient history").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] The fascination with multistable perception probably comes from the active nature of [endogenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogenous "Endogenous") perceptual changes or from the dissociation of dynamic perception from constant sensory stimulation. 
Multistable perception was a common feature in the artwork of the [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlands "Netherlands") [lithographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithography "Lithography") [M. C. Escher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M._C._Escher "M. C. Escher"), who was strongly influenced by mathematical physicists such as [Roger Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Penrose "Roger Penrose").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
## Examples
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Multistable_perception&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Examples")]
### Real-world phenomena
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Multistable_perception&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Real-world phenomena")]
Photographs of craters, from either the moon or other planets including our own, can exhibit this phenomenon. Craters in stereo vision, such as our eyes, normally appear concave. However, in monocular presentations, such as photographs, the elimination of our depth perception causes multistable perception, which can cause the craters to look like plateaus rather than pits. For humans, the "default" interpretation comes from an assumption of [top-left lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Top-left_lighting "Top-left lighting"), so that rotating the image by 180 degrees can cause the perception to suddenly switch.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-7) This phenomenon is called the concave/convex, or simply up/down, ambiguity, and it confuses [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision "Computer vision") as well.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-8)
### In popular culture
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Multistable_perception&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: In popular culture")]
In literature, the [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction "Science fiction") [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel "Novel") _[Dhalgren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhalgren "Dhalgren")_ by [Samuel R. Delany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_R._Delany "Samuel R. Delany"), contains circular text, multistable perception, and multiple entry points.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Multistable perception arises with the theater segments of _[Mystery Science Theater 3000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mystery_Science_Theater_3000 "Mystery Science Theater 3000")_ , as due to the construction of the [Crow T. Robot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crow_T._Robot "Crow T. Robot") puppet, its head can appear to be facing towards the camera rather than towards the film being shown. This was addressed by the creators of the series, even likening Crow to a [Necker cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necker_cube "Necker cube")[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistable_perception#cite_note-necker-9) or [The Spinning Dancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Spinning_Dancer "The Spinning Dancer").[_[when?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items "Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers")_]
