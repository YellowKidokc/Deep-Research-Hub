Theorem in physics
"Bell inequality" redirects here. For the related experiments, see [Bell test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_test "Bell test").
**Bell's theorem** is a term encompassing a number of closely related results in [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), all of which determine that [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") is incompatible with [local hidden-variable theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_hidden-variable_theory "Local hidden-variable theory"), given some basic assumptions about the nature of measurement. The first such result was introduced by [John Stewart Bell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Stewart_Bell "John Stewart Bell") in 1964, building upon the [Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein%E2%80%93Podolsky%E2%80%93Rosen_paradox "Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox"), which had called attention to the phenomenon of [quantum entanglement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_entanglement "Quantum entanglement"). 
In the context of Bell's theorem, "local" refers to the [principle of locality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_locality "Principle of locality"), the idea that a [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle "Particle") can only be influenced by its immediate surroundings, and that interactions mediated by [physical fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_\(physics\) "Field \(physics\)") cannot propagate faster than the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light "Speed of light"). "[Hidden variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden-variable_theory "Hidden-variable theory")" are supposed properties of quantum particles that are not included in quantum theory but nevertheless affect the outcome of experiments. In the words of Bell, "If [a hidden-variable theory] is local it will not agree with quantum mechanics, and if it agrees with quantum mechanics it will not be local."[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-1)
In his original paper[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Bell1964-2), Bell analyzed independent measurements on two spatially separated particles of an entangled pair. He assumed that each outcome is determined by local hidden variables. Under this assumption, the correlations between the outcomes must obey a specific mathematical constraint. Such a constraint would later be named a **Bell inequality**. Bell then showed that quantum physics predicts correlations that violate this [inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_\(mathematics\) "Inequality \(mathematics\)"). Multiple variations on Bell's theorem were put forward in the years following his original paper, using different assumptions and obtaining different Bell (or "Bell-type") inequalities. 
The first rudimentary experiment designed to test Bell's theorem was performed in 1972 by [John Clauser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Clauser "John Clauser") and [Stuart Freedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Freedman "Stuart Freedman").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-3) More advanced experiments, known collectively as _[Bell tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_test "Bell test")_ , have been performed many times since. Often, these experiments have had the goal of "closing loopholes", that is, ameliorating problems of experimental design or set-up that could in principle affect the validity of the findings of earlier Bell tests. Bell tests have consistently found that physical systems obey quantum mechanics and violate Bell inequalities; which is to say that the results of these experiments are incompatible with local hidden-variable theories.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-NAT-20180509-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-5)
The exact nature of the assumptions required to prove a Bell-type constraint on correlations has been debated by physicists and by [philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_physics "Philosophy of physics"). While the significance of Bell's theorem is not in doubt, different [interpretations of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics "Interpretations of quantum mechanics") disagree about what exactly it implies. 
## Theorem
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Theorem")]
There are many variations on the basic idea, some employing stronger mathematical assumptions than others.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Stanford-6) Significantly, Bell-type theorems do not refer to any particular theory of local hidden variables, but instead show that quantum physics violates general assumptions behind classical pictures of nature. The original theorem proved by Bell in 1964 is not the most amenable to experiment, and it is convenient to introduce the genre of Bell-type inequalities with a later example.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-mike-and-ike-7)
Hypothetical characters [Alice and Bob](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_and_Bob "Alice and Bob") stand in widely separated locations. Their colleague Victor prepares a pair of particles and sends one to Alice and the other to Bob. When Alice receives her particle, she chooses to perform one of two possible measurements (perhaps by flipping a coin to decide which). Denote these measurements by  A 0 {\displaystyle A_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle A_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7c98dbd929292d464de6942e95db306b8b8a6fca) and  A 1 {\displaystyle A_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle A_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6bc2435b217c1a0f46f8a517ffa225c6f9440e81). Both  A 0 {\displaystyle A_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle A_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7c98dbd929292d464de6942e95db306b8b8a6fca) and  A 1 {\displaystyle A_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle A_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6bc2435b217c1a0f46f8a517ffa225c6f9440e81) are _binary_ measurements: the result of  A 0 {\displaystyle A_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle A_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7c98dbd929292d464de6942e95db306b8b8a6fca) is either  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958) or  − 1 {\displaystyle -1} ![{\\displaystyle -1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/704fb0427140d054dd267925495e78164fee9aac), and likewise for  A 1 {\displaystyle A_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle A_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6bc2435b217c1a0f46f8a517ffa225c6f9440e81). When Bob receives his particle, he chooses one of two measurements,  B 0 {\displaystyle B_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle B_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/03a3f39a56ba486e7c6ec89b99f5ae2a21fa75b6) and  B 1 {\displaystyle B_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle B_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1fa091eb428443c9c5c5fcf32a69d3665c89e00c), which are also both binary. 
Suppose that each measurement reveals a property that the particle already possessed. obtains the result  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958), then the particle she received carried a value of  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958) for a property  a 0 {\displaystyle a_{0}b_{0}+a_{0}b_{1}+a_{1}b_{0}-a_{1}b_{1}=(a_{0}+a_{1})b_{0}+(a_{0}-a_{1})b_{1}\,.} ![{\\displaystyle both  a 0 {\displaystyle a_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/693ad9f934775838bd72406b41ada4a59785d7ba) and  a 1 {\displaystyle a_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bbf42ecda092975c9c69dae84e16182ba5fe2e07) take the values  ± 1 {\displaystyle \pm 1} ![{\\displaystyle \\pm 1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0bfeaa85da53ad1947d8000926cfea33827ef1e0), then either  a 0 = a 1 {\displaystyle a_{0}=a_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{0}=a_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bc49820ff0c3e8fbe0e51cd2562682d54ef84e31) or  a 0 = − a 1 {\displaystyle a_{0}=-a_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{0}=-a_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b297172b3885d08ce24baafd2e8afad5eb170a0b). In the former case, the quantity  ( a 0 − a 1 ) b 1 {\displaystyle (a_{0}-a_{1})b_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle \(a_{0}-a_{1}\)b_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c2161443f541ad370ddbd3201fe6c7afc4caaed6) must equal 0, while in the latter case,  ( a 0 + a 1 ) b 0 = 0 {\displaystyle (a_{0}+a_{1})b_{0}=0} ![{\\displaystyle \(a_{0}+a_{1}\)b_{0}=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/47a9a4aa56fc083664c66c1e7dcc8f99e38312a9). So, one of the terms on the right-hand side of the above expression will vanish, and the other will equal  ± 2 {\displaystyle \pm 2} ![{\\displaystyle \\pm 2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5535e5dda77acfaf4c88d62923ff40b40175c91d). Consequently, if the experiment is repeated over many trials, with Victor preparing new pairs of particles, the absolute value of the average of the combination  a 0 b 0 + a 0 b 1 + a 1 b 0 − a 1 b 1 {\displaystyle a_{0}b_{0}+a_{0}b_{1}+a_{1}b_{0}-a_{1}b_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{0}b_{0}+a_{0}b_{1}+a_{1}b_{0}-a_{1}b_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9927e97001497cd2d6ab68269e548dd18da1f758) across all the trials will be less than or equal to 2. No _single_ trial can measure this quantity, because Alice and Bob can only choose one measurement each, but on the assumption that the underlying properties exist, the average value of the sum is just the sum of the averages for each term. Using angle brackets to denote averages | ⟨ A 0 B 0 ⟩ + ⟨ A 0 B 1 ⟩ + ⟨ A 1 B 0 ⟩ − ⟨ A 1 B 1 ⟩ | ≤ 2 . {\displaystyle |\langle A_{0}B_{0}\rangle +\langle A_{0}B_{1}\rangle +\langle A_{1}B_{0}\rangle -\langle A_{1}B_{1}\rangle |\leq 2\,.} ![{\\displaystyle |\\langle A_{0}B_{0}\\rangle +\\langle A_{0}B_{1}\\rangle +\\langle A_{1}B_{0}\\rangle -\\langle A_{1}B_{1}\\rangle |\\leq 2\\,.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8bf31903c9d7582f47999568fce8372fab2e6709) This is a Bell inequality, specifically, the [CHSH inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CHSH_inequality "CHSH inequality").[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-mike-and-ike-7): 115  Its derivation here depends upon two assumptions: first, that the underlying physical properties  a 0 , a 1 , b 0 , {\displaystyle a_{0},a_{1},b_{0},} ![{\\displaystyle a_{0},a_{1},b_{0},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cbcd5e64bf1fe7b2acd839da3ebea05346dd5e7d) and  b 1 {\displaystyle b_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle b_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9af2720c91be489f57ecde4bb651b95e113d0144) exist independently of being observed or measured (sometimes called the assumption of _realism_); and second, that Alice's choice of action cannot influence Bob's result or vice versa (often called the assumption of _locality_).[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-mike-and-ike-7): 117

The CHSH inequality can also be thought of as [a _game_ in which Alice and Bob try to coordinate their actions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CHSH_game "CHSH game").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-12)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-13) Victor prepares two bits,  x {\displaystyle x} ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4) and  y {\displaystyle y} ![{\\displaystyle y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b8a6208ec717213d4317e666f1ae872e00620a0d), independently and at random. Alice and Bob win if they return answer bits  a {\displaystyle a} ![{\\displaystyle a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffd2487510aa438433a2579450ab2b3d557e5edc) and  b {\displaystyle b} ![{\\displaystyle b}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f11423fbb2e967f986e36804a8ae4271734917c3) to Victor, satisfying  x y = a + b mod 2 . {\displaystyle xy=a+b\mod 2\,.} ![{\\displaystyle xy=a+b\\mod 2\\,.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/119a4dbd9e7831b80c0f41c2f571bcca91183569) Or, equivalently, Alice and Bob win if the [logical AND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_AND "Logical AND") of  x {\displaystyle x} ![{\\displaystyle x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87f9e315fd7e2ba406057a97300593c4802b53e4) and  y {\displaystyle y} ![{\\displaystyle y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b8a6208ec717213d4317e666f1ae872e00620a0d) is the [logical XOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_XOR "Logical XOR") of  a {\displaystyle a} ![{\\displaystyle a}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffd2487510aa438433a2579450ab2b3d557e5edc) and  b {\displaystyle b} ![{\\displaystyle b}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f11423fbb2e967f986e36804a8ae4271734917c3). Alice and Bob can agree upon any strategy they desire before the game, but they cannot communicate once the game begins. ## Variations and related results
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Variations and related results")]
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Bell \(1964\)")]
Bell's 1964 paper shows that a very simple local hidden-variable model can [in restricted circumstances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_hidden-variable_theory#Models "Local hidden-variable theory") reproduce the predictions of quantum mechanics, but then he demonstrates that, in general, such models give different predictions.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Bell1964-2)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-ND_Mermin_1993-07-14): 806  Bell considers a refinement by [David Bohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bohm "David Bohm") of the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen (EPR) thought experiment. In this scenario, a pair of particles are formed together in such a way that they are described by a [spin singlet state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singlet_state "Singlet state") (which is an example of an entangled state). The particles then move apart in opposite directions. Each particle is measured by a [Stern–Gerlach device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stern%E2%80%93Gerlach_experiment "Stern–Gerlach experiment"), a measuring instrument that can be oriented in different directions and that reports one of two possible outcomes, representable by  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958) and  − 1 {\displaystyle -1} ![{\\displaystyle -1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/704fb0427140d054dd267925495e78164fee9aac). The configuration of each measuring instrument is represented by a unit [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector "Euclidean vector"), and the quantum-mechanical prediction for the [correlation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_correlation "Quantum correlation") between two detectors with settings  a → {\displaystyle {\vec {a}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {a}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/546e6615827e17295718741fd0b86f639a947f16) and  b → {\displaystyle {\vec {b}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3c9ef58be7103eb0b2bfcb460df23430f6a36216) is  P ( a → , b → ) = − a → ⋅ b → . {\displaystyle P({\vec {a}},{\vec {b}})=-{\vec {a}}\cdot {\vec {b}}.} ![{\\displaystyle P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {b}}\)=-{\\vec {a}}\\cdot {\\vec {b}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6201b312ce68f6e2b93370bf5c598e0b6d13d103) In particular, if the orientation of the two detectors is the same ( a → = b → {\displaystyle {\vec {a}}={\vec {b}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {a}}={\\vec {b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1e92f9a9f48bb9c33563d9bd50137e4433db1efa)), then the outcome of one measurement is certain to be the negative of the outcome of the other, giving  P ( a → , a → ) = − 1 {\displaystyle P({\vec {a}},{\vec {a}})=-1} ![{\\displaystyle P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {a}}\)=-1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/43a4b4f7e706b6af5dc6f65c5c9f8b7b99bcab66). And if the orientations of the two detectors are orthogonal ( a → ⋅ b → = 0 {\displaystyle {\vec {a}}\cdot {\vec {b}}=0} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {a}}\\cdot {\\vec {b}}=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f232b9d764e121872b39a9530bc9237c5baf276f)), then the outcomes are uncorrelated, and  P ( a → , b → ) = 0 {\displaystyle P({\vec {a}},{\vec {b}})=0} ![{\\displaystyle P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {b}}\)=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f7abe176aa1d609f825b86fe38a7bd047c046939). Bell proves by example that these special cases _can_ be explained in terms of hidden variables, then proceeds to show that the full range of possibilities involving intermediate angles _cannot_. 
Bell posited that a local hidden-variable model for these correlations would explain them in terms of an integral over the possible values of some hidden parameter  λ {\displaystyle \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b43d0ea3c9c025af1be9128e62a18fa74bedda2a): P ( a → , b → ) = ∫ d λ ρ ( λ ) A ( a → , λ ) B ( b → , λ ) , {\displaystyle P({\vec {a}},{\vec {b}})=\int d\lambda \,\rho (\lambda )A({\vec {a}},\lambda )B({\vec {b}},\lambda ),} ![{\\displaystyle P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {b}}\)=\\int d\\lambda \\,\\rho \(\\lambda \)A\({\\vec {a}},\\lambda \)B\({\\vec {b}},\\lambda \),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ffada964765bb25f2399bb23ae836916bcc3e83e) where  ρ ( λ ) {\displaystyle \rho (\lambda )} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho \(\\lambda \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/85a737d6c810a0dea4b252efe90f371bbf0d8dc4) is a [probability density function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_density_function "Probability density function"). The two functions  A ( a → , λ ) {\displaystyle A({\vec {a}},\lambda )} ![{\\displaystyle A\({\\vec {a}},\\lambda \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5f0c06fbf3fa121791df6a0ea0edbd92841439c8) and  B ( b → , λ ) {\displaystyle B({\vec {b}},\lambda )} ![{\\displaystyle B\({\\vec {b}},\\lambda \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3f5ef27e9126ea1564e438ecd87ced396088f3f2) provide the responses of the two detectors given the orientation vectors and the hidden variable: A ( a → , λ ) = ± 1 , B ( b → , λ ) = ± 1. {\displaystyle A({\vec {a}},\lambda )=\pm 1,\,B({\vec {b}},\lambda )=\pm 1.} ![{\\displaystyle A\({\\vec {a}},\\lambda \)=\\pm 1,\\,B\({\\vec {b}},\\lambda \)=\\pm 1.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3eb2327aafff91d2822e01dcdead6d9ad38a3b7c) Crucially, the outcome of detector  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) does not depend upon  b → {\displaystyle {\vec {b}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3c9ef58be7103eb0b2bfcb460df23430f6a36216), and likewise the outcome of  B {\displaystyle B} ![{\\displaystyle B}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/47136aad860d145f75f3eed3022df827cee94d7a) does not depend upon  a → {\displaystyle {\vec {a}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {a}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/546e6615827e17295718741fd0b86f639a947f16), because the two detectors are physically separated. Now we suppose that the experimenter has a _choice_ of settings for the second detector: it can be set either to  b → {\displaystyle {\vec {b}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3c9ef58be7103eb0b2bfcb460df23430f6a36216) or to  c → {\displaystyle {\vec {c}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {c}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/965bd8710781b710cbfdb79da0b4e3b097bef506). From the assumption that  A ( a → , λ ) = − B ( a → , λ ) {\displaystyle A({\vec {a}},\lambda )=-B({\vec {a}},\lambda )} ![{\\displaystyle A\({\\vec {a}},\\lambda \)=-B\({\\vec {a}},\\lambda \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ea2f150f436fa3b305cd644c318f6167a17c8fd1), that is, perfect anti-correlations are observed for the same setting, Bell proves that | P ( a → , b → ) − P ( a → , c → ) | ≤ 1 + P ( b → , c → ) . {\displaystyle |P({\vec {a}},{\vec {b}})-P({\vec {a}},{\vec {c}})|\leq 1+P({\vec {b}},{\vec {c}}).} ![{\\displaystyle |P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {b}}\)-P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {c}}\)|\\leq 1+P\({\\vec {b}},{\\vec {c}}\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e9fe5d7f8ad0c96200402b7e82f4262e6817c18) However, it is easy to find situations where quantum mechanics violates the Bell inequality while exhibiting perfect anti-correlations.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-15): 425–426  For example, let the vectors  a → {\displaystyle {\vec {a}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {a}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/546e6615827e17295718741fd0b86f639a947f16) and  b → {\displaystyle {\vec {b}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3c9ef58be7103eb0b2bfcb460df23430f6a36216) be orthogonal, and let  c → {\displaystyle {\vec {c}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {c}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/965bd8710781b710cbfdb79da0b4e3b097bef506) lie in their plane at a 45° angle from both of them. Then P ( a → , b → ) = 0 , {\displaystyle P({\vec {a}},{\vec {b}})=0,} ![{\\displaystyle P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {b}}\)=0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/eb449f4ab46ba39b552c0fe1e178b13f2aabff32) while  P ( a → , c → ) = P ( b → , c → ) = − 2 2 , {\displaystyle P({\vec {a}},{\vec {c}})=P({\vec {b}},{\vec {c}})=-{\frac {\sqrt {2}}{2}},} ![{\\displaystyle P\({\\vec {a}},{\\vec {c}}\)=P\({\\vec {b}},{\\vec {c}}\)=-{\\frac {\\sqrt {2}}{2}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d1a31a36f610ec0db26d14658c89644bccfdb64d) but  2 2 ≰ 1 − 2 2 . {\displaystyle {\frac {\sqrt {2}}{2}}\nleq 1-{\frac {\sqrt {2}}{2}}.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {\\sqrt {2}}{2}}\\nleq 1-{\\frac {\\sqrt {2}}{2}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3de88a1464a1e8c2426d6b6c637257d89d73ff7d) Therefore, there is no local hidden-variable model that can reproduce the predictions of quantum mechanics for all choices of  a → {\displaystyle {\vec {a}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {a}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/546e6615827e17295718741fd0b86f639a947f16),  b → {\displaystyle {\vec {b}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3c9ef58be7103eb0b2bfcb460df23430f6a36216), and  c → . {\displaystyle {\vec {c}}.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {c}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/91f7718a5c0bf2cc1edcacefa32a5f3b79db66be) Experimental results contradict the classical curves and match the curve predicted by quantum mechanics as long as experimental shortcomings are accounted for.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Stanford-6)
Bell's 1964 theorem requires the possibility of perfect anti-correlations: the ability to make a completely certain prediction about the result from the second detector, knowing the result from the first.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Stanford-6) The theorem builds upon the "EPR criterion of reality", a concept introduced in the 1935 paper by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen. This paper posits: "If, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty (i.e., with probability equal to unity) the value of a physical quantity, then there exists an element of reality corresponding to that quantity."[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-EPR-16) Bell noted that this applies when the two detectors are oriented in the same direction ( a → = b → {\displaystyle {\vec {a}}={\vec {b}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\vec {a}}={\\vec {b}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1e92f9a9f48bb9c33563d9bd50137e4433db1efa)), and so the EPR criterion would imply that some element of reality must predetermine the measurement result. Because the quantum description of a particle does not include any such element, the quantum description would have to be incomplete. In other words, Bell's 1964 paper shows that, assuming locality, the EPR criterion implies hidden variables and then he demonstrates that local hidden variables are incompatible with quantum mechanics.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-17)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-18) Because experiments cannot achieve perfect correlations or anti-correlations in practice, Bell-type inequalities based on derivations that relax this assumption are tested instead.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Stanford-6)

[Daniel Greenberger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Greenberger "Daniel Greenberger"), [Michael A. Horne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Horne_\(physicist\) "Michael Horne \(physicist\)"), and [Anton Zeilinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Zeilinger "Anton Zeilinger") presented a four-particle thought experiment in 1990, which [David Mermin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N._David_Mermin "N. David Mermin") then simplified to use only three particles.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-GHZ1990-19)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-mermin1990-20) In this thought experiment, Victor generates a set of three spin-1/2 particles described by the quantum state | ψ ⟩ = 1 2 ( | 000 ⟩ − | 111 ⟩ ) , {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle ={\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}}(|000\rangle -|111\rangle )\,,} ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle ={\\frac {1}{\\sqrt {2}}}\(|000\\rangle -|111\\rangle \)\\,,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/36983c34aa2c4216270aa274e6e03c1cd79c3db8) where as above,  | 0 ⟩ {\displaystyle |0\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |0\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ed066a3ad158da0ad6d6a421a606b1c8a35eb95b) and  | 1 ⟩ {\displaystyle |1\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |1\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2f53021ca18e77477ee5bd3c1523e5830189ec5c) are the eigenvectors of the Pauli matrix  σ z {\displaystyle \sigma _{z}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{z}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/61ea9b1af0a90b25f6dcdc140820ff2428fd92c4). Victor then sends a particle each to Alice, Bob, and Charlie, who wait at widely separated locations. Alice measures either  σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebf4ab08fe163a6c495cad6f4d67653287c4044c) or  σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b22d96fceb022e70169a37383278199a26b3534a) on her particle, and so do Bob and Charlie. The result of each measurement is either  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958) or  − 1 {\displaystyle -1} ![{\\displaystyle -1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/704fb0427140d054dd267925495e78164fee9aac). Applying the Born rule to the three-qubit state  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e), Victor predicts that whenever the three measurements include one  σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebf4ab08fe163a6c495cad6f4d67653287c4044c) and two  σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b22d96fceb022e70169a37383278199a26b3534a)'s, the product of the outcomes will always be  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958). This follows because  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) is an eigenvector of  σ x ⊗ σ y ⊗ σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}\otimes \sigma _{y}\otimes \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}\\otimes \\sigma _{y}\\otimes \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/828faf89bce207443464069d236965fa3e205bf3) with eigenvalue  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958), and likewise for  σ y ⊗ σ x ⊗ σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}\otimes \sigma _{x}\otimes \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}\\otimes \\sigma _{x}\\otimes \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c16e29de4bf285c1dbb5ed87bc21cf51a1a231a4) and  σ y ⊗ σ y ⊗ σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}\otimes \sigma _{y}\otimes \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}\\otimes \\sigma _{y}\\otimes \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/546fd7228896f9238c52ae68acbc0c9c14d4aec5). Therefore, knowing Alice's result for a  σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebf4ab08fe163a6c495cad6f4d67653287c4044c) measurement and Bob's result for a  σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b22d96fceb022e70169a37383278199a26b3534a) measurement, Victor can predict with probability 1 what result Charlie will return for a  σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b22d96fceb022e70169a37383278199a26b3534a) measurement. According to the EPR criterion of reality, there would be an "element of reality" corresponding to the outcome of a  σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b22d96fceb022e70169a37383278199a26b3534a) measurement upon Charlie's qubit. Indeed, this same logic applies to both measurements and all three qubits. Per the EPR criterion of reality, then, each particle contains an "instruction set" that determines the outcome of a  σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebf4ab08fe163a6c495cad6f4d67653287c4044c) or  σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b22d96fceb022e70169a37383278199a26b3534a) measurement upon it. The set of all three particles would then be described by the instruction set ( a x , a y , b x , b y , c x , c y ) , {\displaystyle (a_{x},a_{y},b_{x},b_{y},c_{x},c_{y})\,,} ![{\\displaystyle \(a_{x},a_{y},b_{x},b_{y},c_{x},c_{y}\)\\,,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ace2ae772546cd2e1311b63c8bec54ab3f633db3) with each entry being either  − 1 {\displaystyle -1} ![{\\displaystyle -1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/704fb0427140d054dd267925495e78164fee9aac) or  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958), and each  σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebf4ab08fe163a6c495cad6f4d67653287c4044c) or  σ y {\displaystyle \sigma _{y}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{y}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b22d96fceb022e70169a37383278199a26b3534a) measurement simply returning the appropriate value. 
If Alice, Bob, and Charlie all perform the  σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ebf4ab08fe163a6c495cad6f4d67653287c4044c) measurement, then the product of their results would be  a x b x c x {\displaystyle a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}} ![{\\displaystyle a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/169883bb9bb3755ce5dae36f50513539793355e4). This value can be deduced from ( a x b y c y ) ( a y b x c y ) ( a y b y c x ) = a x b x c x a y 2 b y 2 c y 2 = a x b x c x , {\displaystyle (a_{x}b_{y}c_{y})(a_{y}b_{x}c_{y})(a_{y}b_{y}c_{x})=a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}a_{y}^{2}b_{y}^{2}c_{y}^{2}=a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}\,,} ![{\\displaystyle \(a_{x}b_{y}c_{y}\)\(a_{y}b_{x}c_{y}\)\(a_{y}b_{y}c_{x}\)=a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}a_{y}^{2}b_{y}^{2}c_{y}^{2}=a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}\\,,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/815dc41c5e63205db5eadb16e9a800bec29257be) because the square of either  − 1 {\displaystyle -1} ![{\\displaystyle -1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/704fb0427140d054dd267925495e78164fee9aac) or  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958) is  1 {\displaystyle 1} ![{\\displaystyle 1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/92d98b82a3778f043108d4e20960a9193df57cbf). Each factor in parentheses equals  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958), so a x b x c x = + 1 , {\displaystyle a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}=+1\,,} ![{\\displaystyle a_{x}b_{x}c_{x}=+1\\,,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a18ac85063dc6de93b1ffc124551b708d813a7e1) and the product of Alice, Bob, and Charlie's results will be  + 1 {\displaystyle +1} ![{\\displaystyle +1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d04cf05c67d41d9f39dabf6a90722ce860a76958) with probability unity. But this is inconsistent with quantum physics: Victor can predict using the state  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) that the measurement  σ x ⊗ σ x ⊗ σ x {\displaystyle \sigma _{x}\otimes \sigma _{x}\otimes \sigma _{x}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{x}\\otimes \\sigma _{x}\\otimes \\sigma _{x}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7c1aae5e1a37691678a3aed985ece56afe85d01e) will instead yield  − 1 {\displaystyle -1} ![{\\displaystyle -1}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/704fb0427140d054dd267925495e78164fee9aac) with probability unity. 
This thought experiment can also be recast as a traditional Bell inequality or, equivalently, as a nonlocal game in the same spirit as the CHSH game.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Brassard_2004-21) In it, Alice, Bob, and Charlie receive bits  x , y , z {\displaystyle x,y,z} ![{\\displaystyle x,y,z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bbeca34b28f569a407ef74a955d041df9f360268) from Victor, promised to always have an even number of ones, that is,  x ⊕ y ⊕ z = 0 {\displaystyle x\oplus y\oplus z=0} ![{\\displaystyle x\\oplus y\\oplus z=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4824e314aec4a0ee773966de6080b517eb058a84), and send him back bits  a , b , c {\displaystyle a,b,c} ![{\\displaystyle a,b,c}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f13f068df656c1b1911ae9f81628c49a6181194d). They win the game if  a , b , c {\displaystyle a,b,c} ![{\\displaystyle a,b,c}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f13f068df656c1b1911ae9f81628c49a6181194d) have an odd number of ones for all inputs except  x = y = z = 0 {\displaystyle x=y=z=0} ![{\\displaystyle x=y=z=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/806816016dd7dfb90cb1cd0560506c14a29e2535), when they need to have an even number of ones. That is, they win the game [if and only if](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/If_and_only_if "If and only if") a ⊕ b ⊕ c = x ∨ y ∨ z {\displaystyle a\oplus b\oplus c=x\lor y\lor z} ![{\\displaystyle a\\oplus b\\oplus c=x\\lor y\\lor z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b9bb2737a2c4c599f1a7f5eac66615d9ba522525). With local hidden variables the highest probability of victory they can have is 3/4, whereas using the quantum strategy above they win it with certainty. This is an example of [quantum pseudo-telepathy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_pseudo-telepathy "Quantum pseudo-telepathy"). 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Kochen–Specker theorem \(1967\)")]
Main article: [Kochen–Specker theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kochen%E2%80%93Specker_theorem "Kochen–Specker theorem")
In quantum theory, orthonormal bases for a [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space") represent measurements that can be performed upon a system having that Hilbert space. Each vector in a basis represents a possible outcome of that measurement.[[note 2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-26) Suppose that a hidden variable  λ {\displaystyle \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b43d0ea3c9c025af1be9128e62a18fa74bedda2a) exists, so that knowing the value of  λ {\displaystyle \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b43d0ea3c9c025af1be9128e62a18fa74bedda2a) would imply certainty about the outcome of any measurement. Given a value of  λ {\displaystyle \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b43d0ea3c9c025af1be9128e62a18fa74bedda2a), each measurement outcome – that is, each vector in the Hilbert space – is either _impossible_ or _guaranteed._ A Kochen–Specker configuration is a finite set of vectors made of multiple interlocking bases, with the property that a vector in it will always be _impossible_ when considered as belonging to one basis and _guaranteed_ when taken as belonging to another. In other words, a Kochen–Specker configuration is an "uncolorable set" that demonstrates the inconsistency of assuming a hidden variable  λ {\displaystyle \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b43d0ea3c9c025af1be9128e62a18fa74bedda2a) can be controlling the measurement outcomes.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-27): 196–201 
### Free will theorem
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Free will theorem")]
Main article: [Free will theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will_theorem "Free will theorem")
The Kochen–Specker type of argument, using configurations of interlocking bases, can be combined with the idea of measuring entangled pairs that underlies Bell-type inequalities. This was noted beginning in the 1970s by Kochen,[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-28) Heywood and Redhead,[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-29) Stairs,[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-30) and Brown and Svetlichny.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-31) As EPR pointed out, obtaining a measurement outcome on one half of an entangled pair implies certainty about the outcome of a corresponding measurement on the other half. The "EPR criterion of reality" posits that because the second half of the pair was not disturbed, that certainty must be due to a physical property belonging to it.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-32) In other words, by this criterion, a hidden variable  λ {\displaystyle \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b43d0ea3c9c025af1be9128e62a18fa74bedda2a) must exist within the second, as-yet unmeasured half of the pair. No contradiction arises if only one measurement on the first half is considered. However, if the observer has a choice of multiple possible measurements, and the vectors defining those measurements form a Kochen–Specker configuration, then some outcome on the second half will be simultaneously impossible and guaranteed. 
This type of argument gained attention when an instance of it was advanced by [John Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horton_Conway "John Horton Conway") and [Simon Kochen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_B._Kochen "Simon B. Kochen") under the name of the [free will theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will_theorem "Free will theorem").[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-33)[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-34)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-35) The Conway–Kochen theorem uses a pair of entangled [qutrits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qutrit "Qutrit") and a Kochen–Specker configuration discovered by [Asher Peres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asher_Peres "Asher Peres").[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-36)
### Quasiclassical entanglement
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Quasiclassical entanglement")]
Main articles: [Spekkens toy model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spekkens_toy_model "Spekkens toy model") and [Werner state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_state "Werner state")
As Bell pointed out, some predictions of quantum mechanics can be replicated in local hidden-variable models, including special cases of correlations produced from entanglement. This topic has been studied systematically in the years since Bell's theorem. In 1989, [Reinhard Werner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinhard_F._Werner "Reinhard F. Werner") introduced what are now called [Werner states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_state "Werner state"), joint quantum states for a pair of systems that yield EPR-type correlations but also admit a hidden-variable model.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-37) Werner states are bipartite quantum states that are invariant under [unitaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarity_\(physics\) "Unitarity \(physics\)") of symmetric [tensor-product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronecker_product "Kronecker product") form:  ρ A B = ( U ⊗ U ) ρ A B ( U † ⊗ U † ) . {\displaystyle \rho _{AB}=(U\otimes U)\rho _{AB}(U^{\dagger }\otimes U^{\dagger }).} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho _{AB}=\(U\\otimes U\)\\rho _{AB}\(U^{\\dagger }\\otimes U^{\\dagger }\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ab52b8c0f08b7ccaac64c309b50ddd269644fe38) In 2004, [Robert Spekkens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Spekkens "Robert Spekkens") introduced a [toy model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spekkens_toy_model "Spekkens toy model") that starts with the premise of local, discretized degrees of freedom and then imposes a "knowledge balance principle" that restricts how much an observer can know about those degrees of freedom, thereby making them into hidden variables. The allowed states of knowledge ("epistemic states") about the underlying variables ("ontic states") mimic some features of quantum states. Correlations in the toy model can emulate some aspects of entanglement, like [monogamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monogamy_of_entanglement "Monogamy of entanglement"), but by construction, the toy model can never violate a Bell inequality.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-38)[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-39)
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: History")]
### Background
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Background")]
Main articles: [EPR paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EPR_paradox "EPR paradox") and [History of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_quantum_mechanics "History of quantum mechanics")
The question of whether quantum mechanics can be "completed" by hidden variables dates to the early years of quantum theory. In his [1932 textbook on quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Foundations_of_Quantum_Mechanics "Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics"), the Hungarian-born polymath [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann") presented what he claimed to be a proof that there could be no "hidden parameters". The validity and definitiveness of von Neumann's proof were questioned by [Hans Reichenbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Reichenbach "Hans Reichenbach"), in more detail by [Grete Hermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grete_Hermann "Grete Hermann"), and possibly in conversation though not in print by Albert Einstein.[[note 3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-45) ([Simon Kochen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_B._Kochen "Simon B. Kochen") and [Ernst Specker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Specker "Ernst Specker") rejected von Neumann's key assumption as early as 1961, but did not publish a criticism of it until 1967.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-46)) 
Einstein argued persistently that quantum mechanics could not be a complete theory. His preferred argument relied on a principle of locality:      Consider a mechanical system constituted of two partial systems _A_ and _B_ which have interaction with each other only during limited time. Let the ψ function before their interaction be given. Then the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger_equation "Schrödinger equation") will furnish the ψ function after their interaction has taken place. Let us now determine the physical condition of the partial system _A_ as completely as possible by measurements. Then the quantum mechanics allows us to determine the ψ function of the partial system _B_ from the measurements made, and from the ψ function of the total system. This determination, however, gives a result which depends upon _which_ of the determining magnitudes specifying the condition of _A_ has been measured (for instance coordinates _or_ momenta). Since there can be only _one_ physical condition of _B_ after the interaction and which can reasonably not be considered as dependent on the particular measurement we perform on the system _A_ separated from _B_ it may be concluded that the ψ function is not unambiguously coordinated with the physical condition. This coordination of several ψ functions with the same physical condition of system _B_ shows again that the ψ function cannot be interpreted as a (complete) description of a physical condition of a unit system.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-47)
The EPR thought experiment is similar, also considering two separated systems _A_ and _B_ described by a joint wave function. However, the EPR paper adds the idea later known as the EPR criterion of reality, according to which the ability to predict with probability 1 the outcome of a measurement upon _B_ implies the existence of an "element of reality" within _B_.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-48)
In 1951, [David Bohm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bohm "David Bohm") proposed a variant of the EPR thought experiment in which the measurements have discrete ranges of possible outcomes, unlike the position and momentum measurements considered by EPR.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-49) The year before, [Chien-Shiung Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chien-Shiung_Wu "Chien-Shiung Wu") and Irving Shaknov had successfully measured polarizations of photons produced in entangled pairs, thereby making the Bohm version of the EPR thought experiment practically feasible.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-50)
By the late 1940s, the mathematician [George Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey "George Mackey") had grown interested in the foundations of quantum physics, and in 1957 he drew up a list of postulates that he took to be a precise definition of quantum mechanics.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-51) Mackey conjectured that one of the postulates was redundant, and shortly thereafter, [Andrew M. Gleason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_M._Gleason "Andrew M. Gleason") proved that it was indeed deducible from the other postulates.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-gleason1957-52)[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-chernoff2009-53) [Gleason's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gleason%27s_theorem "Gleason's theorem") provided an argument that a broad class of hidden-variable theories are incompatible with quantum mechanics.[[note 4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-55) More specifically, Gleason's theorem rules out hidden-variable models that are "noncontextual". Any hidden-variable model for quantum mechanics must, in order to avoid the implications of Gleason's theorem, involve hidden variables that are not properties belonging to the measured system alone but also dependent upon the external context in which the measurement is made. This type of dependence is often seen as contrived or undesirable; in some settings, it is inconsistent with [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity").[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-ND_Mermin_1993-07-14)[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-56) The Kochen–Specker theorem refines this statement by constructing a specific finite subset of rays on which no such probability measure can be defined.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-ND_Mermin_1993-07-14)[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-57)
[Tsung-Dao Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsung-Dao_Lee "Tsung-Dao Lee") came close to deriving Bell's theorem in 1960. He considered events where two [kaons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaon "Kaon") were produced traveling in opposite directions, and came to the conclusion that hidden variables could not explain the correlations that could be obtained in such situations. However, complications arose due to the fact that kaons decay, and he did not go so far as to deduce a Bell-type inequality.[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-jammer1974-41): 308 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Bell's publications")]
Bell chose to publish his theorem in a comparatively obscure journal because it did not require [page charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Page_charge "Page charge"), in fact paying the authors who published there at the time. Because the journal did not provide free reprints of articles for the authors to distribute, however, Bell had to spend the money he received to buy copies that he could send to other physicists.[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-:0-58) While the articles printed in the journal themselves listed the publication's name simply as _Physics_ , the covers carried the trilingual version _[Physics Physique Физика](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_Physique_%D0%A4%D0%B8%D0%B7%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B0 "Physics Physique Физика")_ to reflect that it would print articles in English, French and Russian.[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-:1-44): 92–100, 289 
Prior to proving his 1964 result, Bell also proved a result equivalent to the Kochen–Specker theorem (hence the latter is sometimes also known as the Bell–Kochen–Specker or Bell–KS theorem). However, publication of this theorem was inadvertently delayed until 1966.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-ND_Mermin_1993-07-14)[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Bell1966-59) In that paper, Bell argued that because an explanation of quantum phenomena in terms of hidden variables would require nonlocality, the EPR paradox "is resolved in the way which Einstein would have liked least."[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Bell1966-59)
## Experiments
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Experiments")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Bell-test-photon-analyer.svg/500px-Bell-test-photon-analyer.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Bell-test-photon-analyer.svg)**Scheme of a "two-channel" Bell test**  
The source S produces pairs of "photons", sent in opposite directions. Each photon encounters a two-channel polariser whose orientation (a or b) can be set by the experimenter. Emerging signals from each channel are detected and coincidences of four types (++, −−, +− and −+) counted by the coincidence monitor.
Main article: [Bell test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_test "Bell test")
In 1967, the unusual title _Physics Physique Физика_ caught the attention of [John Clauser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Clauser "John Clauser"), who then discovered Bell's paper and began to consider how to perform a [Bell test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_test "Bell test") in the laboratory.[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-60) Clauser and [Stuart Freedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Freedman "Stuart Freedman") would go on to perform a Bell test in 1972.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-61)[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-62) This was only a limited test, because the choice of detector settings was made before the photons had left the source. In 1982, [Alain Aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_Aspect "Alain Aspect") and collaborators performed the [first Bell test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect%27s_experiment "Aspect's experiment") to remove this limitation.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-63) This began a trend of progressively more stringent Bell tests. The GHZ thought experiment was implemented in practice, using entangled triplets of photons, in 2000.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-GHZ2000-64) By 2002, testing the CHSH inequality was feasible in undergraduate laboratory courses.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-65)
In Bell tests, there may be problems of experimental design or set-up that affect the validity of the experimental findings. These problems are often referred to as "loopholes". The purpose of the experiment is to test whether nature can be described by [local hidden-variable theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_hidden-variable_theory "Local hidden-variable theory"), which would contradict the predictions of quantum mechanics. 
The most prevalent loopholes in real experiments are the _detection_ and _locality_ loopholes.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-larsson14-66) The detection loophole is opened when a small fraction of the particles (usually photons) are detected in the experiment, making it possible to explain the data with local hidden variables by assuming that the detected particles are an unrepresentative sample. The locality loophole is opened when the detections are not done with a [spacelike separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime#Spacetime_interval "Spacetime"), making it possible for the result of one measurement to influence the other without contradicting relativity. In some experiments there may be additional defects that make local-hidden-variable explanations of Bell test violations possible.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-67)
Although both the locality and detection loopholes had been closed in different experiments, a long-standing challenge was to close both simultaneously in the same experiment. This was finally achieved in three experiments in 2015.[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-68)[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-NYT-20151021-69)[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-NTR-20151021-70)[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-PRL115-250402-71)[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-PRL115-250401-72) Regarding these results, [Alain Aspect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_Aspect "Alain Aspect") writes that "no experiment ... can be said to be totally loophole-free," but he says the experiments "remove the last doubts that we should renounce" local hidden variables, and refers to examples of remaining loopholes as being "far fetched" and "foreign to the usual way of reasoning in physics."[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-73)
These efforts to experimentally validate violations of the Bell inequalities would later result in Clauser, Aspect, and [Anton Zeilinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Zeilinger "Anton Zeilinger") being awarded the 2022 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics").[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-74)
## Interpretations
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Interpretations")]
Main article: [Interpretations of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics "Interpretations of quantum mechanics")
Reactions to Bell's theorem have been many and varied. Maximilian Schlosshauer, Johannes Kofler, and Zeilinger write that Bell inequalities provide "a wonderful example of how we can have a rigorous theoretical result tested by numerous experiments, and yet disagree about the implications."[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-75)
### The Copenhagen interpretation
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: The Copenhagen interpretation")]
[Copenhagen-type interpretations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_interpretation "Copenhagen interpretation") generally take the violation of Bell inequalities as grounds to reject the assumption often called [counterfactual definiteness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterfactual_definiteness "Counterfactual definiteness") or "realism", which is not necessarily the same as abandoning realism in a broader philosophical sense.[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-76)[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-77) For example, [Roland Omnès](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roland_Omn%C3%A8s "Roland Omnès") argues for the rejection of hidden variables and concludes that "quantum mechanics is probably as realistic as any theory of its scope and maturity ever will be".[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-omnes-78): 531  Likewise, [Rudolf Peierls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Peierls "Rudolf Peierls") took the message of Bell's theorem to be that, because the premise of locality is physically reasonable, "hidden variables cannot be introduced without abandoning some of the results of quantum mechanics".[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-79)[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-80)
This is also the route taken by interpretations that descend from the Copenhagen tradition, such as [consistent histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent_histories "Consistent histories") (often advertised as "Copenhagen done right"),[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-81): 2839 as well as [QBism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QBism "QBism").[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-82)
### Many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics")]
The [many-worlds interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation "Many-worlds interpretation"), also known as the [Everett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Everett_III "Hugh Everett III") interpretation, is dynamically local, meaning that it does not call for [action at a distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_at_a_distance "Action at a distance"),[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-BrownTimpson-83): 17  and deterministic, because it consists of the unitary part of quantum mechanics without collapse. It can generate correlations that violate a Bell inequality because it violates an implicit assumption by Bell that measurements have a single outcome. In fact, Bell's theorem can be proven in the Many-Worlds framework from the assumption that a measurement has a single outcome. Therefore, a violation of a Bell inequality can be interpreted as a demonstration that measurements have multiple outcomes.[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-84)
The explanation it provides for the Bell correlations is that when Alice and Bob make their measurements, they split into local branches. From the point of view of each copy of Alice, there are multiple copies of Bob experiencing different results, so Bob cannot have a definite result, and the same is true from the point of view of each copy of Bob. They will obtain a mutually well-defined result only when their future light cones overlap. At this point we can say that the Bell correlation starts existing, but it was produced by a purely local mechanism. Therefore, the violation of a Bell inequality cannot be interpreted as a proof of non-locality.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-BrownTimpson-83): 28
### Non-local hidden variables
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Non-local hidden variables")]
Most advocates of the hidden-variables idea believe that experiments have ruled out local hidden variables.[[note 5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-87) They are ready to give up locality, explaining the violation of Bell's inequality by means of a non-local [hidden variable theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_variable_theory "Hidden variable theory"), in which the particles exchange information about their states. This is the basis of the [Bohm interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohm_interpretation "Bohm interpretation") of quantum mechanics, which requires that all particles in the universe be able to instantaneously exchange information with all others. One challenge for non-local hidden variable theories is to explain why this instantaneous communication can exist at the level of the hidden variables, but it cannot be used to send signals.[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-88) A 2007 experiment ruled out a large class of non-Bohmian non-local hidden variable theories, though not Bohmian mechanics itself.[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-89)
The [transactional interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transactional_interpretation "Transactional interpretation"), which postulates waves traveling both backwards and forwards in time, is likewise non-local.[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-90)
### Superdeterminism
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Bell%27s_theorem&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Superdeterminism")]
Main article: [Superdeterminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superdeterminism "Superdeterminism")
A necessary assumption to derive Bell's theorem is that the hidden variables are not correlated with the measurement settings. This assumption has been justified on the grounds that the experimenter has "[free will](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_will "Free will")" to choose the settings, and that it is necessary to do science in the first place. A (hypothetical) theory where the choice of measurement is necessarily correlated with the system being measured is known as _superdeterministic_.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-larsson14-66)
A few advocates of deterministic models have not given up on local hidden variables. For example, [Gerard 't Hooft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_%27t_Hooft "Gerard 't Hooft") has argued that superdeterminism cannot be dismissed.[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-91)

## Notes

  1. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_ref-10 "Jump up")** We are for convenience assuming that the response of the detector to the underlying property is deterministic. This assumption can be replaced; it is equivalent to postulating a joint probability distribution over all the observables of the experiment.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-9)
  2. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_ref-26 "Jump up")** In more detail, as developed by [Paul Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dirac "Paul Dirac"),[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-22) [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert"),[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-23) [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann"),[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-24) and [Hermann Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Weyl "Hermann Weyl"),[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-25) the state of a quantum mechanical system is a vector  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) belonging to a ([separable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_space "Separable space")) Hilbert space  H {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {H}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/19ef4c7b923a5125ac91aa491838a95ee15b804f). Physical quantities of interest — position, momentum, energy, spin — are represented by "observables", which are [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint_operator "Self-adjoint operator") linear [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_\(physics\) "Operator \(physics\)") acting on the Hilbert space. When an observable is measured, the result will be one of its eigenvalues with probability given by the [Born rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule "Born rule"): in the simplest case the eigenvalue  η {\displaystyle \eta } ![{\\displaystyle \\eta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e4d701857cf5fbec133eebaf94deadf722537f64) is non-degenerate and the probability is given by  | ⟨ η | ψ ⟩ | 2 {\displaystyle |\langle \eta |\psi \rangle |^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle |\\langle \\eta |\\psi \\rangle |^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/38b0862b588e2ecc8975dc68c457e5a17844f4cd), where  | η ⟩ {\displaystyle |\eta \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\eta \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1731d8f806cedf7f01c6012042f4ccae2a230a39) is its associated eigenvector. More generally, the eigenvalue is degenerate and the probability is given by  ⟨ ψ | P η ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle \psi |P_{\eta }\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\psi |P_{\\eta }\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d18d4db25091fa5dc2f8109bd81a9e6a121bda05), where  P η {\displaystyle P_{\eta }} ![{\\displaystyle P_{\\eta }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/678d243a7593224cc7a342b2ac662b509f75d075) is the projector onto its associated eigenspace. For the purposes of this discussion, we can take the eigenvalues to be non-degenerate.

  4. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_ref-55 "Jump up")** A hidden-variable theory that is [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinism "Determinism") implies that the probability of a given outcome is _always_ either 0 or 1. For example, a Stern–Gerlach measurement on a [spin-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_\(physics\) "Spin \(physics\)") atom will report that the atom's angular momentum along the chosen axis is one of three possible values, which can be designated  − {\displaystyle -} ![{\\displaystyle -}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/04bd52ce670743d3b61bec928a7ec9f47309eb36),  0 {\displaystyle 0} ![{\\displaystyle 0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2aae8864a3c1fec9585261791a809ddec1489950) and  + {\displaystyle +} ![{\\displaystyle +}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fe6ef363cd19902d1a7a71fb1c8b21e8ede52406). In a deterministic hidden-variable theory, there exists an underlying physical property that fixes the result found in the measurement. Conditional on the value of the underlying physical property, any given outcome (for example, a result of  + {\displaystyle +} ![{\\displaystyle +}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fe6ef363cd19902d1a7a71fb1c8b21e8ede52406)) must be either impossible or guaranteed. But Gleason's theorem implies that there can be no such deterministic probability measure, because it proves that any probability measure must take the form of a mapping  u → ⟨ ρ u , u ⟩ {\displaystyle u\to \langle \rho u,u\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle u\\to \\langle \\rho u,u\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4f9dd8a2ca37be0af5c3c49b5036aefba0be8209) for some density operator  ρ {\displaystyle \rho } ![{\\displaystyle \\rho }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f7d439671d1289b6a816e6af7a304be40608d64). This mapping is continuous on the [unit sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_sphere "Unit sphere") of the Hilbert space, and since this unit sphere is [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_\(topology\) "Connected \(topology\)"), no continuous probability measure on it can be deterministic.[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-wilce2017-54): §1.3

Jaynes") was one exception,[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-E.T._Jaynes_1989-85) but Jaynes' arguments have not generally been found persuasive.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem#cite_note-Gill2002-86)
