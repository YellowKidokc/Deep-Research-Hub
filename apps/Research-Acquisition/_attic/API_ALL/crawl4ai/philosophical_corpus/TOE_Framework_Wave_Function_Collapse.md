From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Process by which a quantum system takes on a definitive state
For the constraint-solving algorithm, see [Wave function collapse (algorithm)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse_\(algorithm\) "Wave function collapse \(algorithm\)").
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7d/Wave-particle_duality.gif/250px-Wave-particle_duality.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wave-particle_duality.gif)Particle impacts during a [double-slit experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-slit_experiment "Double-slit experiment"). The total [interference pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_pattern "Interference pattern") represents the original [wave function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function "Wave function"), while each particle impact represents an individual wave function collapse. In various [interpretations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics "Interpretations of quantum mechanics") of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), **wave function collapse** , also called **reduction of the state vector** ,[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-1) occurs when a [wave function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function "Wave function")—initially in a [superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_superposition "Quantum superposition") of several [eigenstates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenstates "Eigenstates")—reduces to a single eigenstate due to [interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction "Fundamental interaction") with the external world. This interaction is called an [_observation_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation_\(physics\) "Observation \(physics\)") and is the essence of a [measurement in quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_in_quantum_mechanics "Measurement in quantum mechanics"), which connects the wave function with classical [observables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable "Observable") such as [position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_\(vector\) "Position \(vector\)") and [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum "Momentum"). Collapse is one of the two processes by which [quantum systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_system "Quantum system") evolve in time; the other is the continuous evolution governed by the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger_equation "Schrödinger equation").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Grundlagen-2)
In the [Copenhagen interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_interpretation "Copenhagen interpretation"), wave function collapse connects quantum to classical models, with a special [role for the observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_interpretation#Role_of_the_observer "Copenhagen interpretation"). By contrast, [objective-collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective-collapse_theory "Objective-collapse theory") proposes an origin in physical processes. In the [many-worlds interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation "Many-worlds interpretation"), collapse does not exist; all wave function outcomes occur while [quantum decoherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_decoherence "Quantum decoherence") accounts for the appearance of collapse. 
Historically, [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg") was the first to use the idea of wave function reduction to explain quantum measurement.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-C._Kiefer-2002-4)
## Mathematical description
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Mathematical description")]
For an explanation of the notation used, see [Bra–ket notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bra%E2%80%93ket_notation "Bra–ket notation"). For details on this formalism, see [Quantum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state "Quantum state").
In quantum mechanics each measurable physical quantity of a quantum system is called an [observable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable "Observable") which, for example, could be the position  r {\displaystyle r} ![{\\displaystyle r}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0d1ecb613aa2984f0576f70f86650b7c2a132538) and the momentum  p {\displaystyle p} ![{\\displaystyle p}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81eac1e205430d1f40810df36a0edffdc367af36) but also energy  E {\displaystyle E} ![{\\displaystyle E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4232c9de2ee3eec0a9c0a19b15ab92daa6223f9b),  z {\displaystyle z} ![{\\displaystyle z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bf368e72c009decd9b6686ee84a375632e11de98) components of spin ( s z {\displaystyle s_{z}} ![{\\displaystyle s_{z}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fe17b6a1d1ce8d16e8753353f2b8b575ae4381d)), and so on. The observable acts as a [linear function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_mapping "Linear mapping") on the states of the system; its eigenvectors correspond to the quantum state (i.e. [eigenstate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state#Pure_states_of_wave_functions "Quantum state")) and the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue "Eigenvalue") to the possible values of the observable. The collection of eigenstates/eigenvalue pairs represent all possible values of the observable. Writing  ϕ i {\displaystyle \phi _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0182dbf29b54844c92fd9b0311778a02a38398ec) for an eigenstate and  c i {\displaystyle c_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle c_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01acb7953ba52c2aa44264b5d0f8fd223aa178a2) for the corresponding observed value, any arbitrary state of the quantum system can be expressed as a vector using [bra–ket notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bra%E2%80%93ket_notation "Bra–ket notation"):  | ψ ⟩ = ∑ i c i | ϕ i ⟩ . {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle =\sum _{i}c_{i}|\phi _{i}\rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle =\\sum _{i}c_{i}|\\phi _{i}\\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/15dc1f8043f6e6aa04cc43d6fe0599362aad6b25) The kets  { | ϕ i ⟩ } {\displaystyle \\{|\phi _{i}\rangle \\}} ![{\\displaystyle \\{|\\phi _{i}\\rangle \\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c1b096484c065039acf661b662d7fe01f3a475e9) specify the different available quantum "alternatives", i.e., particular quantum states. 
The [wave function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function "Wave function") is a specific representation of a quantum state. Wave functions can therefore always be expressed as eigenstates of an observable though the converse is not necessarily true. 
### Collapse
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Collapse")]
To account for the experimental result that repeated measurements of a quantum system give the same results, the theory postulates a "collapse" or "reduction of the state vector" upon observation,[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-GriffithsSchroeter3rd-5): 566 abruptly converting an arbitrary state into a single component eigenstate of the observable:       | ψ ⟩ = ∑ i c i | ϕ i ⟩ ↦ | ψ ′ ⟩ = | ϕ i ⟩ . {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle =\sum _{i}c_{i}|\phi _{i}\rangle \mapsto |\psi '\rangle =|\phi _{i}\rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle =\\sum _{i}c_{i}|\\phi _{i}\\rangle \\mapsto |\\psi '\\rangle =|\\phi _{i}\\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d8af82f845ca9eb3163a8f42034bd98246631ab2)
where the arrow represents a measurement of the observable corresponding to the  ϕ {\displaystyle \phi } ![{\\displaystyle \\phi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72b1f30316670aee6270a28334bdf4f5072cdde4) basis.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-6) For any single event, only one eigenvalue is measured, chosen randomly from among the possible values. 
### Meaning of the expansion coefficients
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Meaning of the expansion coefficients")]
The [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number "Complex number") coefficients  { c i } {\displaystyle \\{c_{i}\\}} ![{\\displaystyle \\{c_{i}\\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9c181688b279d6a1e343259c49156d91e8345fb0) in the expansion of a quantum state in terms of eigenstates  { | ϕ i ⟩ } {\displaystyle \\{|\phi _{i}\rangle \\}} ![{\\displaystyle \\{|\\phi _{i}\\rangle \\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c1b096484c065039acf661b662d7fe01f3a475e9),  | ψ ⟩ = ∑ i c i | ϕ i ⟩ . {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle =\sum _{i}c_{i}|\phi _{i}\rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle =\\sum _{i}c_{i}|\\phi _{i}\\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/15dc1f8043f6e6aa04cc43d6fe0599362aad6b25) can be written as an (complex) overlap of the corresponding eigenstate and the quantum state:  c i = ⟨ ϕ i | ψ ⟩ . {\displaystyle c_{i}=\langle \phi _{i}|\psi \rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle c_{i}=\\langle \\phi _{i}|\\psi \\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/669a3aebf214e1cb35176d66700f561e5cf4ea78) They are called the [probability amplitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude "Probability amplitude"). The [square modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_value#Complex_numbers "Absolute value") | c i | 2 {\displaystyle |c_{i}|^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle |c_{i}|^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7616cf4bbd92dbb939ef4eccd47e711faa4086f9) is the probability that a measurement of the observable yields the eigenstate  | ϕ i ⟩ {\displaystyle |\phi _{i}\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\phi _{i}\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/afe78aae7ac03e9cfc121eb4917e6a1d15a441d3). The sum of the probability over all possible outcomes must be one:[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-7)      ⟨ ψ | ψ ⟩ = ∑ i | c i | 2 = 1. {\displaystyle \langle \psi |\psi \rangle =\sum _{i}|c_{i}|^{2}=1.} ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\psi |\\psi \\rangle =\\sum _{i}|c_{i}|^{2}=1.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/74e9b1662bf601c77e66d03559ae974bff52c5c9)
As examples, individual counts in a [double slit experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_slit_experiment "Double slit experiment") with electrons appear at random locations on the detector; after many counts are summed the distribution shows a wave interference pattern.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Bach_Pope_Liou_Batelaan_2013_p=033018-8) In a [Stern-Gerlach experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stern-Gerlach_experiment "Stern-Gerlach experiment") with silver atoms, each particle appears in one of two areas unpredictably, but the final conclusion has equal numbers of events in each area. 
This statistical aspect of quantum measurements differs fundamentally from [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics"). In quantum mechanics the only information we have about a system is its wave function and measurements of its wave function can only give statistical information.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-GriffithsSchroeter3rd-5): 17 
## Terminology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Terminology")]
The two terms "reduction of the state vector" (or "state reduction" for short) and "wave function collapse" are used to describe the same concept. A [quantum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state "Quantum state") is a mathematical description of a quantum system; a [quantum state vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state_vector "Quantum state vector") uses Hilbert space vectors for the description.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-messiah-9): 159  Reduction of the state vector replaces the full state vector with a single eigenstate of the observable. 
The term "wave function" is typically used for a different mathematical representation of the quantum state, one that uses spatial coordinates also called the "position representation".[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-messiah-9): 324  When the wave function representation is used, the "reduction" is called "wave function collapse". 
## The measurement problem
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: The measurement problem")]
The Schrödinger equation describes quantum systems but does not describe their measurement. Solution to the equations include all possible observable values for measurements, but measurements only result in one definite outcome. This difference is called the [measurement problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_problem "Measurement problem") of quantum mechanics. To predict measurement outcomes from quantum solutions, the orthodox interpretation of quantum theory postulates wave function collapse and uses the [Born rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule "Born rule") to compute the probable outcomes.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-10) Despite the widespread quantitative success of these postulates scientists remain dissatisfied and have sought more detailed physical models. Rather than suspending the Schrödinger equation during the process of measurement, the measurement apparatus should be included and governed by the laws of quantum mechanics.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-11): 127 
## Physical approaches to collapse
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Physical approaches to collapse")]
Quantum theory offers no dynamical description of the "collapse" of the wave function. Viewed as a statistical theory, no description is expected. As Fuchs and Peres put it, "collapse is something that happens in our description of the system, not to the system itself".[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-FuchsPeresNo-12)
Various [interpretations of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpretations_of_quantum_mechanics "Interpretations of quantum mechanics") attempt to provide a physical model for collapse.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Stamatescu-13): 816  Three treatments of collapse can be found among the common interpretations. The first group includes hidden-variable theories like [de Broglie–Bohm theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Broglie%E2%80%93Bohm_theory "De Broglie–Bohm theory"); here random outcomes only result from unknown values of hidden variables. Results from [tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_test "Bell test") of [Bell's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem "Bell's theorem") shows that these variables would need to be non-local. The second group models measurement as quantum entanglement between the quantum state and the measurement apparatus. This results in a simulation of classical statistics called quantum decoherence. This group includes the [many-worlds interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation "Many-worlds interpretation") and [consistent histories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent_histories "Consistent histories") models. The third group postulates additional, but as yet undetected, physical basis for the randomness; this group includes for example the [objective-collapse interpretations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective-collapse_interpretation "Objective-collapse interpretation"). While models in all groups have contributed to better understanding of quantum theory, no alternative explanation for individual events has emerged as more useful than collapse followed by statistical prediction with the Born rule.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Stamatescu-13): 819 
The significance ascribed to the wave function varies from interpretation to interpretation and even within an interpretation (such as the [Copenhagen interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_interpretation "Copenhagen interpretation")). If the wave function merely encodes an observer's knowledge of the universe, then the wave function collapse corresponds to the receipt of new information. This is somewhat analogous to the situation in classical physics, except that the classical "wave function" does not necessarily obey a wave equation. If the wave function is physically real, in some sense and to some extent, then the collapse of the wave function is also seen as a real process, to the same extent.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
### Quantum decoherence
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Quantum decoherence")]
Main article: [Quantum decoherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_decoherence "Quantum decoherence")
Quantum decoherence explains why a system interacting with an environment transitions from being a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state#Pure_states_as_rays_in_a_complex_Hilbert_space "Quantum state"), exhibiting superpositions, to a [mixed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state#Mixed_states "Quantum state"), an incoherent combination of classical alternatives.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Stanford1-14) This transition is fundamentally reversible, as the combined state of system and environment is still pure, but for all practical purposes irreversible in the same sense as in the [second law of thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_law_of_thermodynamics "Second law of thermodynamics"): the environment is a very large and complex quantum system, and it is not feasible to reverse their interaction. Decoherence is thus very important for explaining the [classical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit "Classical limit") of quantum mechanics, but cannot explain wave function collapse, as all classical alternatives are still present in the mixed state, and wave function collapse selects only one of them.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Schlosshauer-15)[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-16)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Stanford1-14)
The form of decoherence known as [environment-induced superselection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einselection "Einselection") proposes that when a quantum system interacts with the environment, the superpositions _apparently_ reduce to mixtures of classical alternatives. The combined wave function of the system and environment continue to obey the Schrödinger equation throughout this _apparent_ collapse.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Zurek-17) More importantly, this is not enough to explain _actual_ wave function collapse, as decoherence does not reduce it to a single eigenstate.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Schlosshauer-15)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Stanford1-14)
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Wave_function_collapse&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: History")]
The concept of wavefunction collapse was introduced by [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg") in his 1927 paper on the [uncertainty principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_principle "Uncertainty principle"), "Über den anschaulichen Inhalt der quantentheoretischen Kinematik und Mechanik", and incorporated into the [mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_formulation_of_quantum_mechanics "Mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics") by [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann"), in his 1932 treatise _Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik_.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-C._Kiefer-2002-4) Heisenberg did not try to specify exactly what the collapse of the wavefunction meant. However, he emphasized that it should not be understood as a physical process.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-18) Niels Bohr never mentions wave function collapse in his published work, but he repeatedly cautioned that we must give up a "pictorial representation". Despite the differences between Bohr and Heisenberg, their views are often grouped together as the "Copenhagen interpretation", of which wave function collapse is regarded as a key feature.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-19)
[John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann")'s influential 1932 work _[Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Foundations_of_Quantum_Mechanics "Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics")_ took a more formal approach, developing an "ideal" measurement scheme[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-HartleQMCosmology-20)[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-SchlosshauerReview-21): 1270 that postulated that there were two processes of wave function change: 
  1. The [probabilistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability "Probability"), non-[unitary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_transformation "Unitary transformation"), [non-local](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_realism "Local realism"), discontinuous change brought about by observation and [measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_measurement "Quantum measurement") (state reduction or collapse).
  2. The [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic "Deterministic"), unitary, continuous [time evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_evolution "Time evolution") of an isolated system that obeys the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger_equation "Schrödinger equation").


In 1957 [Hugh Everett III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugh_Everett_III "Hugh Everett III") proposed a model of quantum mechanics that dropped von Neumann's first postulate. Everett observed that the measurement apparatus was also a quantum system and its quantum interaction with the system under observation should determine the results. He proposed that the discontinuous change is instead a splitting of a wave function representing the universe.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-SchlosshauerReview-21): 1288  While Everett's approach rekindled interest in foundational quantum mechanics, it left core issues unresolved. Two key issues relate to origin of the observed classical results: what causes quantum systems to appear classical and to resolve with the observed probabilities of the [Born rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule "Born rule").[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-SchlosshauerReview-21): 1290 [[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-HartleQMCosmology-20): 5 
Beginning in 1970 [H. Dieter Zeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Dieter_Zeh "H. Dieter Zeh") sought a detailed quantum decoherence model for the discontinuous change without postulating collapse. Further work by [Wojciech H. Zurek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wojciech_H._Zurek "Wojciech H. Zurek") in 1980 lead eventually to a large number of papers on many aspects of the concept.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-22) Decoherence assumes that every quantum system interacts quantum mechanically with its environment and such interaction is not separable from the system, a concept called an "open system".[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-SchlosshauerReview-21): 1273  Decoherence has been shown to work very quickly and within a minimal environment, but as yet it has not succeeded in a providing a detailed model replacing the collapse postulate of orthodox quantum mechanics.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-SchlosshauerReview-21): 1302 
By explicitly dealing with the interaction of object and measuring instrument, von Neumann[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-Grundlagen-2) described a quantum mechanical measurement scheme consistent with wave function collapse. However, he did not prove the _necessity_ of such a collapse. Von Neumann's projection postulate was conceived based on experimental evidence available during the 1930s, in particular [Compton scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compton_scattering "Compton scattering"). Later work refined the notion of measurements into the more easily discussed _first kind_ , that will give the same value when immediately repeated, and the _second kind_ that give different values when repeated.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-23)[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-24)[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_collapse#cite_note-25)
