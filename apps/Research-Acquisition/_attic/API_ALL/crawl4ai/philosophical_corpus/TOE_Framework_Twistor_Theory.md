From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Theory proposed by Roger Penrose
In [theoretical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics"), **twistor theory** was proposed by [Roger Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Penrose "Roger Penrose") in 1967[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Pen1-1) as a possible path[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-PenMac-2) to [quantum gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_gravity "Quantum gravity") and has evolved into a widely studied branch of [theoretical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics "Theoretical physics") and [mathematical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics "Mathematical physics"). Penrose's idea was that [twistor space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_space "Twistor space") should be the basic arena for physics from which [space-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-time "Space-time") itself should emerge. It has led to powerful mathematical tools that have applications to [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry") and [integral geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_geometry "Integral geometry"), [nonlinear differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_differential_equation "Nonlinear differential equation") and [representation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_theory "Representation theory"), and in physics to [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"), [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"), and the theory of [scattering amplitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_amplitude "Scattering amplitude"). 
Twistor theory arose in the context of the rapidly expanding mathematical developments in Einstein's theory of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") in the late 1950s and in the 1960s and carries a number of influences from that period. In particular, [Roger Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Penrose "Roger Penrose") has credited [Ivor Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivor_Robinson_\(physicist\) "Ivor Robinson \(physicist\)") as an important early influence in the development of twistor theory, through his construction of so-called _Robinson congruences_.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-3)
## Overview
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Twistor_theory&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview")]
[Projective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_space "Projective space") [twistor space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_space "Twistor space") P T {\displaystyle \mathbb {PT} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {PT} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a5cb12846bd6e3df92c17bb5683d670b58e0813f) is [projective 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_space "Projective space") C P 3 {\displaystyle \mathbb {CP} ^{3}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {CP} ^{3}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc0027650c56bc444e5afc7adc18206cb49c120f), the simplest [3-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-dimensional "3-dimensional") [compact algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_manifold "Complex manifold"). It has a physical interpretation as the space of [massless particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle "Massless particle") with [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_\(physics\) "Spin \(physics\)"). It is the [projectivisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectivisation "Projectivisation") of a 4-dimensional [complex vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space "Complex vector space"), non-projective twistor space  T {\displaystyle \mathbb {T} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {T} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9c039979935c00b3b216cbb065999207872677f6), with a [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form "Hermitian form") of [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_signature "Metric signature") (2, 2) and a [holomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic "Holomorphic") [volume form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume_form "Volume form"). This can be most naturally understood as the space of [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_\(physics\) "Chirality \(physics\)") ([Weyl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_spinor "Weyl spinor")) [spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor "Spinor") for the [conformal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_group "Conformal group") SO(4,2)/Z2 of [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space "Minkowski space"); it is the [fundamental representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_representation "Fundamental representation") of the [spin group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_group "Spin group") SU(2,2) of the conformal group. This definition can be extended to arbitrary dimensions except that beyond dimension four, one defines projective twistor space to be the space of projective [pure spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_spinor "Pure spinor")[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-HS1-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-HS2-5) for the conformal group.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-PenRin-6)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-7)
In its original form, twistor theory encodes [physical fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_field "Physical field") on Minkowski space in terms of [complex analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analytic "Complex analytic") objects on twistor space via the [Penrose transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose_transform "Penrose transform"). This is especially natural for [massless fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle "Massless particle") of arbitrary [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_\(physics\) "Spin \(physics\)"). In the first instance these are obtained via [contour integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_integral "Contour integral") formulae in terms of free holomorphic functions on regions in twistor space. The holomorphic twistor functions that give rise to solutions to the massless field equations can be more deeply understood as [Čech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%8Cech_cohomology "Čech cohomology") representatives of analytic [cohomology classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_classes "Cohomology classes") on regions in  P T {\displaystyle \mathbb {PT} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {PT} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a5cb12846bd6e3df92c17bb5683d670b58e0813f). These correspondences have been extended to certain nonlinear fields, including [self-dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-dual "Self-dual") gravity in Penrose's [nonlinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_field_equations#Nonlinearity "Einstein field equations") [graviton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton "Graviton") construction[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Penrose1976-8) and self-dual [Yang–Mills fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang%E2%80%93Mills_field "Yang–Mills field") in the so-called Ward construction;[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-9) the former gives rise to [deformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_\(mathematics\) "Deformation \(mathematics\)") of the underlying complex structure of regions in  P T {\displaystyle \mathbb {PT} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {PT} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a5cb12846bd6e3df92c17bb5683d670b58e0813f), and the latter to certain holomorphic vector bundles over regions in  P T {\displaystyle \mathbb {PT} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {PT} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a5cb12846bd6e3df92c17bb5683d670b58e0813f). These constructions have had wide applications, including inter alia the theory of [integrable systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_system "Integrable system").[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-10)[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-11)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-12)
The self-duality condition is a major limitation for incorporating the full nonlinearities of physical theories, although it does suffice for [Yang–Mills–Higgs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang%E2%80%93Mills%E2%80%93Higgs_equations "Yang–Mills–Higgs equations") [monopoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole "Magnetic monopole") and [instantons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instanton "Instanton") (see [ADHM construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ADHM_construction "ADHM construction")).[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-13) An early attempt to overcome this restriction was the introduction of **ambitwistors** by Isenberg, Yasskin and Green,[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-IYG-14) and their [superspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superspace "Superspace") extension, **super-ambitwistors** , by [Edward Witten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Witten "Edward Witten").[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Wi1-15) Ambitwistor space is the space of complexified light rays or massless particles and can be regarded as a [complexification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexification "Complexification") or [cotangent bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotangent_bundle "Cotangent bundle") of the original twistor description. By extending the ambitwistor correspondence to suitably defined formal neighborhoods, Isenberg, Yasskin and Green[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-IYG-14) showed the equivalence between the vanishing of the curvature along such extended null lines and the full Yang–Mills field equations.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-IYG-14) Witten[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Wi1-15) showed that a further extension, within the framework of super Yang–Mills theory, including [fermionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermionic_field "Fermionic field") and scalar fields, gave rise, in the case of _N_ = 1 or 2 [supersymmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersymmetry "Supersymmetry"), to the constraint equations, while for _N_ = 3 (or 4), the vanishing condition for supercurvature along super null lines (super ambitwistors) implied the full set of [field equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N_%3D_4_supersymmetric_Yang%E2%80%93Mills_theory "N = 4 supersymmetric Yang–Mills theory"), including those for the fermionic fields. This was subsequently shown to give a 1-1[_[clarify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] equivalence between the null curvature constraint equations and the supersymmetric Yang-Mills field equations.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-HLHS-16)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-HHS-17) Through dimensional reduction, it may also be deduced from the analogous super-ambitwistor correspondence for 10-dimensional, _N_ = 1 super-Yang–Mills theory.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-W2-18)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-HS-19)
Twistorial formulae for [interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction "Fundamental interaction") beyond the self-dual sector also arose in Witten's [twistor string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_string_theory "Twistor string theory"),[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Witten2004-20) which is a quantum theory of holomorphic maps of a [Riemann surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surface "Riemann surface") into twistor space. This gave rise to the remarkably compact RSV (Roiban, Spradlin and Volovich) formulae for tree-level [S-matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-matrices "S-matrices") of Yang–Mills theories,[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-21) but its gravity degrees of freedom gave rise to a version of conformal [supergravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supergravity "Supergravity") limiting its applicability; [conformal gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_gravity "Conformal gravity") is an unphysical theory containing [ghosts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_\(physics\) "Ghost \(physics\)"), but its interactions are combined with those of Yang–Mills theory in loop amplitudes calculated via twistor string theory.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-22)
Despite its shortcomings, twistor string theory led to rapid developments in the study of scattering amplitudes. One was the so-called MHV formalism[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-23) loosely based on disconnected strings, but was given a more basic foundation in terms of a twistor action for full Yang–Mills theory in twistor space.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-24) Another key development was the introduction of [BCFW recursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCFW_recursion "BCFW recursion").[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-25) This has a natural formulation in twistor space[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-26)[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-27) that in turn led to remarkable formulations of scattering amplitudes in terms of [Grassmann integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassmann_integral "Grassmann integral") formulae[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-28)[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-29) and [polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytope "Polytope").[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-30) These ideas have evolved more recently into the positive [Grassmannian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grassmannian "Grassmannian")[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-31) and [amplituhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplituhedron "Amplituhedron"). 
Twistor string theory was extended first by generalising the RSV Yang–Mills amplitude formula, and then by finding the underlying [string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory "String theory"). The extension to gravity was given by Cachazo & Skinner,[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-32) and formulated as a twistor string theory for [maximal supergravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_supergravity "Maximal supergravity") by David Skinner.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-33) Analogous formulae were then found in all dimensions by Cachazo, He and Yuan for Yang–Mills theory and gravity[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-34) and subsequently for a variety of other theories.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-35) They were then understood as string theories in ambitwistor space by Mason and Skinner[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-36) in a general framework that includes the original twistor string and extends to give a number of new models and formulae.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-37)[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-38)[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-39) As string theories they have the same [critical dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_dimension "Critical dimension") as conventional string theory; for example the [type II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_II_string_theory "Type II string theory") supersymmetric versions are critical in ten dimensions and are equivalent to the full field theory of type II supergravities in ten dimensions (this is distinct from conventional string theories that also have a further infinite hierarchy of massive higher spin states that provide an [ultraviolet completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet_completion "Ultraviolet completion")). They extend to give formulae for loop amplitudes[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-40)[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-41) and can be defined on curved backgrounds.[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-42)
## Twistor correspondence
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Twistor_theory&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Twistor correspondence")]
Denote [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space "Minkowski space") by ⁠ M {\displaystyle M} ![{\\displaystyle M}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f82cade9898ced02fdd08712e5f0c0151758a0dd)⁠, with coordinates  x a = ( t , x , y , z ) {\displaystyle x^{a}=(t,x,y,z)} ![{\\displaystyle x^{a}=\(t,x,y,z\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0086173db7e92ad5d2a5d196af3a7fde8c86ff94) and Lorentzian metric  η a b {\displaystyle \eta _{ab}} ![{\\displaystyle \\eta _{ab}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4544d099bcc5697d9eab4c80180fb7b96e90207f) signature  ( 1 , 3 ) {\displaystyle (1,3)} ![{\\displaystyle \(1,3\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8c80e593e3953231c56d0887f5b247bbe517461f). Introduce 2-component spinor indices  A = 0 , 1 ; A ′ = 0 ′ , 1 ′ , {\displaystyle A=0,1;\;A'=0',1',} ![{\\displaystyle A=0,1;\\;A'=0',1',}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d79c6cf0b8de094021ab2e68e1955af7a5def2a5) and set       x A A ′ = 1 2 ( t − z x + i y x − i y t + z ) . {\displaystyle x^{AA'}={\frac {1}{\sqrt {2}}}{\begin{pmatrix}t-z&x+iy\\\x-iy&t+z\end{pmatrix}}.} ![{\\displaystyle x^{AA'}={\\frac {1}{\\sqrt {2}}}{\\begin{pmatrix}t-z&x+iy\\\\x-iy&t+z\\end{pmatrix}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fb281e3609003f3a9513d110814ac46a742e9a5f)
Non-projective twistor space  T {\displaystyle \mathbb {T} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {T} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9c039979935c00b3b216cbb065999207872677f6) is a four-dimensional complex vector space with coordinates denoted by  Z α = ( ω A , π A ′ ) {\displaystyle Z^{\alpha }=\left(\omega ^{A},\,\pi _{A'}\right)} ![{\\displaystyle Z^{\\alpha }=\\left\(\\omega ^{A},\\,\\pi _{A'}\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6ab4223d816f2cfbfb1d32e168ec87b23ea3b77f) where  ω A {\displaystyle \omega ^{A}} ![{\\displaystyle \\omega ^{A}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/17f36caf71ef0f641c7a8388f33316c0b33923b4) and  π A ′ {\displaystyle \pi _{A'}} ![{\\displaystyle \\pi _{A'}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3ec8f6270d547fc610ad0e69a48be1125cc22eaf) are two constant [Weyl spinors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weyl_spinor "Weyl spinor"). The hermitian form can be expressed by defining a complex conjugation from  T {\displaystyle \mathbb {T} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {T} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9c039979935c00b3b216cbb065999207872677f6) to its dual  T ∗ {\displaystyle \mathbb {T} ^{*}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {T} ^{*}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/16dfeeba73d0d191694bc893bcff40e4c9b81195) by  Z ¯ α = ( π ¯ A , ω ¯ A ′ ) {\displaystyle {\bar {Z}}_{\alpha }=\left({\bar {\pi }}_{A},\,{\bar {\omega }}^{A'}\right)} ![{\\displaystyle {\\bar {Z}}_{\\alpha }=\\left\({\\bar {\\pi }}_{A},\\,{\\bar {\\omega }}^{A'}\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/52f83378a6f9c0a785a827d22681ff4bb1069408) so that the Hermitian form can be expressed as       Z α Z ¯ α = ω A π ¯ A + ω ¯ A ′ π A ′ . {\displaystyle Z^{\alpha }{\bar {Z}}_{\alpha }=\omega ^{A}{\bar {\pi }}_{A}+{\bar {\omega }}^{A'}\pi _{A'}.} ![{\\displaystyle Z^{\\alpha }{\\bar {Z}}_{\\alpha }=\\omega ^{A}{\\bar {\\pi }}_{A}+{\\bar {\\omega }}^{A'}\\pi _{A'}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/181bd097a1b4dcdfd16ab310903ba82aae7ea3cd)
This together with the holomorphic volume form,  ε α β γ δ Z α d Z β ∧ d Z γ ∧ d Z δ {\displaystyle \varepsilon _{\alpha \beta \gamma \delta }Z^{\alpha }dZ^{\beta }\wedge dZ^{\gamma }\wedge dZ^{\delta }} ![{\\displaystyle \\varepsilon _{\\alpha \\beta \\gamma \\delta }Z^{\\alpha }dZ^{\\beta }\\wedge dZ^{\\gamma }\\wedge dZ^{\\delta }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e6d9cbb1e9e837de6292a6ea1f0571f4c9684630) is invariant under the group SU(2,2), a quadruple cover of the conformal group C(1,3) of compactified Minkowski spacetime. 
Points in Minkowski space are related to subspaces of twistor space through the incidence relation       ω A = i x A A ′ π A ′ . {\displaystyle \omega ^{A}=ix^{AA'}\pi _{A'}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\omega ^{A}=ix^{AA'}\\pi _{A'}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5260345f52eb01cd50c6c9a336d93dfedb1d00d9)
The incidence relation is preserved under an overall complex rescaling of the twistor. This scaling assigns a flagpole extent, flag-plane direction, and a spinorial sign to the ray ⁠ Z ∈ P T {\displaystyle \mathbf {Z} \in \mathbb {PT} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbf {Z} \\in \\mathbb {PT} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4e26f6142888547dbda8b1f414bcb7ec6366fd96)⁠. A real translation  x a → x a + q a {\displaystyle x^{a}\rightarrow x^{a}+q^{a}} ![{\\displaystyle x^{a}\\rightarrow x^{a}+q^{a}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8f218fdce03acf3bd3d61684446fe79402fd154b) will leave  Z α {\displaystyle Z^{\alpha }} ![{\\displaystyle Z^{\\alpha }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/df940f06b549f18f77762e86cfa3adfe15d98a87) invariant whenever  q a {\displaystyle q^{a}} ![{\\displaystyle q^{a}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4211df01a7b05611f5c3aa7fd00beabef9f48117) has the form  q a = u π ¯ A π A ′ {\displaystyle q^{a}=u{\bar {\pi }}^{A}\pi ^{A'}} ![{\\displaystyle q^{a}=u{\\bar {\\pi }}^{A}\\pi ^{A'}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ef779aefd297f5fa636cff9c0611788760d5022e), where  u {\displaystyle u} ![{\\displaystyle u}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3e6bb763d22c20916ed4f0bb6bd49d7470cffd8) is a real number, so the translation moves the point  x {\displaystyle \mathbf {x} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbf {x} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/32adf004df5eb0a8c7fd8c0b6b7405183c5a5ef2) to some other point on the ray  Z {\displaystyle \mathbf {Z} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbf {Z} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b776aaf12c2da4b78ca777cb8295c2000bfd51f5). Fixing a point  x ∈ M {\displaystyle x\in M} ![{\\displaystyle x\\in M}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9df57d73e9532bb93a1439890bcddbc2806f5859) instead determines a line  C P 1 {\displaystyle \mathbb {CP} ^{1}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {CP} ^{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/82c98c461bccbd4567716df0a6a2ff97bf9371af) in  P T {\displaystyle \mathbb {PT} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {PT} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a5cb12846bd6e3df92c17bb5683d670b58e0813f) parametrised by ⁠ π A ′ {\displaystyle \pi _{A'}} ![{\\displaystyle \\pi _{A'}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3ec8f6270d547fc610ad0e69a48be1125cc22eaf)⁠. If  x ∈ R {\displaystyle x\in \mathbb {R} } ![{\\displaystyle x\\in \\mathbb {R} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a9c6d458566aec47a7259762034790c8981aefab), then  Z α Z ¯ α {\displaystyle Z^{\alpha }{\bar {Z}}_{\alpha }} ![{\\displaystyle Z^{\\alpha }{\\bar {Z}}_{\\alpha }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7d182da24937b9b894c5b62564bb63ebc8f7f2d) necessarily vanishes and the corresponding complex projective line lies within the five-dimensional space of null twistors  P N {\displaystyle \mathbb {PN} } ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbb {PN} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/31c93c154657b3e22ee666215d1e95ffd9961682). For non-null twistors  Z α Z ¯ α {\displaystyle Z^{\alpha }{\bar {Z}}_{\alpha }} ![{\\displaystyle Z^{\\alpha }{\\bar {Z}}_{\\alpha }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7d182da24937b9b894c5b62564bb63ebc8f7f2d) is non-vanishing, so that there are no real solutions. Non-null twistors have a physical interpretation as massless particles with spin. They are not localized in spacetime, since no ray is singled out which could be regarded as the particle's world-line. 
## Variations
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Twistor_theory&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Variations")]
### Supertwistors
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Twistor_theory&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Supertwistors")]
Supertwistors are a [supersymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersymmetry "Supersymmetry") extension of twistors introduced by Alan Ferber in 1978.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Fer-43) Non-projective twistor space is extended by [fermionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion "Fermion") coordinates where  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd) is the [number of supersymmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_supersymmetry "Extended supersymmetry") so that a twistor is now given by  ( ω A , π A ′ , η i ) , i = 1 , … , N {\displaystyle \left(\omega ^{A},\,\pi _{A'},\,\eta ^{i}\right),i=1,\ldots ,{\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\left\(\\omega ^{A},\\,\\pi _{A'},\\,\\eta ^{i}\\right\),i=1,\\ldots ,{\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/96bffb98c2c4c79d9e60c8232b243274c7f22a0e) with  η i {\displaystyle \eta ^{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\eta ^{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9173d77bff3e3c6a6271c518f629b4442e433c21) anticommuting. The super conformal group SU(2,2 |  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd)) naturally acts on this space and a supersymmetric version of the Penrose transform takes cohomology classes on supertwistor space to massless supersymmetric multiplets on super Minkowski space. The  N {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b7551c7bed2cd2ee83e10536d157c94a5f8f72fd) = 4 case provides the target for Penrose's original twistor string and the  N = 8 {\displaystyle {\mathcal {N}}=8} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {N}}=8}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fed7512847b7119664c945e0c3b955e5d20c494c) case is that for Skinner's supergravity generalisation.

### Palatial twistor theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Twistor_theory&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Palatial twistor theory")]
The nonlinear graviton construction encodes only anti-self-dual, i.e., left-handed fields.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Penrose1976-8) A first step towards the problem of modifying twistor space so as to encode a general gravitational field is the encoding of [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_\(physics\) "Chirality \(physics\)") fields. Infinitesimally, these are encoded in twistor functions or [cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology "Cohomology") classes of [homogeneity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_function "Homogeneous function") −6. The task of using such twistor functions in a fully nonlinear way so as to obtain a _[right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicity_\(particle_physics\) "Helicity \(particle physics\)")_ nonlinear graviton has been referred to as the (**gravitational**) **googly problem**.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-Penrose1000-45) (The word "[googly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googly "Googly")" is a term used in the game of [cricket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cricket "Cricket") for a ball bowled with right-handed helicity using the apparent action that would normally give rise to left-handed helicity.) The most recent proposal in this direction by Penrose in 2015 was based on [noncommutative geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_geometry "Noncommutative geometry") on twistor space and referred to as **palatial twistor theory**.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-46) The theory is named after [Buckingham Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckingham_Palace "Buckingham Palace"), where [Michael Atiyah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Atiyah "Michael Atiyah")[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twistor_theory#cite_note-47) suggested to Penrose the use of a type of "[noncommutative algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noncommutative_algebra "Noncommutative algebra")", an important component of the theory. (The underlying twistor structure in palatial twistor theory was modeled not on the twistor space but on the non-commutative [holomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic "Holomorphic") twistor [quantum algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_group "Quantum group").)
