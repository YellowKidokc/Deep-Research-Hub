Concept in philosophy, religion, rhetoric, and psychology
For other uses, see [Logos (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_\(disambiguation\) "Logos \(disambiguation\)").
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/98/Logos.svg/250px-Logos.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Logos.svg)[Greek spelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet "Greek alphabet") of _logos_
_**Logos**_ ([UK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_English "British English"): [/ˈloʊɡɒs,ˈlɒɡɒs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"), [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_English "American English"): [/ˈloʊɡoʊs/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): [λόγος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BB%CF%8C%CE%B3%CE%BF%CF%82 "wikt:λόγος"), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek "Romanization of Ancient Greek"):  _lógos_ , [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation "Literal translation") 'word, discourse, or reason') is a term used in [Western philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy "Western philosophy"), [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology "Psychology") and [rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric "Rhetoric"), as well as [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion") (notably [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_\(Christianity\) "Logos \(Christianity\)")), that most broadly means reason, logic, order, or understanding. Among its connotations is that of a [rational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationality "Rationality") form of discourse that relies on [inductive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_reasoning "Inductive reasoning") and [deductive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_reasoning "Deductive reasoning") reasoning. 
[Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") first systematized the usage of the word, making it one of the [three principles of rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modes_of_persuasion "Modes of persuasion") alongside [ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos "Ethos") and [pathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathos "Pathos"). This original use identifies the word closely to the structure and content of [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language "Language") or [text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_\(literary_theory\) "Text \(literary theory\)"). Both [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") and [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") used the term _logos_ (along with _[rhema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhema "Rhema")_) to refer to [sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_\(language\) "Sentence \(language\)") and [propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition_\(philosophy\) "Proposition \(philosophy\)"). 
## Background
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Background")]
[Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): [λόγος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BB%CF%8C%CE%B3%CE%BF%CF%82 "wikt:λόγος"), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek "Romanization of Ancient Greek"):  _lógos_ , [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation "Literal translation") 'word, discourse, or reason' is related to Ancient Greek: [λέγω](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BB%CE%AD%CE%B3%CF%89 "wikt:λέγω"), [romanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization_of_Ancient_Greek "Romanization of Ancient Greek"):  _légō_ , [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation "Literal translation") 'I say' which is cognate with [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language"): _lex_ , [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation "Literal translation") 'law'. The word derives from a Proto-Indo-European root, *leǵ-, which can have the meanings "I put in order, arrange, gather, choose, count, reckon, discern, say, speak". In modern usage, it typically connotes the verbs "account", "measure", "reason" or "discourse".[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-LSJlogos-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-perseus.tufts.edu-2) It is occasionally used in other contexts, such as for "ratio" in mathematics.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-3)
## Origins of the term
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Origins of the term")]
_Logos_ became a technical term in [Western philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy "Western philosophy") beginning with [Heraclitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus "Heraclitus") ([c.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/circa#English "wikt:circa") 535 – c. 475 BC), who used the term for a principle of order and knowledge.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-4) [Ancient Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy "Ancient Greek philosophy") used the term in different ways. The [sophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophist "Sophist") used the term to mean "[discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse "Discourse")". [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") applied the term to refer to "reasoned discourse"[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-PaulRahe-5) or "the argument" in the field of rhetoric, and considered it one of the three [modes of persuasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modes_of_persuasion "Modes of persuasion") alongside _[ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos "Ethos")_ and _[pathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathos "Pathos")_.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-6) [Pyrrhonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrrhonism "Pyrrhonism") philosophers used the term to refer to [dogmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma "Dogma") accounts of non-evident matters. The [Stoics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoics "Stoics") spoke of the _[logos spermatikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_spermatikos "Logos spermatikos")_ (the generative principle of the Universe) which foreshadows related concepts in [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism "Neoplatonism").[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-7)
Within [Hellenistic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Judaism "Hellenistic Judaism"), [Philo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo "Philo") (c. 20 BC – c. 50 AD) integrated the term into [Jewish philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_philosophy "Jewish philosophy").[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-cdpPhilo-8) Philo distinguished between _logos prophorikos_ ("the utterer word or speaker"), logos spermatikos ("the speech") and the _logos endiathetos_ ("the word remaining within").[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-9)
The [Gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John "Gospel of John") identifies the [Christian Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_\(Christianity\) "Logos \(Christianity\)"), through which all things are made, as divine ([_theos_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity "God in Christianity")),[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-May_Metzger-10) and further identifies [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_Christ "Jesus Christ") as the _[incarnate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation_\(Christianity\) "Incarnation \(Christianity\)") Logos_. Early translators of the Greek [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament"), such as [Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome "Jerome") (in the 4th century AD), experienced frustration with the inadequacy of any single Latin word to convey the meaning of the word _logos_ as used to describe Jesus Christ in the [Gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John "Gospel of John"). The [Vulgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgate "Vulgate") Bible usage of _in principio erat verbum_ was thus constrained to use the (perhaps inadequate) noun _verbum_ for "word"; later [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_language "Romance language") translations had the advantage of nouns such as _le Verbe_ in French. Reformation translators took another approach. [Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther "Martin Luther") rejected _Zeitwort_ (verb) in favor of _Wort_ (word), for instance, although later commentators repeatedly turned to a more dynamic use involving _the living word_ as used by Jerome and [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo "Augustine of Hippo").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-11) The term is also used in [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism"), and the analytical psychology of [Carl Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung "Carl Jung"). 
Despite the conventional translation as "word", _logos_ is not used for a [word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word "Word") in the grammatical sense—for that, the term _lexis_ ([λέξις](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BB%CE%AD%CE%BE%CE%B9%CF%82 "wikt:λέξις"), _léxis_) was used.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-LSJlexis-12) However, both _logos_ and _lexis_ derive from the same verb _légō_ ([λέγω](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BB%CE%AD%CE%B3%CF%89 "wikt:λέγω")), meaning "(I) count, tell, say, speak".[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-LSJlogos-1)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-LSJlexis-12)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-LSJlego-13)
In the ancient Greek context, the term _logos_ in the sense of "word" or "discourse" also contrasted with _mythos_ ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language "Ancient Greek language"): [μῦθος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CE%BC%E1%BF%A6%CE%B8%CE%BF%CF%82 "wikt:μῦθος")). Classical Greek usage sees reasoned argument (_logos_) as distinct from imaginative tales (_mythos_).[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-14)
## Ancient Greek philosophy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Ancient Greek philosophy")]
Further information: [Heraclitus § Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus#Logos "Heraclitus")
The writing of [Heraclitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus "Heraclitus") ([c.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/circa#English "wikt:circa") 535 – c. 475 BC) was the first place where the word _logos_ was given special attention in [ancient Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy "Ancient Greek philosophy"),[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-15) although Heraclitus seems to use the word with a meaning not significantly different from the way in which it was used in ordinary Greek of his time.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-16) For Heraclitus, _logos_ provided the link between rational discourse and the world's rational structure.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-17)
> This _logos_ holds always but humans always prove unable to ever understand it, both before hearing it and when they have first heard it. For though all things come to be in accordance with this _logos_ , humans are like the inexperienced when they experience such words and deeds as I set out, distinguishing each in accordance with its nature and saying how it is. But other people fail to notice what they do when awake, just as they forget what they do while asleep.
— [Diels–Kranz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diels%E2%80%93Kranz "Diels–Kranz"), 22B1
> For this reason it is necessary to follow what is common. But although the _logos_ is common, most people live as if they had their own private understanding.
— Diels–Kranz, 22B2
> Listening not to me but to the _logos_ it is wise to agree that all things are one.
— Diels–Kranz, 22B50[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-18)
What _logos_ means here is not certain; it may mean "reason" or "explanation" in the sense of an objective cosmic law, or it may signify nothing more than "saying" or "wisdom".[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-19) Yet, an independent existence of a universal _logos_ was clearly suggested by Heraclitus.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-20)

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ae/Aristotle_Altemps_Inv8575.jpg/250px-Aristotle_Altemps_Inv8575.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Aristotle_Altemps_Inv8575.jpg)Aristotle, 384–322 BC
Following one of the other meanings of the word, [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") gave _logos_ a different technical definition in the _[Rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric_\(Aristotle\) "Rhetoric \(Aristotle\)")_ , using it as meaning argument from reason, one of the three [modes of persuasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modes_of_persuasion "Modes of persuasion"). The other two modes are _[pathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathos "Pathos")_ ([πᾰ́θος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%80%CE%AC%CE%B8%CE%BF%CF%82 "wikt:πάθος"), _páthos_), which refers to persuasion by means of emotional appeal, "putting the hearer into a certain frame of mind";[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-AR-21) and _[ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos "Ethos")_ ([ἦθος](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BC%A6%CE%B8%CE%BF%CF%82 "wikt:ἦθος"), _êthos_), persuasion through convincing listeners of one's "moral character".[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-AR-21) According to Aristotle, _logos_ relates to "the speech itself, in so far as it proves or seems to prove".[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-AR-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-22) In the words of Paul Rahe: 
> For Aristotle, _logos_ is something more refined than the capacity to make private feelings public: it enables the human being to perform as no other animal can; it makes it possible for him to perceive and make clear to others through reasoned discourse the difference between what is advantageous and what is harmful, between what is just and what is unjust, and between what is good and what is evil.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-PaulRahe-5)
_Logos_ , _pathos_ , and _ethos_ can all be appropriate at different times.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-23) Arguments from reason (logical arguments) have some advantages, namely that [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data "Data") are (ostensibly) difficult to manipulate, so it is harder to argue against such an argument. On the other hand, trust in the speaker—built through _ethos_ —enhances the appeal of arguments from reason.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
[Robert Wardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wardy "Robert Wardy") suggests that what Aristotle rejects in supporting the use of _logos_ "is not emotional appeal [per se](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_se_\(Latin\) "Per se \(Latin\)"), but rather emotional appeals that have no 'bearing on the issue', in that the _pathē_ [[πᾰ́θη](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%80%CE%AC%CE%B8%CE%B7 "wikt:πάθη"), _páthē_] they stimulate lack, or at any rate are not shown to possess, any intrinsic connection with the point at issue—as if an advocate were to try to whip an [antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitic "Antisemitic") audience into a fury because the accused is Jewish; or as if another in drumming up support for a politician were to exploit his listeners's reverential feelings for the politician's ancestors".[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-24)
Aristotle comments on the three modes by stating: 
> Of the modes of persuasion furnished by the spoken word there are three kinds.  
>  The first kind depends on the personal character of the speaker;  
>  the second on putting the audience into a certain frame of mind;  
>  the third on the proof, or apparent proof, provided by the words of the speech itself. 
— Aristotle, _[Rhetoric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetoric_\(Aristotle\) "Rhetoric \(Aristotle\)")_ , 350 BC[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-25)
[Stoic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoic_philosophy "Stoic philosophy") began with [Zeno of Citium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeno_of_Citium "Zeno of Citium") [c.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/circa#English "wikt:circa") 300 BC, in which the _logos_ was the active [reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reason "Reason") pervading and animating the [Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe "Universe"). It was conceived as material and is usually identified with [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God "God") or [Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature "Nature"). The Stoics also referred to the _seminal logos_ ("_[logos spermatikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_spermatikos "Logos spermatikos")_ "), or the law of generation in the Universe, which was the principle of the active reason working in inanimate [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter "Matter"). Humans, too, each possess a portion of the divine _logos_.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-26)
The Stoics took all activity to imply a _logos_ or spiritual principle. As the operative principle of the world, the _logos_ was _[anima mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_mundi "Anima mundi")_ to them, a concept which later influenced [Philo of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo_of_Alexandria "Philo of Alexandria"), although he derived the contents of the term from Plato.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-27) In his Introduction to the 1964 edition of [Marcus Aurelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius "Marcus Aurelius")' _[Meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditations "Meditations")_ , the Anglican priest Maxwell Staniforth wrote that "_Logos_ ... had long been one of the leading terms of [Stoicism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism "Stoicism"), chosen originally for the purpose of explaining how deity came into relation with the universe".[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-28)
Public discourse on ancient Greek rhetoric has historically emphasized Aristotle's appeals to _logos_ , _pathos_ , and _ethos_ , while less attention has been directed to [Isocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isocrates "Isocrates")' teachings about philosophy and _logos_ ,[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-David_M_2010-29) and their partnership in generating an ethical, mindful _[polis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis "Polis")_. Isocrates does not provide a single definition of _logos_ in his work, but Isocratean _logos_ characteristically focuses on speech, reason, and civic discourse.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-David_M_2010-29) He was concerned with establishing the "common good" of [Athenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Athens "Ancient Athens") citizens, which he believed could be achieved through the pursuit of philosophy and the application of _logos_.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-David_M_2010-29)
## In Hellenistic Judaism
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: In Hellenistic Judaism")]
See also: [Hellenistic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Judaism "Hellenistic Judaism")
### Philo of Alexandria
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Philo of Alexandria")]
[Philo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo "Philo") ([c.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/circa#English "wikt:circa") 20 BC – c. 50 AD), a [Hellenized Jew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenized_Jew "Hellenized Jew"), used the term _logos_ to mean an intermediary divine being or [demiurge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demiurge "Demiurge").[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-cdpPhilo-8) Philo followed the Platonic distinction between imperfect matter and perfect Form, and therefore intermediary beings were necessary to bridge the enormous gap between God and the material world.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-hpPhilo-30) The _logos_ was the highest of these intermediary beings, and was called by Philo "the first-born of God".[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-hpPhilo-30) Philo also wrote that "the Logos of the living God is the bond of everything, holding all things together and binding all the parts, and prevents them from being dissolved and separated".[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-31)
[Plato's Theory of Forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato%27s_Theory_of_Forms "Plato's Theory of Forms") was located within the _logos_ , but the _logos_ also acted on behalf of God in the physical world.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-hpPhilo-30) In particular, the [Angel of the Lord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_of_the_Lord "Angel of the Lord") in the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") ([Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament")) was identified with the _logos_ by Philo, who also said that the _logos_ was God's instrument in the creation of the Universe.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-hpPhilo-30)
### Targums
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Targums")]
The concept of _logos_ also appears in the [Targums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targum "Targum") (Aramaic translations of the Hebrew Bible dating to the first centuries AD), where the term _memra_ ([Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic "Aramaic") for "word") is often used instead of 'the Lord', especially when referring to a manifestation of God that could be construed as [anthropomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphic "Anthropomorphic").[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-32)
## Christianity
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Christianity")]
Further information: [Logos (Christianity)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_\(Christianity\) "Logos \(Christianity\)")
In [Christology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christology "Christology"), the Logos ([Koine Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek_language "Koine Greek language"): Λόγος, [lit.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literal_translation "Literal translation") 'word, discourse, or reason')[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-perseus.tufts.edu-2) is a name or title of [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Christianity "Jesus in Christianity"), seen as the preeminent expression in fulness of all the attributes, the complete thought, and the entire "knowable" reality of the infinite and spiritually transcendent Godhead. This concept is applied to [John 1:1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_1:1 "John 1:1") in the [Douay–Rheims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douay%E2%80%93Rheims "Douay–Rheims") (1582), [King James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version "King James Version") (1604), as well as the [New International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_International_Version "New International Version") and other versions of the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible"), where "logos" is capitalized in translation as "Word"; thereby rendering the verse as: 
> In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-33)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-34)[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-35)

## Neoplatonism
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Neoplatonism")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/Roman_sarcophagus_of_a_reader_identified_to_Plotinus_and_disciples.jpg/250px-Roman_sarcophagus_of_a_reader_identified_to_Plotinus_and_disciples.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Roman_sarcophagus_of_a_reader_identified_to_Plotinus_and_disciples.jpg)[Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus "Plotinus") with his disciples
[Neoplatonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism "Neoplatonism") philosophers such as [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus "Plotinus") ([c.](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/circa#English "wikt:circa") 204/5 – 270 AD) used _logos_ in ways that drew on Plato and the [Stoics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoics "Stoics"),[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-37) but the term _logos_ was interpreted in different ways throughout Neoplatonism, and similarities to Philo's concept of _logos_ appear to be accidental.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-38) The _logos_ was a key element in the [meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditation "Meditation") of Plotinus[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-39) regarded as the first neoplatonist. Plotinus referred back to [Heraclitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus "Heraclitus") and as far back as [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales "Thales")[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-40) in interpreting _logos_ as the principle of meditation, existing as the interrelationship between the [hypostases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostasis_\(philosophy_and_religion\) "Hypostasis \(philosophy and religion\)")—the [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul "Soul"), the intellect (_[nous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous "Nous")_), and the [One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monad_\(philosophy\) "Monad \(philosophy\)").[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-41)
Plotinus used a trinity concept that consisted of "The One", the "Spirit", and "Soul". The comparison with the Christian [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity "Trinity") is inescapable, but for Plotinus these were not equal and "The One" was at the highest level, with the "Soul" at the lowest.[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-42) For Plotinus, the relationship between the three elements of his trinity is conducted by the outpouring of _logos_ from the higher principle, and _[eros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eros_\(concept\) "Eros \(concept\)")_ (loving) upward from the lower principle.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-43) Plotinus relied heavily on the concept of _logos_ , but no explicit references to Christian thought can be found in his works, although there are significant traces of them in his doctrine.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Plotinus specifically avoided using the term _logos_ to refer to the second person of his trinity.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-44) However, Plotinus influenced [Gaius Marius Victorinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Marius_Victorinus "Gaius Marius Victorinus"), who then influenced [Augustine of Hippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo "Augustine of Hippo").[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-45) Centuries later, [Carl Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung "Carl Jung") acknowledged the influence of Plotinus in his use of the term.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-46)
Victorinus differentiated between the _logos_ interior to God and the _logos_ related to the world by creation and [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation "Salvation").[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-47)
Augustine of Hippo, often seen as the father of [medieval philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_philosophy "Medieval philosophy"), was also greatly influenced by Plato and is famous for his re-interpretation of Aristotle and Plato in the light of [early Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christian "Early Christian") thought.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-48) A young [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine "Augustine") experimented with, but failed to achieve [ecstasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecstasy_\(philosophy\) "Ecstasy \(philosophy\)") using the meditations of Plotinus.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-49) In his _[Confessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_\(St._Augustine\) "Confessions \(St. Augustine\)")_ , Augustine described _logos_ as the _Divine Eternal Word_ ,[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-50) by which he, in part, was able to motivate the early Christian thought throughout the [Hellenized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenized "Hellenized") world (of which the Latin speaking West was a part)[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-51) Augustine's _logos_ _had taken body_ in Christ, the man in whom the _logos_ (i.e. _veritas_ or _sapientia_) was present as in no other man.[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-52)
## Islam
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Islam")]
Main article: [Logos (Islam)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_\(Islam\) "Logos \(Islam\)")
The concept of the _logos_ also exists in [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam"), where it was definitively articulated primarily in the writings of the classical [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni "Sunni") [mystics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism") and [Islamic philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy "Islamic philosophy"), as well as by certain [Shi'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi%27a "Shi'a") thinkers, during the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age").[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-53)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-EoI-54) In [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam "Sunni Islam"), the concept of the _logos_ has been given many different names by the denomination's [metaphysicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysician "Metaphysician"), mystics, and philosophers, including _ʿaql_ ("Intellect"), _al-insān al-kāmil_ ("Universal Man"), _kalimat Allāh_ ("Word of God"), _haqīqa muḥammadiyya_ ("The Muhammadan Reality"), and _nūr muḥammadī_ ("The Muhammadan Light"). 
### _ʿAql_
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: ʿAql")]
Main article: ['Aql](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%27Aql "'Aql")
One of the names given to a concept very much like the Christian Logos by the classical Muslim metaphysicians is _ʿaql_ , which is the "Arabic equivalent to the Greek [νοῦς](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous "Nous") (intellect)."[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-EoI-54) In the writings of the Islamic [neoplatonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonist "Neoplatonist") philosophers, such as [al-Farabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Farabi "Al-Farabi") (c. 872 – c. 950 AD) and [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna") (d. 1037),[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-EoI-54) the idea of the _ʿaql_ was presented in a manner that both resembled "the late Greek doctrine" and, likewise, "corresponded in many respects to the Logos Christology."[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-EoI-54)
The concept of _logos_ in Sufism is used to relate the "Uncreated" (God) to the "Created" (humanity). In Sufism, for the Deist, no contact between man and God can be possible without the _logos_. The _logos_ is everywhere and always the same, but its personification is "unique" within each region. [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus") and [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") are seen as the personifications of the _logos_ , and this is what enables them to speak in such absolute terms.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-55)[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-56)
One of the boldest and most radical attempts to reformulate the neoplatonic concepts into Sufism arose with the philosopher [Ibn Arabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Arabi "Ibn Arabi"), who traveled widely in Spain and North Africa. His concepts were expressed in two major works _The Ringstones of Wisdom_ (_Fusus al-Hikam_) and _The Meccan Illuminations_ (_Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya_). To Ibn Arabi, every prophet corresponds to a reality which he called a _logos_ (_[Kalimah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalimah "Kalimah")_), as an aspect of the unique divine being. In his view the divine being would have for ever remained hidden, had it not been for the prophets, with _logos_ providing the link between man and divinity.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-57)
Ibn Arabi seems to have adopted his version of the _logos_ concept from neoplatonic and Christian sources,[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-58) although (writing in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language "Arabic language") rather than Greek) he used more than twenty different terms when discussing it. For Ibn Arabi, the _logos_ or "Universal Man" was a mediating link between individual human beings and the divine essence.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-59)
Other Sufi writers also show the influence of the neoplatonic _logos_.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-60) In the 15th century [Abd al-Karīm al-Jīlī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Kar%C4%ABm_al-J%C4%ABl%C4%AB "Abd al-Karīm al-Jīlī") introduced the _Doctrine of Logos and the Perfect Man_. For al-Jīlī, the "perfect man" (associated with the _logos_ or the [Prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed "Mohammed")) has the power to assume different forms at different times and to appear in different guises.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-61)
In [Ottoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire "Ottoman Empire") Sufism, Şeyh Gâlib (d. 1799) articulates Sühan (_logos_ -_Kalima_) in his _Hüsn ü Aşk_ (_Beauty and Love_) in parallel to Ibn Arabi's Kalima. In the romance, _Sühan_ appears as an embodiment of Kalima as a reference to the Word of God, the Perfect Man, and the Reality of Muhammad.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-62)[_[relevant?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Writing_better_articles#Stay_on_topic "Wikipedia:Writing better articles")_]
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Jung's analytical psychology")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/80/Carl_Jung_%281912%29.png/250px-Carl_Jung_%281912%29.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Carl_Jung_\(1912\).png)A 37-year-old [Carl Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung "Carl Jung") in 1912
[Carl Jung](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Jung "Carl Jung") contrasted the critical and rational faculties of _logos_ with emotional, non-reason oriented and mythical elements.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-63) In Jung's approach, _logos_ vs _eros_ can be represented as "science vs mysticism", or "reason vs imagination" or "conscious activity vs the unconscious".[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-64)
For Jung, _logos_ represented the masculine principle of rationality, in contrast to its feminine counterpart, _[eros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eros_\(concept\) "Eros \(concept\)")_ : 
> Woman’s psychology is founded on the principle of _Eros_ , the great binder and loosener, whereas from ancient times the ruling principle ascribed to man is _Logos_. The concept of _Eros_ could be expressed in modern terms as psychic relatedness, and that of _Logos_ as objective interest.[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-65)
## Rhetoric
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Rhetoric")]
Author and professor Jeanne Fahnestock describes _logos_ as a "premise". She states that, to find the reason behind a [rhetor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetor "Rhetor")'s backing of a certain position or stance, one must acknowledge the different "premises" that the rhetor applies via their chosen diction.[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-members.tripod.com-66) The rhetor's success, she argues, will come down to "certain objects of agreement...between arguer and audience". 
## Rhema
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Logos&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Rhema")]
The word _logos_ has been used in different senses along with _[rhema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhema "Rhema")_. Both [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato "Plato") and [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") used the term _logos_ along with _rhema_ to refer to sentences and propositions.[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-Dinneen-67)[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-68)
The [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint "Septuagint") translation of the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") into Greek uses the terms _rhema_ and _logos_ as equivalents and uses both for the [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language "Hebrew language") word _[dabar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dabar_\(Hebrew_word\) "Dabar \(Hebrew word\)")_ , as the Word of God.[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-69)[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-70)[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-71)
Some modern usage in [Christian theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_theology "Christian theology") distinguishes _rhema_ from _logos_ (which here refers to the written scriptures) while _rhema_ refers to the revelation received by the reader from the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit_in_Christianity "Holy Spirit in Christianity") when the Word (_logos_) is read,[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-Rogers-72)[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-73)[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-74)[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-75) although this distinction has been criticized.[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-Draper-76)[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos#cite_note-JohnMacArthur-77)
