## The Software Update: Why Version 2.0 Doesn't Contradict Version 1.0

44 contradictions resolved by understanding the Old-to-New Covenant transition
———

## The Most Predictable Category

The Bible explicitly teaches that the old covenant would be replaced by a new one. Jeremiah 31:31-34 announces it. Jesus claims to inaugurate it at the Last Supper. The entire book of Hebrews argues it theologically. Paul's letters explain the transition in detail. The Old-to-New Covenant shift is not a hidden assumption scholars impose on the text — it is the text's own central narrative arc.
And yet 44 alleged contradictions — 6.9% of the total — treat differences between Old Covenant regulations and New Covenant teaching as if the Bible never acknowledged or explained the transition. This is like reading a software changelog that says 'Version 2.0 replaces Feature X with Feature Y' and then filing a bug report that Feature X and Feature Y are 'contradictory.'

## The Three Types of Old Testament Law

Traditional Christian theology — not as post-hoc rationalization, but as interpretive framework present from the earliest church — distinguishes three categories of Old Testament law: moral (timeless ethical principles like prohibitions against murder, theft, and adultery), ceremonial (sacrificial system, dietary laws, purity regulations pointing forward to Christ), and civil (specific governance rules for the nation of Israel). The New Covenant explicitly continues the moral law, fulfills and supersedes the ceremonial law, and renders the civil law historically instructive but not directly binding.
Nearly every entry in this category mistakes a ceremonial or civil law change for a contradiction. 'Is circumcision required?' Under Moses, yes — it was the covenant sign. Under the New Covenant, no — the reality it pointed to (heart transformation) has arrived. Paul explains this at length in Galatians and Romans. There is no contradiction between 'circumcision was required' and 'circumcision is no longer required' when the text explicitly narrates and explains the transition.

## Dietary Laws and Sacrificial System

The dietary laws of Leviticus distinguished clean and unclean animals for Israel's consumption. In Acts 10, Peter receives a vision declaring all foods clean — explicitly connecting this to the inclusion of Gentiles in the covenant community. In Mark 7:19, Jesus 'declared all foods clean.' The New Testament does not quietly contradict the Old. It loudly announces the change and explains why.
Similarly, the elaborate sacrificial system of Leviticus is not contradicted by the New Testament's teaching that Christ is the final sacrifice. It is fulfilled by it. The book of Hebrews spends thirteen chapters explaining this precise relationship: the old sacrifices were shadows; the new reality has arrived; therefore the shadows are no longer needed. The critic who presents 'Must we sacrifice animals?' as a contradiction has not read the Bible's own extensive commentary on this exact question.

## Penalties and Punishments

Several entries ask: 'Should adulterers be executed?' The Mosaic law prescribed death for adultery within the theocratic nation of Israel. Jesus, in John 8, redirects from civil penalty to personal repentance. This is not a contradiction but a jurisdictional change: Israel's civil government no longer exists, and the church was never commissioned to execute civil penalties. The moral prohibition remains; the enforcement mechanism changed because the governing structure changed.
The same applies to Sabbath-breaking penalties, blasphemy laws, and other civil regulations. The principle (honor God, keep commitments, maintain holiness) persists. The specific penalty structure belonged to a particular political arrangement that the New Covenant explicitly supersedes.

## National vs. Universal Ethics

Several entries involve how specific foreign nations should be treated. The Old Testament contains specific commands about Ammonites, Moabites, Canaanites, and others — commands rooted in particular historical circumstances (these nations' specific actions toward Israel). The New Testament teaches universal love of neighbor and enemy. The critic sees contradiction; the text presents progressive revelation: God worked through a particular nation with particular enemies, then expanded the scope to encompass all humanity.
Notably, even within the Old Testament, the trajectory toward universalism is visible: Ruth the Moabite is welcomed into the covenant community despite the general prohibition against Moabites. Rahab the Canaanite is saved despite the general command against Canaanites. The Old Testament itself contains the seeds of its own expansion, making the New Testament fulfillment a continuation rather than a reversal.

## The Hermeneutical Bottom Line

Every entry in this category requires ignoring the Bible's own explicit teaching about covenant transition. The critic is welcome to argue that the transition is poorly reasoned, or that the New Testament's reinterpretation of the Old is illegitimate. Those are real theological arguments. But presenting the differences as 'contradictions' when the text itself explains and defends the transition is not intellectual honesty. It is the equivalent of calling a legal amendment a 'contradiction' with the original statute it was designed to modify.
