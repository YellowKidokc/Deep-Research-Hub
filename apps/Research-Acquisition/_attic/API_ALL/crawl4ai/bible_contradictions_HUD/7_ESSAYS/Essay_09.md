## Writing for Different Readers

Matthew wrote primarily for a Jewish audience. He emphasizes Old Testament fulfillment, Davidic kingship, and Jewish customs. Mark wrote for a Roman audience. He emphasizes action, power, and brevity. Luke wrote for a Greek intellectual audience. He emphasizes careful research, historical context, and universal themes. John wrote to supplement the other three. He emphasizes theology, symbolism, and the deity of Christ.
These different audiences explain twelve alleged contradictions where the same event is reported with different emphasis, detail selection, or framing. This is not deception — it is communication. Every effective communicator adapts their message to their audience without changing the underlying truth.

## Representative Cases

How did God address Jesus at his baptism? Matthew reports 'This is my beloved Son' (third person — God speaking about Jesus to the crowd). Mark and Luke report 'You are my beloved Son' (second person — God speaking to Jesus directly). Both happened: God addressed Jesus personally, and the crowd heard the declaration. Matthew, writing for an audience that needs to know who Jesus is, records the public declaration. Mark records the personal address.
Did Jesus ride one animal or two into Jerusalem? Matthew mentions both the donkey and its colt, explicitly connecting the event to Zechariah's prophecy (important for his Jewish audience). Mark mentions only the colt. Jesus rode the colt; the mother donkey walked alongside. Matthew includes both because the prophetic fulfillment matters to his readers. Mark simplifies because the prophetic detail is less relevant to his Roman audience.
Jesus sometimes taught in parables publicly and explained plainly to his disciples privately. When one gospel records a parable and another records plain teaching on the same topic, the critic sees contradiction. The text sees audience adaptation — exactly what any effective teacher does when addressing different groups.

## The Communication Principle

Twelve entries in the database confuse audience-appropriate communication with contradiction. The remedy is simply to recognize that the gospel writers were communicators, not court stenographers. They selected, arranged, and emphasized their material to serve their readers — which is not a flaw in their method but the basic requirement of all effective writing.
