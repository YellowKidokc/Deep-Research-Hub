## The Assumption of Singularity

Twenty-two alleged contradictions assume that because two accounts describe similar events, they must be describing the same event. When the details differ, the critic declares contradiction. But the simpler explanation — that similar events happened more than once — is not only possible but expected across a historical record spanning millennia.

## The Temple Cleansings

John places Jesus' temple cleansing at the beginning of his ministry. The Synoptic Gospels place it at the end. The critic sees one event with two contradictory dates. But nothing prevents Jesus from cleansing the temple twice — once at the beginning of a three-year public ministry and once at the end. Indeed, the theological symbolism is powerful: he began and ended his public ministry by confronting corruption in his Father's house.
The details differ between the accounts in ways consistent with two events: John's account includes details (making a whip, mentioning oxen and sheep) absent from the Synoptics. The Synoptic accounts occur in a different narrative context with different surrounding events. Two similar-but-distinct events explain the data better than one event with irreconcilable chronology.

## The Beersheba Pattern

Abraham named Beersheba after a well-digging dispute (Genesis 21). Isaac named Beersheba after a similar dispute at the same location (Genesis 26). The critic sees one event attributed to two people. But the text explicitly describes two different disputes with two different Philistine leaders generations apart. The same place was named (or re-named) for the same reason by father and son — which is, if anything, a realistic generational pattern.
Jacob named Bethel after his ladder dream (Genesis 28) and again after God appeared to him there (Genesis 35). These are two separate naming events at the same location, years apart, each commemorating a distinct divine encounter. Places can be named more than once.

## Multiple Healings

Jesus healed more than one blind man. He taught in Nazareth more than once. He delivered similar teachings to different audiences at different times (the Sermon on the Mount vs. the Sermon on the Plain may be two separate sermons, or the same core teaching delivered in different settings). When the critic assumes singularity, any difference in detail becomes a contradiction. When you allow for the obvious possibility that an itinerant teacher repeated and adapted his material, the contradictions vanish.

## The Principle

The assumption that similar events must be the same event is not a principle of historical analysis. It is a bias that creates contradictions where none exist. Competent historians ask: 'Could this have happened more than once?' For 22 entries in this database, the answer is clearly yes.
