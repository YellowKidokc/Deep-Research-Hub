## The Translation Gap

Thirty-five alleged contradictions — 5.5% of the database — exist because English words have narrower ranges of meaning than their Hebrew or Greek originals. The 'contradiction' appears in translation but vanishes when you consult the source languages. This is not an obscure academic point. It is the single most important principle for reading any ancient text: the translation is not the text.

## The Hebrew 'Ben' Problem

The Hebrew word 'ben' means son, grandson, great-grandson, descendant, or even member of a class. When Genesis says Abraham had 'one son' (Isaac, the promised heir) and elsewhere lists Ishmael and Keturah's children, the 'contradiction' exists because English 'son' is unambiguous in a way Hebrew 'ben' is not. Abraham had one 'ben' of promise. He had multiple 'ben' biologically. Different senses of the same word.
This word-range issue cascades through genealogies. 'Jehu son of Nimshi' may mean grandson. 'Iddo son of' may skip a generation. English demands precision that Hebrew deliberately leaves flexible, because the genealogical purpose is to establish lineage, not to document every generation.

## The Greek Distinction Collapse

New Testament Greek makes distinctions that English collapses. The word 'love' maps to at least four Greek words: agape (unconditional/divine love), phileo (brotherly affection), storge (familial love), and eros (romantic love). When Jesus asks Peter 'Do you love [agapao] me?' and Peter responds 'You know I love [phileo] you,' the English reader sees repetition. The Greek reader sees a poignant exchange where Peter cannot yet bring himself to claim the highest form of love. No contradiction — but an emotional depth invisible in translation.
The same principle explains the 'burden' passage in Galatians: 'baros' (crushing weight) and 'phortion' (soldier's pack) are both translated 'burden' in some English versions, creating an apparent contradiction that does not exist in Greek. Paul says share the crushing weights and carry your own daily pack. The English word 'burden' covers both; the Greek does not confuse them.

## Name Variants and Titles

Ancient persons routinely bore multiple names. Matthew = Levi. Peter = Simon = Cephas. Jacob = Israel. Jethro = Reuel = Hobab (possibly father, father-in-law, and brother-in-law, or different names for the same person in different traditions). Gideon = Jerub-Baal. The skeptic counts each variant as a 'contradiction.' The historian recognizes that multiple names for the same individual is the norm in the ancient Near East, not the exception.
Throne names vs. birth names explain many apparent discrepancies in the kings of Israel and Judah. Uzziah = Azariah. Jehoiachin = Jeconiah = Coniah. These are not different people or contradictory records — they are the ancient equivalent of 'Her Majesty' vs. 'Elizabeth Windsor.'

## Idiomatic Expressions

Jesus says we must 'hate' our father and mother (Luke 14:26). The Mosaic law commands us to 'honor' father and mother. Contradiction? Only if you ignore that the Hebrew-Aramaic idiom behind 'hate' means 'love less by comparison.' In a culture of absolute loyalty claims, 'hate X' means 'if forced to choose between X and Y, choose Y.' Jesus is not commanding emotional hatred. He is commanding ultimate allegiance to God above family — which the Old Testament itself teaches.
Was Jonah swallowed by a fish or a whale? The Hebrew 'dag gadol' means 'great fish.' The Greek 'ketos' means 'sea creature.' 'Whale' is an English translator's choice. The original languages specify a large marine creature without zoological precision. The 'contradiction' exists entirely in English translation traditions, not in the text.

## The Translation Principle

Every entry in this category resolves by the same method: consult the original language. The contradictions are artifacts of English's more limited word ranges, collapsed distinctions, and cultural unfamiliarity with ancient naming conventions. A serious critic of the Bible would work from the Hebrew and Greek. A critic who builds a case from English translations alone has built a case against the translation, not the text.
