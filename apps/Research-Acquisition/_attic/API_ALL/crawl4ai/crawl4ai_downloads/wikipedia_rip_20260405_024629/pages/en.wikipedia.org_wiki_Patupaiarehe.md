**Patupaiarehe** are supernatural beings (_he[iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi "Iwi") [atua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atua "Atua")_) in [Māori mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_mythology "Māori mythology") that are described as pale to fair skinned with [blonde hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blonde_hair "Blonde hair") or [red hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_hair "Red hair"), usually having the same stature as ordinary people, and never tattooed.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Wikaira-1) They can draw mist to themselves, but tend to be nocturnal or active on misty or foggy days as direct sunlight can be fatal to them.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan-2) They prefer raw food and have an aversion to steam and fire. 
Patupaiarehe can be hostile to humans, especially those who intrude on their lands. They are believed to live in deep forests and hilly or mountainous regions, in large guarded communities,[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Wikaira-1) though their buildings and structures are invisible to human eyes.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Royal-3)
The music of their _[kōauau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C5%8Dauau "Kōauau")_ and _pūtōrino_ (bugle flutes), along with their singing of [waiata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_music#Waiata "Māori music") occasionally reveals their presence on foggy days. The music of the Patupaiarehe is described as 'sweeter' than the music that Māori could play.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-CowanJournal-4) According to most traditions, the [Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people "Māori people") are able to converse with them.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Wikaira-1)
Another little-known term for patupaiarehe was _pakehakeha_ , which has been suggested as a possible origin of the word _[pākehā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81keh%C4%81 "Pākehā")_ , used to refer to [Europeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe "Europe").[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Maori_News-6) It has been theorised that when the first European explorers clashed with [Ngāti Tūmatakōkiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_T%C5%ABmatak%C5%8Dkiri "Ngāti Tūmatakōkiri") (of the _[Kurahaupō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurahaup%C5%8D "Kurahaupō")_) in mid-December 1642, the _[iwi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iwi "Iwi")_ may have interpreted the newcomers as patupaiarehe.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-theprow-8)
## North Island traditions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Patupaiarehe&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: North Island traditions")]
In the North Island, patupaiarehe inhabited mountains such as [Mount Pirongia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Pirongia "Mount Pirongia"), the [Coromandel Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coromandel_Range "Coromandel Range") from [Mount Moehau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moehau_Range "Moehau Range") to Mount [Te Aroha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_Aroha "Te Aroha"), the [Urewera Ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urewera_Ranges "Urewera Ranges"), and the [Waitākere Ranges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wait%C4%81kere_Ranges "Waitākere Ranges").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Royal-3)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Wikaira-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan-2)
According to [Ngāi Tūhoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81i_T%C5%ABhoe "Ngāi Tūhoe") they were small beings, while the [Whanganui Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whanganui_M%C4%81ori "Whanganui Māori") say they were at least two metres tall. Mohi Tūrei of [Ngāti Porou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Porou "Ngāti Porou") described their skin as white, albino, or the colour of red [ochre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochre "Ochre").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Wikaira-1)
In one story, a man named [Kahukura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uenuku#Kahukura "Uenuku") happened across the patupaiarehe pulling in their fishing nets during the night, and offered to help them. When they realised he was a mortal, they fled from him.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Tregear-9)
### Moehau patupaiarehe
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Patupaiarehe&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Moehau patupaiarehe")]
In traditions recounted by the [Ngāti Maru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Maru_\(Hauraki\) "Ngāti Maru \(Hauraki\)") elder Hoani Nahe, the patupaiarehe were inhabitants of the country before Māori arrived. Their _[hapū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hap%C5%AB "Hapū")_ were Ngāti Kura, Ngāti Korakorako, and Ngāti Tūrehu. Their chiefs were Tahurangi, Whanawhana, Nukupori, Tuku, Ripiroaitu, Tapu-te-uru, and Te Rangipouri.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Royal-3)
Part of the reason that patupaiarehe were hostile was said to be because the Māori had driven them from [Mount Moehau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Moehau "Mount Moehau"), where the ancestor [Tamatekapua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tama-te-kapua "Tama-te-kapua") is buried in a cave at the peak of the mountain, which was said to be marked by a [tree fern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_fern "Tree fern"). Mount Moehau was described as their most treasured place.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Grey-10)
In the traditions of the [Hauraki Māori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hauraki_M%C4%81ori "Hauraki Māori"), a patupaiarehe woman named Hinerehia is credited as bringing the knowledge of weaving to the Māori.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-11) Hinerehia hailed from [Moehau Range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moehau_Range "Moehau Range") and fell in love with a Māori man who she met while gathering shellfish on a misty low tide in the [Waitematā Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waitemat%C4%81_Harbour "Waitematā Harbour"). She lived with him at Mōtuihe and they had several children together. Hinerehia wove only at night; frustrated by this, the women of the village asked the [tohunga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tohunga "Tohunga") to trick her into weaving past dawn so that they could learn the skill. The tohunga asked the women to cover the windows from the dawn light and he asked the birds to be silent instead of ushering in the dawn with song as they usually did. In this way they succeeded, but when Hinerehia realised she had been tricked she flew back to Moehau within a cloud, distraught at leaving her husband and children.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-12)
During the time of chief Matatahi, five [Ngāti Rongoū](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Rongo%C5%AB "Ngāti Rongoū") (or Ngāi Rongoi) men went out hunting. After setting out they discovered a [calabash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabash "Calabash") hanging from a [rewarewa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knightia_excelsa "Knightia excelsa") tree, which they cut down and claimed as theirs. They continued walking and eventually found the path to be blocked by [supplejack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripogonum_scandens "Ripogonum scandens"), which had been twisted around so that, while it was still growing, it formed a fence within which the patupaiarehe were growing plants such as [rangiora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brachyglottis_repanda "Brachyglottis repanda"). They continued, caught a pig, and returned to the calabash. One man tried to carry the calabash back but it was so heavy that he almost passed out from the weight; he constantly needed to rest, so they discarded it and continued to their village. The next day when they cooked the pig they had caught, they found nothing in the [hāngī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C4%81ng%C4%AB "Hāngī") but skin and bone once they had opened it. That night, the man who tried to carry the calabash was dragged from his home. He tried to resist by clinging to a couple of trees, but the patupaiarehe were so strong that the trees were pulled out of the ground, and he was carried to the water and drowned. The other four men were also killed.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Grey-10)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Patupaiarehe&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Ngongotahā patupaiarehe")]
According to one Te Matehaere of _[Arawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arawa_\(canoe\) "Arawa \(canoe\)")_ , the peak of [Mount Ngongotahā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ngongotah%C4%81#Mount_Ngongotah%C4%81 "Ngongotahā") was called _Te Tuahu a te Atua_ (“The Altar of the God”), and served as the principal home of the Ngāti Rua tribe of patupaiarehe 600 years ago during the time of [Īhenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%AAhenga "Īhenga"). Their chiefs were Tuehu, Te Rangitamai, Tongakohu, and Rotokohu. They were not an aggressive people, and they were not war-loving.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan2-13)
They were thought to number at least a thousand, and their skin colours ranged from _pākehā_ -white, to the same colour as an ordinary Māori, with most of them being 'reddish'. Their hair had a red or golden tinge, and their eyes were black or, like some _pākehā_ , blue, and they were as tall as any other human. The women were beautiful, described as 'very fair of complexion, with shining fair hair'. Their clothes were _pakerangi_ (flax garments dyed red), and _pora_ and _pureke_ ('rough mats').[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan2-13)
Their diets consisted of forest-food and [whitebait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitebait "Whitebait") caught from [Lake Rotorua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Rotorua "Lake Rotorua"). These patupaiarehe had an aversion to steam, however. Whenever the people living close to a patupaiarehe home (such as at Te Raho-o-te-Rangipiere) opened their _[hāngī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C4%81ng%C4%AB "Hāngī")_ , the patupaiarehe would allegedly lock themselves away to avoid the steam. Where they lived, Te Tuahu a te Atua, was a dry place with no sources of water (possibly as a further precaution against humid conditions), so they had to climb down to the 'northern cliffs, near the side of the Kauae spur', which happened to be the sacred burial place of the [Ngāti Whakaue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81ti_Whakaue "Ngāti Whakaue") _iwi_. They carried the water back to the summit of the mountain inside _tahā_ (gourds, [calabashes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calabash "Calabash")).[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan2-13)
An early _Arawa_ explorer of the [Rotorua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotorua "Rotorua") region, Īhenga, had many encounters with the patupaiarehe who lived at Mount Ngongotahā. When he first ventured into their _[pā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81 "Pā")_ , the patupaiarehe were very inquisitive and wanted to keep him, particularly a beautiful woman patupaiarehe who wanted Īhenga for a husband. Īhenga drank water proffered in a calabash, then, sensing a trap, fled the mountain in hot pursuit, only escaping the patupaiarehe by smearing foul-smelling shark oil on his skin.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan2-13)
## South Island traditions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Patupaiarehe&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: South Island traditions")]
In the [South Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Island "South Island"), patupaiarehe are mostly replaced by the [Maero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maero "Maero"), a different race of supernatural beings, though legends about patupaiarehe are still present. [James Cowan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Cowan_\(New_Zealand_writer\) "James Cowan \(New Zealand writer\)") suggested that if the patupaiarehe did exist they might have been descendants of an early South Island _iwi_ called [Hāwea](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=H%C4%81wea_\(iwi\)&action=edit&redlink=1 "Hāwea \(iwi\) \(page does not exist\)"), whose skin was described as 'ruddy or copper-coloured'.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-CowanJournal-4)
According to [Hōne Taare Tīkao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H%C5%8Dne_Taare_T%C4%ABkao "Hōne Taare Tīkao"), they inhabited the hills of [Banks Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banks_Peninsula "Banks Peninsula") and in the heights above [Lyttelton Harbour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyttelton_Harbour "Lyttelton Harbour") on mountains and ranges such as Poho-o-Tamatea immediately behind [Rāpaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Te_R%C4%81paki-o-Te_Rakiwhakaputa "Te Rāpaki-o-Te Rakiwhakaputa") Bay; Te Pohue, between [Purau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purau "Purau") and [Port Levy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Port_Levy "Port Levy"); Hukuika Peak, between Pigeon Bay and [Little River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_River,_New_Zealand "Little River, New Zealand"); Te U-kura near Hilltop, Banks Peninsula; and the high, rocky peaks of French Hill; O-te-hore, above French Farm; O-te-patatu (Purple Peak), Tara-te-rehu, and Otaki, all which overlook [Akaroa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akaroa "Akaroa"), and lastly Tuhiraki (Mt Bossu).[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-CowanJournal-4) Other places which they lived included the hills between [Lake Brunner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Brunner "Lake Brunner") and the [Arahura River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arahura_River "Arahura River"),[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Wikaira-1) and the mountains around [Lake Wakatipu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Wakatipu "Lake Wakatipu").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan15-14)
[Kāi Tahu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C4%81i_Tahu "Ngāi Tahu") tradition, as recounted by Tikao, states that patupaiarehe drove the _[titi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sooty_shearwater "Sooty shearwater")_ to extinction on O-te-patatu due to [overexploitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overexploitation "Overexploitation"). A Kāi Tahu-[Kāti Māmoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C4%81ti_M%C4%81moe "Kāti Māmoe") woman of the area was said to have a lover who was patupaiarehe, and after the birds were driven away she chanted a waiata pleading that the birds return so that the spirit-people come back to the mountain peak and play their flutes.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-CowanJournal-4)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Patupaiarehe&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Kaiheraki of Tākitimu")]
In a story recounted from Hone Te Paina of Oraka, [Foveaux Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foveaux_Strait "Foveaux Strait");[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-CowanJournal-4) on the [Tākitimu Mountains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takitimu_Mountains "Takitimu Mountains") there haunts a woman patupaiarehe named Kaiheraki, who appears as a spectrelike giantess striding along the mountaintops on misty days.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan14-15)
Kaiheraki's story begins with a mortal man named Hautapu who was a skilled hunter and tohunga. While out hunting with his dog (_[kurī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kur%C4%AB "Kurī")_), Hautapu heard a 'sharp metallic noise' which sounded like two pieces of [pounamu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pounamu "Pounamu") being hit together. At first he suspected it to be a [takahē](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takah%C4%93 "Takahē"), but just as he was about to go and investigate, he noticed a pair of dark gleaming eyes against a pale-white face staring back at him from behind a bush, with just 'a glint of coppery hair'. The appearance was startling, but Hautapu ran into the thickets nonetheless. The face turned out to belong to a crouched woman who had been too astonished and too terrified to flee from the hunter. Hautapu took her by her exposed shoulder and brought her out into the sunlight, and right then and there claimed her as his wife on account of her appearance; tall, young, fair-skinned, full and round 'erect breasts', broad vigorous hips, 'generous curves', sturdy limbs, and flowing thick hair which 'shone with a ruddy bronze tint in the sun.' Her garment was a waistmat made from the leaves of _[Cordyline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cordyline "Cordyline")_.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan14-15)
When asked who she was and where she had come from, she replied with “Kaiheraki is my name. I have no people, I come of no race, and I know no one. My home is yonder, [the Tākitimu Mountains].” and further “I am a Māori, yet not a Māori. I know many tongues; I know the tongues of the birds. I am the child of the mountain; Tākitimu is my mother.” Hautapu immediately recognised that she was a patupaiarehe, and though he wanted her as his wife, he as a tohunga knew that her supernatural nature might forever bind him to the mountain. He decided to perform a ritual to free her from her status as such a being, and set about to prepare a ritual involving fire. Kaiheraki gasped in amazement at the sight of the smoke and fire once it had actually been started, but a tiny spark of the flame jumped onto her bare foot and immediately spurt forth a thin stream of blood. She made an attempt to run from this but Hautapu quickly caught her, bringing her back to continue the fire, only for her to quickly dart away in an instant that he stopped paying attention to her. After that, he never saw her again.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patupaiarehe#cite_note-Cowan14-15)
