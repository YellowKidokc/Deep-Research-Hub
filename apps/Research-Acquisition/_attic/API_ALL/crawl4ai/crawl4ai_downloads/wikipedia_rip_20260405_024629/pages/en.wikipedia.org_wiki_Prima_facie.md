From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Latin expression meaning "at first sight"
For the play by Suzie Miller, see [_Prima Facie_ (play)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_Facie_\(play\) "Prima Facie \(play\)"). For the film adaptation of the play, see [_Prima Facie_ (film)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_Facie_\(film\) "Prima Facie \(film\)").
_**Prima facie**_ ([/ˌpraɪməˈfeɪʃi,ʃə,ʃiiː/](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English "Help:IPA/English"); from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language") _prīmā faciē_) is a [Latin expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Latin_phrases "List of Latin phrases") meaning "at first sight",[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-1) or "based on first impression".[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-reamer-2) The literal translation would be "at first face" or "at first appearance", from the feminine forms of _[primus](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/primus "wikt:primus")_ ("first") and _[facies](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/facies "wikt:facies")_ ("face"), both in the [ablative case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ablative_\(Latin\) "Ablative \(Latin\)"). In modern, colloquial, and conversational English, a common translation would be "on the face of it". 
The term _prima facie_ is used in modern [legal English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_English "Legal English") (including both [civil law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_law_\(common_law\) "Civil law \(common law\)") and [criminal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_law "Criminal law")) to signify that upon initial examination, sufficient corroborating [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence "Evidence") appears to exist to support a case. In [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law "Common law") jurisdictions, a reference to _prima facie evidence_ denotes evidence that, unless [rebutted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebuttal "Rebuttal"), would be sufficient to prove a particular proposition or fact.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-herlitz-3) The term is used similarly in academic [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-reamer-2) Most legal proceedings, in most [jurisdictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction "Jurisdiction"), require a _prima facie_ case to exist, following which proceedings may then commence to test it, and create a ruling.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-herlitz-3)
The similar _**ex facie**_ , [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") for "on the face [of it]," is a legal term typically used to note that a document's explicit terms are defective without further investigation. For example, a contract between two parties would be void _ex facie_ if, under a legal system where it was a binding requirement for validity, the document did not require party A to give [consideration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consideration "Consideration") to party B for services rendered. 
## Burden of proof
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Prima_facie&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Burden of proof")]
Main article: [Burden of proof (law)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burden_of_proof_\(law\) "Burden of proof \(law\)")
In most legal proceedings, one [party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party_\(law\) "Party \(law\)") has a burden of proof, which requires it to present _prima facie_ evidence for all of the essential facts in its case. If it cannot, its claim may be dismissed without any need for a response by other parties.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-4) A _prima facie_ case might not stand or fall on its own; if an opposing party introduces other evidence or asserts an [affirmative defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affirmative_defense "Affirmative defense"), it can be reconciled only with a full [trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trial "Trial"). Sometimes the introduction of _prima facie_ evidence is informally called _making a case_ or _building a case_. For example, in a trial under [criminal law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_law "Criminal law"), the [prosecution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosecution "Prosecution") has the burden of presenting _prima facie_ evidence of each element of the crime charged against the [defendant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defendant "Defendant"). In a [murder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder "Murder") case, this would include evidence that the victim was in fact dead, that the defendant's act caused the death, and that the defendant acted with [malice aforethought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malice_aforethought "Malice aforethought"). If no party introduces new evidence, the case stands or falls just by the _prima facie_ evidence or lack thereof, respectively. 
_Prima facie_ evidence does not need to be conclusive or irrefutable: at this stage, evidence rebutting the case is not considered, only whether any party's case has enough merit to take it to a full trial. In [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law "Common law") jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom and the United States, the prosecution in a criminal trial must disclose all evidence to the defense. This includes the _prima facie_ evidence. An aim of the doctrine of _prima facie_ is to prevent litigants from bringing spurious charges which simply waste all other parties' time. 
## _Res ipsa loquitur_
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Prima_facie&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Res ipsa loquitur")]
_Prima facie_ is often confused with _[res ipsa loquitur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Res_ipsa_loquitur "Res ipsa loquitur")_ ('the thing speaks for itself', or literally 'the thing itself speaks'), the common law doctrine that when the facts make it self-evident that negligence or other responsibility lies with a party, it is not necessary to provide extraneous details, since any reasonable person would immediately find the facts of the case. The difference between the two is that _prima facie_ is a term meaning there is enough evidence for there to be a case to answer, while _res ipsa loquitur_ means that the facts are so obvious a party does not need to explain any more. For example: "There is a _prima facie_ case that the defendant is liable. They controlled the pump. The pump was left on and flooded the [plaintiff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaintiff "Plaintiff")'s house. The plaintiff was away and had left the house in the control of the defendant. _Res ipsa loquitur_." In Canadian tort law, this doctrine has been subsumed by general negligence law.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-5)
## Use in academic philosophy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Prima_facie&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Use in academic philosophy")]

The phrase is also used in academic [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"). Among its most notable uses is in the theory of [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics") first proposed by [W. D. Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._D._Ross "W. D. Ross") in his 1930 book _[The Right and the Good](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Right_and_the_Good "The Right and the Good")_ , often called the _Ethic of Prima Facie Duties_ , as well as in [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology"), as used, for example, by [Robert Audi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Audi "Robert Audi"). It is generally used in reference to an obligation. "I have a _prima facie_ obligation to keep my promise and meet my friend" means that I am under an obligation, but this may yield to a more pressing duty. A more modern usage prefers the title _[pro tanto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_tanto "Pro tanto") obligation_: an obligation that may be later overruled by another more pressing one; it exists only _[pro tempore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pro_tempore "Pro tempore")_.

The phrase _prima facie_ is sometimes misspelled _prima facia_ in the mistaken belief that _facia_ is the actual Latin word; however, _faciē_ is in fact the [ablative case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ablative_\(Latin\) "Ablative \(Latin\)") of _faciēs_ , a [fifth declension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_declension#Fifth_declension_\(e_stems\) "Latin declension") Latin noun. In [policy debate theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy_debate#Theory "Policy debate"), _prima facie_ is used to describe the mandates or planks of an affirmative case, or, in some rare cases, a negative [counterplan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterplan "Counterplan"). When the negative team appeals to _prima facie_ , it appeals to the fact that the affirmative team cannot add or amend anything in its plan after being stated in the first affirmative constructive. 
A common usage of the phrase is the concept of a "_prima facie_ speed limit", which has been used in Australia and the United States. A _prima facie_ speed limit is a default speed limit that applies when no other specific speed limit is posted, and may be exceeded by a driver;[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_facie#cite_note-6) however, if the driver is detected, and cited by police for exceeding the limit, the onus of proof is on the driver to show that the speed at which the driver was travelling was safe under the circumstances. In most jurisdictions, this type of speed limit has been replaced by absolute speed limits.
