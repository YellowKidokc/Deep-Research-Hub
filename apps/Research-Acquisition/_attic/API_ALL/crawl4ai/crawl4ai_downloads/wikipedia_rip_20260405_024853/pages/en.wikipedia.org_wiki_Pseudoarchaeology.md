Scientifically insubstantial theories interpreting archaeology
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0a/Erich_von_Daniken.jpg/120px-Erich_von_Daniken.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Erich_von_Daniken.jpg)
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1c/Graham-Hancock.jpg/120px-Graham-Hancock.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Graham-Hancock.jpg)
[Erich von Däniken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_von_D%C3%A4niken "Erich von Däniken") (left) and [Graham Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Hancock "Graham Hancock") (right) are two of the most widely published proponents of pseudoarchaeological opinions.
**Pseudoarchaeology** (sometimes called **fringe archaeology** and previously also called **alternative archaeology**) consists of attempts to study, interpret, or teach about the subject-matter of [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology "Archaeology") while rejecting, ignoring, or misunderstanding the accepted [data-gathering and analytical methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method") of the discipline.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan720-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-3) These [pseudoscientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience") interpretations involve the use of artifacts, sites or materials to construct scientifically insubstantial theories to strengthen the pseudoarchaeologists' claims. Methods include exaggeration of evidence, dramatic or romanticized conclusions, use of [fallacious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacy "Fallacy") arguments, and fabrication of evidence. 
There is no unified pseudoarchaeological theory or method, but rather many different interpretations of the past which are jointly at odds with those developed by the scientific community as well as with each other. These include religious philosophies such as [creationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creationism "Creationism") or "[creation science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_science "Creation science")" that apply to the archaeology of historic periods such as those that would have included the supposed worldwide [flood myth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_myth "Flood myth"), the [Genesis flood narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesis_flood_narrative "Genesis flood narrative"), [Nephilim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nephilim "Nephilim"), [Noah's Ark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah%27s_Ark "Noah's Ark"), and the [Tower of Babel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_Babel "Tower of Babel"). Some pseudoarchaeological theories concern the idea that prehistoric and ancient human societies were aided in their development by [intelligent extraterrestrial life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_astronauts "Ancient astronauts"), an idea propagated by those such as Italian author [Peter Kolosimo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Kolosimo "Peter Kolosimo"), French authors [Louis Pauwels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Pauwels "Louis Pauwels") and [Jacques Bergier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Bergier "Jacques Bergier") in _[The Morning of the Magicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Morning_of_the_Magicians "The Morning of the Magicians")_ (1963), and Swiss author [Erich von Däniken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_von_D%C3%A4niken "Erich von Däniken") in _[Chariots of the Gods?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chariots_of_the_Gods%3F "Chariots of the Gods?")_ (1968). Others instead argue there were human societies in the ancient period which were significantly technologically advanced, such as [Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis "Atlantis"), and this idea has been propagated by some people such as [Graham Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Hancock "Graham Hancock") in his publication _[Fingerprints of the Gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerprints_of_the_Gods "Fingerprints of the Gods")_ (1995). Pseudoarchaeology has also been manifest in [Mayanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayanism "Mayanism") and the [2012 phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_phenomenon "2012 phenomenon"). 
Many pseudoarchaeological theories are intimately linked with the [occult/Western esoteric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_esotericism "Western esotericism") tradition.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:12-4) Many alternative archaeologies have been adopted by religious groups. Fringe archaeological ideas such as [archaeocryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeocryptography "Archaeocryptography") and [pyramidology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramidology "Pyramidology") have been endorsed by religions ranging from the [British Israelites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Israelites "British Israelites") to the [theosophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophy_\(Blavatskian\) "Theosophy \(Blavatskian\)"). Other alternative archaeologies include those that have been adopted by members of [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age "New Age") and [contemporary pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neopaganism "Neopaganism") belief systems. [_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Academic archaeologists have often criticised pseudoarchaeology, with one of the major critics, John R. Cole, characterising it as relying on "[sensationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensationalism "Sensationalism"), misuse of logic and evidence, misunderstanding of scientific method, and internal contradictions in their arguments".[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Cole_1980._p._02-5) The relationship between alternative and academic archaeologies has been compared to the relationship between [intelligent design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_design "Intelligent design") theories and [evolutionary biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_biology "Evolutionary biology") by some archaeologists.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan721-6)
## Etymology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Etymology")]
Various terms have been employed to refer to these non-academic interpretations of archaeology. During the 1980s, the term "cult archaeology" was used by some people such as John R. Cole (1980)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-7) and William H. Stiebing Jr. (1987).[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-8) "Fantastic archaeology" was used during the 1980s as the name of an undergraduate course at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University") taught by Stephen Williams, who published a book with the same title.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-9) During the 2000s, the term "alternative archaeology" began to be instead applied by academics like Tim Sebastion (2001),[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-10) Robert J. Wallis (2003),[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-11) Cornelius Holtorf (2006),[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-12) and Gabriel Moshenka (2008).[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-13) Garrett F. Fagan and [Kenneth Feder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Feder "Kenneth Feder") (2006) however claimed this term was only chosen because it "imparts a warmer, fuzzier feel" that "appeals to our higher ideals and progressive inclinations".[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan720-2) They argued that the term "pseudoarchaeology" was much more appropriate,[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan720-2) a term also used by other prominent academic and professional archaeologists such as [Colin Renfrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Renfrew "Colin Renfrew") (2006).[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-14)
Other academic archaeologists have chosen to use other terms to refer to these interpretations. [Glyn Daniel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyn_Daniel "Glyn Daniel"), the editor of _[Antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiquity_\(journal\) "Antiquity \(journal\)")_ , used the derogative term "bullshit archaeology",[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan720-2) and similarly the academic William H. Stiebing Jr. noted that there were certain terms used for pseudoarchaeology that were heard "in the privacy of professional archaeologists' homes and offices but which cannot be mentioned in polite society".[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-15)
## Description
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Description")]
Pseudoarchaeology can be practised intentionally or unintentionally. [Archaeological frauds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_forgery "Archaeological forgery") and [hoaxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoax "Hoax") are considered _intentional pseudoarchaeology_. Genuine archaeological finds may become the subject of unintentional pseudoarchaeology by persons unwittingly employing flawed interpretive methods. 
The difficulty of dating clay tablets has enabled widespread archaeological forgery and associated fraud, especially in the Middle East. "By 1904, during the early period of cuneiform tablet collecting, J. Edgar Banks, a Mesopotamian explorer and tablet dealer, estimated that nearly 80% of tablets offered for sale in Baghdad were fakes. In 2016, Syria's Director General for Antiquities and Museums reported that approximately 70% of seized artefacts in the country are fakes."[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-16)
Especially in the past, but also in the present, pseudoarchaeology has been affected by racism, which can be suggested by [confirmation-bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmation_bias "Confirmation bias")-influenced attempts to attribute ancient indigenous cultural sites and artefacts to non-indigenous creators such as ancient Egyptians, Hebrew [Lost Tribes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ten_Lost_Tribes "Ten Lost Tribes"), civilizations that allegedly succeeded in [Pre-Columbian trans-oceanic contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian_trans-oceanic_contact "Pre-Columbian trans-oceanic contact"), or even [extraterrestrial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_intelligence "Extraterrestrial intelligence"). 
Practitioners of pseudoarchaeology often criticise academic archaeologists and established scientific methods, claiming that practitioners of those methods have ignored or even [conspired to suppress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_theory "Conspiracy theory") critical evidence. 
Cornelius Holtorf states that countering the misleading "discoveries" of pseudoarchaeology creates a dilemma for archaeologists:[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-17) whether to attempt to disprove pseudoarchaeology by "crusading" methods[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] or to concentrate on better public knowledge of the sciences involved. Holtorf suggested a third method[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-18) involving identifying the social and cultural demands that both scientific archaeology and pseudoarchaeology address, and identifying the engagement of present people with the [material remains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_culture "Material culture") of the past (such as [Barbara Bender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Bender "Barbara Bender") explored for [Stonehenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stonehenge "Stonehenge")).[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-19) Holtorf presents the search for truth as a process rather than a result and states that "even non-scientific research contributes to enriching our landscapes."[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-20)
## Characteristics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Characteristics")]
William H. Stiebing Jr. argued that despite their many differences, there were a set of common characteristics shared by almost all pseudoarchaeological interpretations. He believed that because of this, pseudoarchaeology could be categorised as a "single phenomenon". He then identified three main commonalities of pseudeoarchaeological theories: the unscientific nature of its method and evidence, its history of providing "simple, compact answers to complex, difficult issues", and its tendency to present itself as being persecuted by the archaeological establishment, accompanied by an ambivalent attitude towards the scientific ethos of the [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment "Age of Enlightenment").[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-ReferenceA-21) This idea that there are common characteristics of pseudoarchaeologies is shared by other academics.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-22)
### Lack of scientific method
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Lack of scientific method")]
Academic critics have stated that pseudoarchaeologists typically neglect to use the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method").[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-23) Instead of testing evidence to see what hypotheses it satisfies best, pseudoarchaeologists selectively interpret, otherwise distort, or even outright falsify archaeological data to fit a "favored conclusion" that is often arrived at through hunches, intuition, or religious or nationalist dogma.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-24)[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-25) Pseudoarchaeological groups have a variety of basic assumptions that are typically unscientific: the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi "Nazi") pseudoarchaeologists, for instance, invoked the claimed cultural superiority of the ancient [Aryan race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryan_race "Aryan race") as a basis for their theories and for their rejection of those theories' rivals, whilst Christian [fundamentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamentalist "Fundamentalist") pseudoarchaeologists conceive of the Earth as being less than 10,000 years old and [Hindu fundamentalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_fundamentalist "Hindu fundamentalist") pseudoarchaeologists believe that the species _[Homo sapiens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homo_sapiens "Homo sapiens")_ is much older than the 200,000 years old it has been shown to be by archaeologists.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-26) Despite this, many of pseudoarchaeology's proponents claim that they gained their conclusions using scientific techniques and methods even when it is demonstrable that they have not.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-27)[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-28)
Academic archaeologist John R. Cole believed that most pseudoarchaeologists do not understand how scientific investigation works and that they instead believe it to be a "simple, catastrophic right versus wrong battle" between contesting theories.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Cole_1980._p._03-29) It was because of this failure to understand the scientific method, he argued, that pseudoarchaeological arguments were faulty. He went on to argue that most pseudoarchaeologists do not consider alternatives to whatever explanation they wish to propagate and that their "theories" were typically just "notions", not having sufficient evidence to allow them to be considered "theories" in the scientific, academic meaning of the word.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-30)
When pseudoarchaeologists lack scientific evidence, they commonly invoke other types of justifications for their arguments. For instance, they often use "generalized cultural comparisons", using various monuments and other artefacts from one society, and emphasizing similarities with those of another society to conclude that the two artefacts had a common cultural origin, typically an ancient lost civilisation like [Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis "Atlantis"), [Mu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu_\(lost_continent\) "Mu \(lost continent\)"), or an extraterrestrial influence.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-ReferenceA-21) Such comparisons involve examinations of the respective artefacts or monuments entirely outside their original contexts, a practice anathema to academic archaeologists, for whom context is of the utmost importance.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan26-31)
Another type of evidence used by a number of pseudoarchaeologists is the interpretation of various [myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mythology "Mythology") as representing historical events, but in doing so these myths are often taken out of their cultural contexts.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-ReferenceB-32) For instance, pseudoarchaeologist [Immanuel Velikovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Velikovsky "Immanuel Velikovsky") claimed that the myths of migrations and war gods in the Central American [Aztec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec "Aztec") civilisation represented a cosmic catastrophe that occurred during the 7th and 8th centuries BCE. This was criticised by academic archaeologist William H. Stiebing Jr., who noted that such myths only developed during the 12th to the 14th centuries CE, two millennia after Velikovsky claimed that the events had occurred, and that the Aztec society itself had not even developed by the 7th century BCE.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-ReferenceB-32)
### Opposition to the archaeological establishment
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Opposition to the archaeological establishment")]
> [Academics] have formed a massive and global network through universities, museums, institutes, societies and foundations. And this immense powerhouse and clearing-house of knowledge has presented their dogma of history to the general public totally unhindered and unchallenged from the outside. ... On a more sinister note: now this "church of science" has formed a network of watchdog organisations such as [CSICOP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Committee_for_Skeptical_Inquiry "Committee for Skeptical Inquiry") and [The Skeptical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Skeptics_Society "The Skeptics Society") [_[sic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sic "Sic")_] (to name but a few) in order to act as the gatekeepers of the truth (as they see it), ready to come down like the proverbial ton of bricks on all those whom they perceive as "frauds", "charlatans", and "pseudo-scientists"—in short, heretics. 
Pseudoarchaeologist Robert Bauval on his views of academia (2000)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-33)
Pseudoarchaeologists typically present themselves as being disadvantaged with respect to the much larger archaeological establishment.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Cole_1980._p._02-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan721-6)[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-ReferenceA-21) They often use language that disparages academics and dismisses them as being unadventurous, spending all their time in dusty libraries and refusing to challenge the orthodoxies of the establishment lest they lose their jobs. In some more extreme examples, pseudoarchaeologists have accused academic archaeologists of being members of a widespread [conspiracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_theory "Conspiracy theory") to hide the truth about history from the public.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-34) When academics challenge pseudoarchaeologists and criticise their theories, many pseudoarchaeologists claim it as further evidence that their own ideas are right, and that they are simply being harassed by members of this academic conspiracy.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-35)
The prominent English archaeologist [Colin Renfrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Renfrew "Colin Renfrew") admitted that the archaeological establishment was often "set in its ways and resistant to radical new ideas" but that this was not the reason why pseudoarchaeological theories were rejected by academics.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Renfrew_2006._p._xii-36) [Garrett G. Fagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_G._Fagan "Garrett G. Fagan") expanded on this, noting how in the academic archaeological community, "New evidence or arguments have to be thoroughly scrutinised to secure their validity ... and longstanding, well-entrenched positions will take considerable effort and particularly compelling data to overturn." Fagan noted that pseudoarchaeological theories simply do not have sufficient evidence to allow them to be accepted by professional archaeologists.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan26-31)
Conversely, many pseudoarchaeologists, whilst criticising the academic archaeological establishment, also attempt to get endorsements from people with academic credentials and affiliations.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-37) At times, they quote historical, and in most cases dead academics to strengthen their arguments; for instance prominent pseudoarchaeologist [Graham Hancock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Hancock "Graham Hancock"), in his seminal _[Fingerprints of the Gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerprints_of_the_Gods "Fingerprints of the Gods")_ (1995), repeatedly notes that the eminent physicist [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") once commented positively on the [pole shift hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_shift_hypothesis "Pole shift hypothesis"), a theory that has been abandoned by the academic community but which Hancock endorses. As Fagan noted however, the fact that Einstein was a physicist and not a geologist is not even mentioned by Hancock, nor is the fact that the present understanding of [plate tectonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plate_tectonics "Plate tectonics") (which came to disprove earth crustal displacement) only became accepted generally after Einstein's death.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-38)
## Academic archaeological responses
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Academic archaeological responses")]
Pseudoarchaeological theories have come to be much criticised by academic and professional archaeologists. One of the first books to address these directly was by archaeologist [Robert Wauchope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wauchope_\(archaeologist\) "Robert Wauchope \(archaeologist\)") of [Tulane University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulane_University "Tulane University").[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-39) Prominent academic archaeologist [Colin Renfrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_Renfrew "Colin Renfrew") stated his opinion that it was appalling that pseudoarchaeologists treated archaeological evidence in such a "frivolous and self-serving way", something he believed trivialised the "serious matter" of the study of human origins.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-40) Academics like John R. Cole,[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Cole_1980._p._02-5) [Garrett G. Fagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_G._Fagan "Garrett G. Fagan") and Kenneth L. Feder[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan720-2) have argued that pseudoarchaeological interpretations of the past were based upon sensationalism, self-contradiction, fallacious logic, manufactured or misinterpreted evidence, quotes taken out of context and incorrect information. Fagan and Feder characterised such interpretations of the past as being "anti-reason and anti-science" with some being "hyper-nationalistic, racist and hateful".[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan720-2) In turn, many pseudoarchaeologists have dismissed academics as being closed-minded and not willing to consider theories other than their own.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Cole_1980._p._02-5)
Many academic archaeologists have argued that the spread of alternative archaeological theories is a threat to the general public's understanding of the past. Fagan was particularly scathing of television shows that presented pseudoarchaeological theories to the general public, believing that they did so because of the difficulties in making academic archaeological ideas comprehensible and interesting to the average viewer.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-41) Renfrew however believed that those television executives commissioning these documentaries knew that they were erroneous, and that they had allowed them to be made and broadcast simply for the hope of "short-term financial gain".[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Renfrew_2006._p._xii-36)
Fagan and Feder believed that it was not possible for academic archaeologists to successfully engage with pseudoarchaeologists, remarking that "you cannot reason with unreason". Speaking from their own experiences, they thought that attempted dialogues just became "slanging matches in which the expertise and motives of the critic become the main focus of attention."[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-fagan721-6) Fagan has maintained this idea elsewhere, remarking that arguing with supporters of pseudoarchaeological theories was "pointless" because they denied logic. He noted that they included those "who openly admitted to not having read a word written by a trained Egyptologist" but who at the same time "were pronouncing how academic Egyptology was all wrong, even sinister."[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Fagan_2006a._p._xvii-42)
### Conferences and anthologies
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Conferences and anthologies")]
At the 1986 meeting of the [Society for American Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_American_Archaeology "Society for American Archaeology"), its organizers, [Kenneth Feder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenneth_Feder "Kenneth Feder"), Luanne Hudson and Francis Harrold decided to hold a symposium to examine pseudoarchaeological beliefs from a variety of academic standpoints, including archaeology, physical anthropology, sociology, history and psychology.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-43) From this symposium, an anthology was produced, entitled _Cult Archaeology & Creationism: Understanding Pseudoarchaeological Beliefs about the Past_ (1987). 
At the 2002 annual meeting of the [Archaeological Institute of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeological_Institute_of_America "Archaeological Institute of America"), a workshop was held on the topic of pseudoarchaeology. It subsequently resulted in the publication of an academic anthology, _Archaeological Fantasies: How Pseudoarchaeology Misrepresents the Past and Misleads the Public_ (2006), which was edited by [Garrett G. Fagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_G._Fagan "Garrett G. Fagan").[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Fagan_2006a._p._xvii-42)
On 23 and 24 April 2009, The [American Schools of Oriental Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Schools_of_Oriental_Research "American Schools of Oriental Research") and the [Duke University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_University "Duke University") Center for Jewish Studies, along with the Duke Department of Religion, the Duke Graduate Program in Religion, the [Trinity College of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_of_Arts_and_Sciences "Trinity College of Arts and Sciences") Committee on Faculty Research, and the [John Hope Franklin Humanities Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hope_Franklin_Humanities_Institute "John Hope Franklin Humanities Institute"), sponsored a conference entitled "Archaeology, Politics, and the Media," which addressed the abuse of archaeology in the [Holy Land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Land "Holy Land") for political, religious, and ideological purposes. Emphasis was placed on the media's reporting of sensational and politically motivated archaeological claims and the academy's responsibility in responding to it.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-44)[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-45)[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-46)
### Inclusive attitudes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Inclusive attitudes")]
Academic archaeologist Cornelius Holtorf believed however that critics of alternative archaeologies like Fagan were "opinionated and patronizing" towards alternative theories, and that purporting their opinions in such a manner was damaging to the public's perception of archaeologists.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-47) Holtorf emphasized that there were similarities between academic and alternative archaeological interpretations, with the former being influenced by the latter. As evidence, he emphasized [archaeoastronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeoastronomy "Archaeoastronomy"), which was once considered as a component of fringe archaeological interpretations before being adopted by mainstream academics.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Holtorf_2005._p._547-48) He also noted that certain archaeological scholars, like [William Stukeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Stukeley "William Stukeley") (1687–1765), [Margaret Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Murray "Margaret Murray") (1863–1963) and [Marija Gimbutas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marija_Gimbutas "Marija Gimbutas") (1921–1994) were formerly considered to be eminent by both academic and alternative archaeologists.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Holtorf_2005._p._547-48) He came to the conclusion that a constructive dialogue should be begun between academic and alternative archaeologists.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-49) Fagan and Feder have responded to Holtorf's statements in detail, asserting that such a dialogue is no more possible than is one between evolutionary biologists and creationists or between astronomers and astrologers: one is scientific, the other is anti-scientific.[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-50)
During the early 1980s, Kenneth Feder performed a survey of his archaeology students. On the 50-question survey, 10 questions had to do with archaeology and/or pseudoscience. Some of the claims were more rational; the world is 5 billion years old, and human beings came about through evolution. However, questions also included issues such as, [King Tut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Tut "King Tut")'s tomb actually killed people upon discovery, and there is good evidence for the existence of Atlantis. As it resulted, some of the students Feder was teaching gave some credibility to the pseudoscience claims. Twelve percent actually believed people on [Howard Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Carter "Howard Carter")'s expedition were killed by [an ancient Egyptian curse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_the_pharaohs "Curse of the pharaohs").[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-51)
## Historical pseudoarchaeology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Historical pseudoarchaeology")]
During the mid-2nd century, those exposed by [Lucian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucian "Lucian")'s sarcastic essay ["Alexander the false prophet"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_of_Abonoteichus "Alexander of Abonoteichus") prepared an archaeological "find" in [Chalcedon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chalcedon "Chalcedon") to prepare a public for the supposed oracle they planned to establish at [Abonoteichus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abonoteichus "Abonoteichus") in [Paphlagonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paphlagonia "Paphlagonia") (Pearse, 2001):[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-52)
> [I]n the temple of Apollo, which is the most ancient in Chalcedon, they buried bronze tablets which said that very soon [Asclepius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asclepius "Asclepius"), with his father Apollo, would move to [Pontus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontus_\(region\) "Pontus \(region\)") and take up his residence at Abonoteichus. The opportune discovery of these tablets caused this story to spread quickly to all [Bithynia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bithynia "Bithynia") and Pontus, and to Abonoteichus sooner than anywhere else.
At [Glastonbury Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glastonbury_Abbey "Glastonbury Abbey") in 1291, at a time when [King Edward I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_I_of_England "Edward I of England") desired to emphasize his "Englishness", an alleged discovery was made: the supposed coffin of [King Arthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Arthur "King Arthur"), identified helpfully with an inscribed plaque. Arthur was reinterred at Glastonbury with a magnificent ceremonial attended by the king and queen. 
## Nationalist motivations
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Nationalist motivations")]
See also: [Nationalism and archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism_and_archaeology "Nationalism and archaeology")
Pseudoarchaeology can be motivated by [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism") (_[cf.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cf. "Cf.")_ [Nazi archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_archaeology "Nazi archaeology"), using cultural superiority of the ancient Aryan race as a basic assumption to establish the Germanic people as the descendants of the original Aryan 'master race') or a desire to prove a particular religious (_cf._ [intelligent design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligent_design "Intelligent design")), [pseudohistorical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudohistory "Pseudohistory"), political, or [anthropological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology") theory. In many cases, an _[a priori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_priori_and_a_posteriori "A priori and a posteriori")_ conclusion is established, and fieldwork is performed explicitly to corroborate the theory in detail.[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-53) According to archaeologist John Hoopes, writing for the magazine of the [Society for American Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_American_Archaeology "Society for American Archaeology"), "Pseudoarchaeology actively promotes myths that are routinely used in the service of [white supremacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_supremacy "White supremacy"), racialized [nationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nationalism "Nationalism"), [colonialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonialism "Colonialism"), and the dispossession and oppression of [indigenous peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigenous_peoples "Indigenous peoples")."[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Hoo19-54)
Archaeologists distinguish their research from pseudoarchaeology by indicating differences of research methods, including recursive methods, falsifiable theories, peer review, and a generally systematic approach to collecting data. Though there is overwhelming evidence of cultural associations informing folk traditions about the past,[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-LOWENTHAL1985-55) objective analysis of folk archaeology—in [anthropological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology "Anthropology") terms of their cultural contexts and the cultural desires to which they respond—have been comparatively few. However, in this vein, [Robert Silverberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Silverberg "Robert Silverberg") located the [Mormons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon "Mormon")' use of [Mound Builder culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mound_Builders "Mound Builders") within a larger cultural nexus[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-SILVERBERG1968-56) and the voyage of [Madoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madoc "Madoc") and "Welsh Indians" was set in its changing and evolving sociohistorical contexts by Gwyn Williams.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-GWYNWILLIAMS1987-57)
### List of examples
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: List of examples")]
  * The [Kensington Runestone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensington_Runestone "Kensington Runestone") of Minnesota used to allege Nordic [Viking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viking "Viking") primacy of exploring the Americas.
  * [Nazi archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi_archaeology "Nazi archaeology"), the [Thule Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thule_Society "Thule Society"), and expeditions sent by the [Ahnenerbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahnenerbe "Ahnenerbe") to research the existence of an alleged [Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryan "Aryan") race. The research of [Edmund Kiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Kiss "Edmund Kiss") at [Tiwanaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiwanaku "Tiwanaku") would be one example.
  * The [Bosnian pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnian_pyramids "Bosnian pyramids") project, which has projected that several hills in Visoko, Bosnia are ancient [pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid "Pyramid").
  * [Piltdown Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piltdown_Man "Piltdown Man"), a proven hoax from the early 1900s.
  * Jovan I. Deretić's [Serbocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbian_nationalism "Serbian nationalism") claims for the ancient history of the [Old World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_World "Old World").
  * The claim that the remains of [Cuauhtémoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuauht%C3%A9moc "Cuauhtémoc"), last tlatoani of [Tenochtitlán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tenochtitl%C3%A1n "Tenochtitlán"), were found in the church of [Ixcateopan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ixcateopan_de_Cuauht%C3%A9moc "Ixcateopan de Cuauhtémoc"). The research of [Eulalia Guzmán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulalia_Guzm%C3%A1n "Eulalia Guzmán") has been proven to be a hoax, but despite that the populace of the town still claims to guard the last [Aztec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec "Aztec") emperor's bones.
  * Romanian [protochronism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protochronism "Protochronism") also uses pseudoarchaeological interpretations; for more pieces of information, see the [Tărtăria tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T%C4%83rt%C4%83ria_tablets "Tărtăria tablets"), the [Rohonc Codex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohonc_Codex "Rohonc Codex")'s Daco-Romanian hypothesis, or the [Sinaia lead plates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sinaia_lead_plates "Sinaia lead plates").
  * The theory that New Zealand was not settled by the [Māori people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C4%81ori_people "Māori people"), but by a pre-Polynesian race of giants[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-58)
  * Claims of a [Tartarian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartarian_Empire "Tartarian Empire") that colonized the world.
  * [Afrocentrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afrocentrism "Afrocentrism") claims that [Black people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_people "Black people") should be credited with creating the first civilizations.
  * [Temple denial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_denial "Temple denial") and other claims by Palestinians that Jews are not native to the Land of Israel.


## Religious motivations
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Religious motivations")]
Religiously motivated pseudoarchaeological theories include the [young Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Earth_creationism "Young Earth creationism") theory of some Judeo-[Christian fundamentalists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_fundamentalist "Christian fundamentalist"). They argue that the Earth is 4,000–10,000 years old, with claims varying depending on the source. Some Hindu pseudoarchaeologists believe that the _Homo sapiens_ species is much older than the 200,000 years it is generally believed to have existed. Archaeologist John R. Cole refers to such beliefs as "cult archaeology" and believes them to be pseudoarchaeological. He said that this "pseudoarchaeology" had "many of the attributes, causes, and effects of religion".[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-Cole_1980._p._03-29)
A more specific example of religious pseudoarcheology is the claim of [Ron Wyatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ron_Wyatt "Ron Wyatt") to have discovered [Noah's Ark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah%27s_Ark "Noah's Ark"), the graves of [Noah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah "Noah") and his wife, the location of [Sodom and Gomorrah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodom_and_Gomorrah "Sodom and Gomorrah"), the [Tower of Babel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tower_of_Babel "Tower of Babel"), and numerous other important sites. However, he has not presented evidence sufficient to impress Bible scholars, scientists, and historians. The organization [Answers in Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Answers_in_Genesis "Answers in Genesis") propagates many pseudoscientific notions as part of its [creationist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creationist "Creationist") ministry.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-59)[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-60)
### Examples
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Examples")]
  * [Creation science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_science "Creation science"), also known as "scientific creationism," but which is actually [pseudoscientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience"), as it pertains to [human origins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropogeny "Anthropogeny").
  * Repeated [claims of the discovery of Noah's Ark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Searches_for_Noah%27s_Ark "Searches for Noah's Ark") on [Mount Ararat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Ararat "Mount Ararat") or neighbouring mountain ranges.
  * Use of questionable artefacts such as the [Grave Creek Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grave_Creek_Stone "Grave Creek Stone"), the [Los Lunas Decalogue Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Lunas_Decalogue_Stone "Los Lunas Decalogue Stone") and the [Michigan relics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michigan_relics "Michigan relics") to represent proof of the presence of a pre-Columbian Semitic culture in America.
  * [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age "New Age") assertions about [Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis "Atlantis"), [Lemuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemuria_\(continent\) "Lemuria \(continent\)"), and ancient [root races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_race "Root race") derived from the writings of authors such as 19th-century [theosophist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophist "Theosophist") and [occultist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occultist "Occultist") [Helena Blavatsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helena_Blavatsky "Helena Blavatsky").
  * Denial of scientific dating techniques in favor of a [young Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young_Earth_Creationism "Young Earth Creationism") age.
  * Several places have been claimed to be the [tomb of Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomb_of_Jesus "Tomb of Jesus"), including in [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roza_Bal "Roza Bal") and [Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirisuto_no_Haka "Kirisuto no Haka").


## In Egyptology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: In Egyptology")]
Pseudoarchaeology can be found in relation to [Egyptology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptology "Egyptology"), the study of [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt "Ancient Egypt"). Some of this includes [pyramidology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramidology "Pyramidology"), a collection of pseudoscientific beliefs about [pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid "Pyramid") around the world that includes the [pyramids in Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_pyramids "Egyptian pyramids") and specifically the [Great Pyramid of Giza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza "Great Pyramid of Giza").[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-61)
### Pyramids
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Pyramids")]
One belief originally published by [Charles Piazzi Smyth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Piazzi_Smyth "Charles Piazzi Smyth") in 1864 is that the Great Pyramid was not built by humans for the [pharaoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharaoh "Pharaoh") [Khufu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khufu "Khufu"), but was so beautiful that it could have been crafted only by the hand of God.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-62) Though Smyth contributed to the idea of the Great Pyramid not being created originally by Khufu, this belief has been further propagated by [Zecharia Sitchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zecharia_Sitchin "Zecharia Sitchin") in books such as _The Stairway to Heaven_ (1983) and more recently by [Scott Creighton](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scott_Creighton&action=edit&redlink=1 "Scott Creighton \(page does not exist\)") in _The Great Pyramid Hoax_ (2017), both of which argue that [Howard Vyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Vyse "Howard Vyse") (the discoverer of Khufu cartouches within the Great Pyramid) presented the earliest evidence that the Great Pyramid's builder[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-63)) faked the markings of Khufu's name. However, Sitchin's research has been challenged as being [pseudoscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscience "Pseudoscience").[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-64)[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-65) Arguments against these theories often detail the discovery of external texts on papyri such as the [Diary of Merer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary_of_Merer "Diary of Merer") that detail the construction of the Great Pyramid.[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-66)
The theory the Egyptian pyramids were not built as tombs of ancient pharaohs, but for other purposes, has resulted in a variety of alternative theories about their purpose and origins. One such pseudoarchaeological theory is from Scott Creighton, who argues that the pyramids were built as recovery vaults to survive a deluge (whether that be associated with [flood geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_geology "Flood geology") or the [Genesis Flood Narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesis_flood_narrative "Genesis flood narrative")). Another alternative theory for the purpose of the pyramids comes from known pseudoarchaeologist Graham Hancock, who argues that the pyramids originated from an early civilization that was destroyed by an asteroid that also began the [Younger Dryas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Dryas "Younger Dryas") period. A third common pseudoarchaeological theory about the Egyptian pyramids is that they were built by [ancient aliens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_astronauts "Ancient astronauts").[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-67) This belief is sometimes explained for why the pyramids supposedly appear suddenly in history. However, this claim is challenged by Egyptologists who describe an evolution of pyramid designs from [mastaba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastaba "Mastaba") tombs, to the [Step Pyramid of Djoser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_of_Djoser "Pyramid of Djoser"), to the collapsed [Meidum Pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meidum_Pyramid "Meidum Pyramid"), to Sneferefu's [Bent Pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bent_Pyramid "Bent Pyramid"), ending with Khufu's [Great Pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid "Great Pyramid").[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:0-68)[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-69) Many alternative beliefs have been criticized as ignoring the knowledge, architectural and constructive capabilities of ancient Egyptians.[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:0-68)
### Mummy curses
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Mummy curses")]
Another pseudoegyptological belief is that of the [curse of the pharaohs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_the_pharaohs "Curse of the pharaohs"), which involves a belief of imprecations being directed against those who enter the tombs of mummies, and pharaohs.[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-70) These curses often include natural disaster or illness or death for those who have entered the tomb.[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:1-71) One of the most influential iterations of this theory comes from the discovery of [King Tutankhamun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tutankhamun "Tutankhamun") by [Howard Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Carter "Howard Carter") in November 1922.[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-72) Several deaths of those present at the excavation have been attributed to a curse, including that of [Lord Carnarvon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Carnarvon "Lord Carnarvon") who died as the result of an infected mosquito bite, [sepsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepsis "Sepsis"), and [pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia "Pneumonia") slightly more than four months after the excavation.[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-73) There were also claims that all lights in Cairo went out at the moment of Lord Carnavon's death. However, skeptics believe that reporters overlooked rational explanations and relied on supernatural legends.[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:1-71) In 2021, mummies discovered mostly from the [New Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Kingdom_of_Egypt "New Kingdom of Egypt") period were to be paraded through Cairo during a transference for study. However, several events occurred, including a ship blocking the [Suez Canal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suez_Canal "Suez Canal") and accidents involving several members of the crew. Many claimed these were the results of a pharaoh's curse,[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:2-74)[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-75) however, Egyptologist [Zahi Hawass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zahi_Hawass "Zahi Hawass") dismissed the claims as random tragedies.[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:2-74)
### Pre-Columbian contact and Mayan connections
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Pre-Columbian contact and Mayan connections")]
Some pseudoarchaeologists speculate that Egypt had contact with the [Maya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") before [Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus "Christopher Columbus") reached [the Bahamas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bahamas "The Bahamas") in 1492.[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-76) Part of these arguments stem from the discovery of nicotine and cocaine traces found in various mummies.[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-77) The argument is that plants producing these were not known to exist outside the Americas, although Duncan Edlin found that plants containing both [nicotine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicotine "Nicotine") and [cocaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocaine "Cocaine") existed in Egypt and therefore could have been used by ancient Egyptians.[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:3-78) Another argument against possible contact is that there is a massive body of literature in the form of [hieroglyphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_hieroglyphs "Egyptian hieroglyphs") from ancient Egypt, however ancient Egyptian scholars never noted contacting the Americas in any of the texts that have been found.[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:3-78)
Another argument in favor of contact between ancient Egyptians and Mayans is from claims of similarities of art, architecture and writing. These theories are explained by authors such as Graham Hancock in _Fingerprints of the Gods_ (1995) and more recently by Richard Cassaro in _Mayan Masonry_.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-79) These similarities commonly mention creation of pyramids, use of archways, and similarities in artwork of the divine.[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-80) Arguments such as these claim an association between ancient Egypt and Maya through either a transatlantic outing that brought Egypt to the Mayas or through a shared origin in both civilizations (either in [Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis "Atlantis") or [Lemuria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemuria "Lemuria")).[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-81) _Voyages of the Pyramid Builders_ (2003) by geologist [Robert Schoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Schoch "Robert Schoch") argues that both Egyptian and Maya pyramids result from a common lost civilization. However, ancient historian [Garrett Fagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garrett_Fagan "Garrett Fagan") criticized Schoch's theory on the grounds that it demonstrated ignorance of relevant facts and that it did not explain variations in appearance or how various civilizations' pyramids were built.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:4-82) Fagan also describes known research by several archaeologists about the development of various civilizations' pyramids that was not used or addressed by Schoch's theory.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:4-82)
### Flood theories and the Great Sphinx
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Flood theories and the Great Sphinx")]
For Egypt-related pseudoarchaeology, there are a variety of [flood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood_myth "Flood myth")-related theories, many of which relate to the Biblical [Genesis flood narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesis_flood_narrative "Genesis flood narrative") or other flood theories. Scott Creighton claims that knowledge of a coming deluge (which he refers to as "Thoth's Flood") generated the idea of building pyramids as recovery vaults from which civilization could rebuild.[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-83) Another fringe theory relating to this is the [Sphinx water erosion hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphinx_water_erosion_hypothesis "Sphinx water erosion hypothesis"), which claims that the [Great Sphinx of Giza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Sphinx_of_Giza "Great Sphinx of Giza")'s modern body appearance is caused by erosion due to flooding or rain.[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:5-84) This theory, which has been perpetuated by Robert Schoch who claims the Sphinx was built between 5000 and 7000 BCE, has been criticized by Zahi Hawass and [Mark Lehner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Lehner "Mark Lehner") as ignoring Old Kingdom societal evidence about the Sphinx and being flawed in citing specifics about a possible erosion.[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-:5-84) Currently Egyptologists tend to date the Sphinx sometime about 2500 B.C., approximately the reign of the pharaoh [Khafre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khafre "Khafre") for whom the Sphinx is commonly attributed.[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-85)
## The Mayas
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: The Mayas")]
See also: [Mayanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayanism "Mayanism")
Many aspects of [Maya civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization "Maya civilization") have inspired pseudoarchaeological speculation. In Mexico, this history can bring more people which in turn brings more money for the area, which the [Maya peoples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_peoples "Maya peoples") usually do not receive. Many examples of pseudoarcheology pertaining to Maya civilization can be found in literature, art, and movies. Many of them have to do with the [2012 phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_phenomenon "2012 phenomenon") and the [Maya calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_calendar "Maya calendar"). These are often referred to as [Mayanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayanism "Mayanism"), a collection of [New Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Age "New Age") beliefs about Mayas and Maya religion and/or spirituality. That said, Mayan culture has long been a subject of scientific archaeology. Archaeologists have uncovered evidence that has furthered our knowledge of the past. Some of these include stone carvings in [Tikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikal "Tikal") that show the earliest stories of [Sihyaj Chan Kʼawiil II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sihyaj_Chan_K%CA%BCawiil_II "Sihyaj Chan Kʼawiil II") and materials recovered from [Chichen Itza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chichen_Itza "Chichen Itza").[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-86)
### Examples of Maya-related pseudoarchaeology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Pseudoarchaeology&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Examples of Maya-related pseudoarchaeology")]
  * A well-known example of Maya pseudoarcheology is the interpretation of the remains of [Kʼinich Janaabʼ Pakal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%CA%BCinich_Janaab%CA%BC_Pakal "Kʼinich Janaabʼ Pakal") and his burial. Pseudoarchaeologists have discussed much about the discovery of Pakal's sarcophagus lid and the answers they gained from studying it. Pseudoarchaeology author Maurice Cotterell writes about this in his book _The Supergods_. One of the main draws in this material for Cotterell and other pseudoarchaeologists is that the ancient Aztec and Maya people possessed knowledge beyond our imagination. From being able to "take off in spaceships", to dealing with complex numbers and equations, these people possessed "godly intelligence". Their biggest study and answer came from analyzing the Mayan calendar and finding correlations with the Sun and Earth. He states that "they (Sun, Earth, Mayan Calendar) come close together every 260 days, this agreed with his suspicion that the Mayan numbering system was connected with solar magnetic cycles". There are no professionals that endorse his statements, and his conclusions are based on insufficient evidence. Cotterell's work is pseudoarcheology because it reports his own non-scientific interpretations, without any scientific [peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review "Peer review") or critical analysis by professional archaeologists.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
  * Another example of pseudoarcheology concerning Maya civilization are some conclusions gained from studying the [Maya calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_calendar "Maya calendar"). The Calendar Round seems to have been based on two overlapping annual cycles: a 260-day sacred year and a 365-day secular year that named 18 months with 20 days each. The Maya calendar also included what were termed Long Counts, these were created by priests at the time and a single cycle lasted 5,126 solar years. From the time this was created, the end of the solar years occurred on 21 December 2012. Ancient hieroglyphs from [Tortuguero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tortuguero_\(Maya_site\) "Tortuguero \(Maya site\)") claimed that when this cycle ended, Bolon Yokte, the Mayan god of creation and war would arrive. Some pseudoarchaeologists assumed to mean that the world would end.
  * The stone carvings in [Tikal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikal "Tikal") that have been important to archaeologists attempting to recreate the past, have also been used by pseudoarchaeologists to fabricate false claims about the past.[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-87) In reality these carvings have been used to reconstruct the stories and history of more than thirty dynastic rulers. Some pseudoarchaeologists claim that these carvings are of ancient aliens or another form of [extraterrestrial life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraterrestrial_life "Extraterrestrial life"). These claims are widely regarded as false by archeologists. When these claims were circulated during the early 1990s, the rate of tourism boomed. In cases like this, pseudoarcheological claims can often garner public attention more effectively than peer-reviewed archeology.
  * Chichén Itzá in Mexico has long been an important archaeological site. Throughout the past few years there have been many wild claims by pseudoarchaeologists. The passageway beneath the Kulkulcan pyramid, a part of Chichén Itzá, was found and this is what many of the pseudoarchaeologists' claims concern. The claim is that this passageway was and still is a direct channel to the underworld. There are many possibilities for what this could have been used for, but there are not any facts to prove this statement. Many experts, including Guillermo de And, an underwater archaeologist who directed a few expeditions to uncover Mayan aqua life, believe that the passageway was a "secret [cenote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cenote "Cenote")".[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoarchaeology#cite_note-88)
