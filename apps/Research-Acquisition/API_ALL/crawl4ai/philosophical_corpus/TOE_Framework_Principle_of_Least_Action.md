Fundamental mechanical principles
**Action principles** are fundamental to physics, from [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics") through [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"), and [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-1) Action principles start with an energy function called a [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_\(mechanics\) "Lagrangian \(mechanics\)") describing the physical system. The accumulated value of this energy function between two states of the system is called the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_\(physics\) "Action \(physics\)"). Action principles apply the [calculus of variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_variation "Calculus of variation") to the action. The action depends on the energy function, and the energy function depends on the position, motion, and interactions in the system: variation of the action allows the derivation of the equations of motion without vectors or forces. 
Several distinct action principles differ in the constraints on their initial and final conditions. The names of action principles have evolved over time and differ in details of the endpoints of the paths and the nature of the variation. Quantum action principles generalize and justify the older classical principles by showing they are a direct result of quantum interference patterns. Action principles are the basis for [Feynman's version of quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation "Path integral formulation"), general relativity and [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"). 
The action principles have applications as broad as physics, including many problems in classical mechanics but especially in modern problems of quantum mechanics and general relativity. These applications built up over two centuries as the power of the method and its further mathematical development rose. 
This article introduces the action principle concepts and summarizes other articles with more details on concepts and specific principles. 
## Common concepts
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Common concepts")]
Action principles are "[integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_equation "Integral equation")" approaches rather than the "[differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation "Differential equation")" approach of [Newtonian mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_mechanics "Newtonian mechanics").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-YourgrauMandelstam1979-2): 162  The core ideas are based on energy, paths, an energy function called the Lagrangian along paths, and selection of a path according to the "action", a continuous sum or integral of the Lagrangian along the path. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Energy, not force")]
Introductory study of mechanics, the science of interacting objects, typically begins with [Newton's laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws "Newton's laws") based on the concept of [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force "Force"), defined by the acceleration it causes when applied to [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass "Mass"): _F_ = _ma_. This approach to mechanics focuses on a single point in space and time, attempting to answer the question: "What happens next?".[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-HancTaylorTuleja-3) Mechanics based on action principles begin with the concept of [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_\(physics\) "Action \(physics\)"), an energy tradeoff between [kinetic energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy "Kinetic energy") and [potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy "Potential energy"), defined by the physics of the problem. These approaches answer questions relating starting and ending points: Which trajectory will place a basketball in the hoop? If we launch a rocket to the Moon today, how can it land there in 5 days?[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-HancTaylorTuleja-3) The Newtonian and action-principle forms are equivalent, and either one can solve the same problems, but selecting the appropriate form will make solutions much easier. 
The energy function in the action principles is not the total energy ([conserved in an isolated system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy "Conservation of energy")), but the [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_\(mechanics\) "Lagrangian \(mechanics\)"), the difference between kinetic and potential energy. The kinetic energy combines the energy of motion for all the objects in the system; the potential energy depends upon the instantaneous position of the objects and drives the motion of the objects. The motion of the objects places them in new positions with new potential energy values, giving a new value for the Lagrangian.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Coopersmith2017-4): 125 
Using energy rather than force gives immediate advantages as a basis for mechanics. Force mechanics involves 3-dimensional [vector calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_calculus "Vector calculus"), with 3 space and 3 momentum coordinates for each object in the scenario; energy is a scalar magnitude combining information from all objects, giving an immediate simplification in many cases. The components of force vary with coordinate systems; the energy value is the same in all coordinate systems.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Lanczos-5): xxv  Force requires an inertial frame of reference;[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Kleppner-6): 65  once velocities approach the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light "Speed of light"), [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity") profoundly affects mechanics based on forces. In action principles, relativity merely requires a different Lagrangian: the principle itself is independent of coordinate systems.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-7)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Paths, not points")]
The explanatory diagrams in force-based mechanics usually focus on a single point, like the [center of momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_momentum "Center of momentum"), and show vectors of forces and velocities. The explanatory diagrams of action-based mechanics have two points with actual and possible paths connecting them.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-FeynmanII-8) These diagrammatic conventions reiterate the different strong points of each method. 
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/Freebodydiagram3_pn.svg/250px-Freebodydiagram3_pn.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Freebodydiagram3_pn.svg)
Diagrammatic aid for forces
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/bf/Feynman_paths.png/250px-Feynman_paths.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Feynman_paths.png)
Diagrammatic aid for action principle
Depending on the action principle, the two points connected by paths in a diagram may represent two particle positions at different times, or the two points may represent values in a [configuration space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Configuration_space_\(physics\) "Configuration space \(physics\)") or in a [phase space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space "Phase space"). The mathematical technology and terminology of action principles can be learned by thinking in terms of physical space, then applied in the more powerful and general abstract spaces. 
### Action along a path
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Action along a path")]
Action principles assign a number—the action—to each possible path between two points. This number is computed by adding an energy value for each small section of the path multiplied by the time spent in that section:[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-FeynmanII-8)     action  S = ∫ t 1 t 2 ( KE ( t ) − PE ( t ) ) d t , {\displaystyle S=\int _{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\bigl (}{\text{KE}}(t)-{\text{PE}}(t){\bigr )}\,dt,} ![{\\displaystyle S=\\int _{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\\bigl \(}{\\text{KE}}\(t\)-{\\text{PE}}\(t\){\\bigr \)}\\,dt,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e514bc96b2de976f60adad43130ef6038d3ecdfd)
where the form of the kinetic energy (KE) and potential energy (PE) expressions depend upon the physics problem, and their value at each point on the path depends upon relative coordinates corresponding to that point. The energy function is called a Lagrangian; in simple problems it is the kinetic energy minus the potential energy of the system. 
### Path variation
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Path variation")]
In classical mechanics, a system moving between two points takes one particular path; other similar paths are not taken. Each conceivable path corresponds to a value of the action. An action principle predicts or explains that the particular path taken has a stationary value for the system's action: similar paths near the one taken have very similar action value. This variation in the action value is key to the action principles. 
In quantum mechanics, every possible path contributes an amplitude to the system's behavior, with the phase of each amplitude determined by the action for that path (phase = ⁠action/[ħ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant "Planck constant")⁠). The classical path emerges because:[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
  * Only near the path of stationary action do neighboring paths have similar phases, leading to [constructive interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_interference "Constructive interference"),
  * Neighboring paths have rapidly varying actions with the phase that [interfere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Destructive_interference "Destructive interference") with other paths,


When the scale of the problem is much larger than the [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant "Planck constant") ħ (the classical limit), only the stationary action path survives the interference. 
The symbol δ is used to indicate the path [variations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calculus_of_variations "Calculus of variations") so an action principle appears mathematically as       ( δ S ) C = 0 , {\displaystyle (\delta S)_{C}=0,} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\delta S\)_{C}=0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/98e70559f47c14b2399058aa8e1f5237db40f161)
meaning that at the [stationary point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_point "Stationary point"), the variation of the action S with some fixed constraints C is zero.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Goldstein3-9): 38  For action principles, the stationary point may be a minimum or a [saddle point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle_point "Saddle point"), but not a maximum.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-10) Elliptical planetary orbits provide a simple example of two paths with equal action – one in each direction around the orbit; neither can be the minimum or "least action".[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-YourgrauMandelstam1979-2): 175  The path variation implied by δ is not the same as a differential like dt. The action integral depends on the coordinates of the objects, and these coordinates depend upon the path taken. Thus the action integral is a [functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_\(mathematics\) "Functional \(mathematics\)"), a function of a function. 
### Conservation principles
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Conservation principles")]
Main article: [Noether's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether%27s_theorem "Noether's theorem")
An important result from geometry known as [Noether's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noether%27s_theorem "Noether's theorem") states that any conserved quantities in a Lagrangian imply a continuous symmetry and conversely.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-11) For examples, a Lagrangian independent of time corresponds to a system with conserved energy; spatial translation independence implies momentum conservation; angular rotation invariance implies angular momentum conservation.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-12): 489  These examples are global symmetries, where the independence is itself independent of space or time; more general _local_ symmetries having a functional dependence on space or time lead to [gauge theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theory "Gauge theory").[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Brading-13) The observed conservation of [isospin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isospin "Isospin") was used by [Yang Chen-Ning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Chen-Ning "Yang Chen-Ning") and [Robert Mills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Mills_\(physicist\) "Robert Mills \(physicist\)") in 1953 to construct a gauge theory for [mesons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson "Meson"), leading some decades later to [modern particle physics theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Baggott40-14): 202 
## Distinct principles
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Distinct principles")]
Action principles apply to a wide variety of physical problems, including all of fundamental physics. The only major exceptions are cases involving friction or when only the initial position and velocities are given.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-HancTaylorTuleja-3) Different action principles have different meaning for the variations; each specific application of an action principle requires a specific Lagrangian describing the physics. A common name for any or all of these principles is "the principle of least action". For a discussion of the names and historical origin of these principles see [action principle names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_variational_principles_in_physics#action_principle_names "History of variational principles in physics"). 
### Fixed endpoints with conserved energy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Fixed endpoints with conserved energy")]
Main article: [Maupertuis's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maupertuis%27s_principle "Maupertuis's principle")
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/Dwyane_Wade_Shooting_Free_Throws_%282751838793%29.jpg/250px-Dwyane_Wade_Shooting_Free_Throws_%282751838793%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Dwyane_Wade_Shooting_Free_Throws_\(2751838793\).jpg)[Dwyane Wade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwyane_Wade "Dwyane Wade") shooting free throws illustrating the kind of physical constraints suitable for application of Maupertuis' least action principle
When total energy and the endpoints are fixed, [Maupertuis's least action principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maupertuis%27s_principle "Maupertuis's principle") applies. For example, to score points in basketball the ball must leave the shooters hand and go through the hoop, but the time of the flight is not constrained.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-HancTaylorTuleja-3) Maupertuis's least action principle is written mathematically as the stationary condition  ( δ W ) E = 0 {\displaystyle (\delta W)_{E}=0} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\delta W\)_{E}=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5a2a6f76c9ab13ff2507aa3a5b65b00c5701b92f) on the [abbreviated action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_\(physics\)#abbreviated_action "Action \(physics\)") W [ q ] = def ∫ q 1 q 2 p ⋅ d q , {\displaystyle W[\mathbf {q} ]\ {\stackrel {\text{def}}{=}}\ \int _{q_{1}}^{q_{2}}\mathbf {p} \cdot \mathbf {dq} ,} ![{\\displaystyle W\[\\mathbf {q} \]\\ {\\stackrel {\\text{def}}{=}}\\ \\int _{q_{1}}^{q_{2}}\\mathbf {p} \\cdot \\mathbf {dq} ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/336df873cb2e485e9c6471927d679636f38d661d) (sometimes written _S_ 0), where **p** = (_p_ 1,_p_ 2,…,_p N_) are the particle momenta or the conjugate momenta of [generalized coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_coordinates "Generalized coordinates"), defined by the equation  p k = def ∂ L ∂ q ˙ k , {\displaystyle p_{k}\ {\stackrel {\text{def}}{=}}\ {\frac {\partial L}{\partial {\dot {q}}_{k}}},} ![{\\displaystyle p_{k}\\ {\\stackrel {\\text{def}}{=}}\\ {\\frac {\\partial L}{\\partial {\\dot {q}}_{k}}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5b73a38938f21ef3c2112ff5a37301c21476d026) where _L_(**q** ,**q̇** ,_t_) is the [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_mechanics "Lagrangian mechanics"). Some textbooks write[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-15): 76 [[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Goldstein3-9): 356  (_δW_)_E_ = 0 as Δ _S_ 0, to emphasize that the variation used in this form of the action principle differs from [Hamilton's variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton%27s_principle "Hamilton's principle"). Here the total energy E is fixed during the variation, but not the time, the reverse of the constraints on Hamilton's principle.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-GrayWeb-16) Consequently, the same path and end points take different times and energies in the two forms. The solutions in the case of this form of Maupertuis's principle are [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbits "Orbits"): functions relating coordinates to each other in which time is simply an index or a parameter.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-GrayWeb-16)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Time-independent potentials; no forces")]
Main article: [Fermat's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_principle "Fermat's principle")
For time-invariant system, the action  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2) relates simply to the abbreviated action W on the stationary path as[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Goldstein3-9): 434  Δ S = Δ W − E Δ t {\displaystyle \Delta S=\Delta W-E\Delta t} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta S=\\Delta W-E\\Delta t}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b348bdf0ba027470c83c34b724cbb293b5b21a6e) for energy E and time difference Δ _t_ = _t_ 2 − _t_ 1. For a rigid body with no net force, the actions are identical, and the variational principles become equivalent to [Fermat's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_principle "Fermat's principle") of least time:[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Goldstein3-9): 360  δ ( t 2 − t 1 ) = 0. {\displaystyle \delta (t_{2}-t_{1})=0.} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta \(t_{2}-t_{1}\)=0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/37781e2606e16b589ea338e64215626b01ed0ead)
### Fixed events
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Fixed events")]
Main article: [Hamilton's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton%27s_principle "Hamilton's principle")
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/05/Trans_Lunar_Injection-01.png/250px-Trans_Lunar_Injection-01.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Trans_Lunar_Injection-01.png)A path to the Moon must account for the motion of the Moon during the voyage.
When the physics problem gives the two endpoints as a position and a time, that is as [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_\(relativity\) "Event \(relativity\)"), [Hamilton's action principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton%27s_principle "Hamilton's principle") applies. For example, imagine planning a trip to the Moon. During your voyage the Moon will continue its orbit around the Earth: it is a moving target. Hamilton's principle for objects at positions **q**(_t_) is written mathematically as  ( δ S ) Δ t = 0 , where S [ q ] = d e f ∫ t 1 t 2 L ( q ( t ) , q ˙ ( t ) , t ) d t . {\displaystyle (\delta {\mathcal {S}})_{\Delta t}=0,\quad {\text{where}}\ {\mathcal {S}}[\mathbf {q} ]\ {\stackrel {\mathrm {def} }{=}}\ \int _{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L(\mathbf {q} (t),{\dot {\mathbf {q} }}(t),t)\,dt.} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\delta {\\mathcal {S}}\)_{\\Delta t}=0,\\quad {\\text{where}}\\ {\\mathcal {S}}\[\\mathbf {q} \]\\ {\\stackrel {\\mathrm {def} }{=}}\\ \\int _{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L\(\\mathbf {q} \(t\),{\\dot {\\mathbf {q} }}\(t\),t\)\\,dt.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/aa9e115bfa72a1e8c9fd3d6dc5fb2ff8ca9f8109) The constraint Δ _t_ = _t_ 2 − _t_ 1 means that we only consider paths taking the same time, as well as connecting the same two points **q**(_t_ 1) and **q**(_t_ 2). The [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_mechanics "Lagrangian mechanics") L = T − V {\displaystyle L=T-V} ![{\\displaystyle L=T-V}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/00644a2f8d4ddaa3b1ae36d472aabaeb63e4d9dc) is the difference between kinetic energy and potential energy at each point on the path.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-17): 62  Solution of the resulting equations gives the [world line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_line "World line") **q**(_t_).[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-HancTaylorTuleja-3) Starting with Hamilton's principle, the local differential [Euler–Lagrange equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%E2%80%93Lagrange_equation "Euler–Lagrange equation") can be derived for systems of fixed energy. The action  S {\displaystyle S} ![{\\displaystyle S}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4611d85173cd3b508e67077d4a1252c9c05abca2) in Hamilton's principle is the [Legendre transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_transformation "Legendre transformation") of the action in Maupertuis's principle.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-18)
### Classical field theory

The concepts and many of the methods useful for particle mechanics also apply to continuous fields. ### Quantum action principles
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Quantum action principles")]
For quantum mechanics, the action principles have significant advantages: only one mechanical postulate is needed, if a covariant Lagrangian is used in the action, the result is relativistically correct, and they transition clearly to classical equivalents.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-YourgrauMandelstam1979-2): 128 
Both [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman") and [Julian Schwinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Schwinger "Julian Schwinger") developed quantum action principles based on early work by [Paul Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dirac "Paul Dirac"). Feynman's integral method was not a variational principle but reduces to the classical least action principle; it led to his [Feynman diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_diagrams "Feynman diagrams"). Schwinger's differential approach relates infinitesimal amplitude changes to infinitesimal action changes.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-YourgrauMandelstam1979-2): 138 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Feynman's action principle")]
Main article: [Path integral formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation "Path integral formulation")
When quantum effects are important, new action principles are needed. Instead of a particle following a path, quantum mechanics defines a probability amplitude _ψ_(_x k_,_t_) at one point _x k_ and time t related to a probability amplitude at a different point later in time:  ψ ( x k + 1 , t + ε ) = 1 A ∫ e i ℏ S ( x k + 1 , x k ) ψ ( x k , t ) d x k , {\displaystyle \psi (x_{k+1},t+\varepsilon )={\frac {1}{A}}\int e^{{\frac {i}{\hbar }}S(x_{k+1},x_{k})}\psi (x_{k},t)\,dx_{k},} ![{\\displaystyle \\psi \(x_{k+1},t+\\varepsilon \)={\\frac {1}{A}}\\int e^{{\\frac {i}{\\hbar }}S\(x_{k+1},x_{k}\)}\\psi \(x_{k},t\)\\,dx_{k},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/93bbc59c182fddfee0e99737cad956a74626fa81) where _S_(_x_ _k_ + 1,_x k_) is the classical action.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-20) Instead of a single path with stationary action, all possible paths add (the integral over _x k_), weighted by a complex probability amplitude _e_ _iS_ ⁄ _ħ_. The phase of the amplitude is given by the action divided by the [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant "Planck constant") or quantum of action: ⁠ _S_ /_ħ_ ⁠. When the action of a particle is much larger than ħ, ⁠ _S_ /_ħ_ ⁠ ≫ 1, the phase changes rapidly along the path: the amplitude averages to a small number.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-FeynmanII-8) Thus the Planck constant sets the boundary between classical and quantum mechanics.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Curtis-21)
All of the paths contribute in the quantum action principle. At the end point, where the paths meet, the paths with similar phases add, and those with phases differing by π subtract. Close to the path expected from classical physics, phases tend to align; the tendency is stronger for more massive objects that have larger values of action. In the classical limit, one path dominates – the path of stationary action.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-22)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Schwinger's action principle")]
Main article: [Schwinger's quantum action principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwinger%27s_quantum_action_principle "Schwinger's quantum action principle")
Schwinger's approach relates variations in the transition amplitudes (_q_ f|_q_ i) to variations in an action matrix element:       δ ( q r f | q r i ) = i ( q r f | δ S | q r i ) , {\displaystyle \delta (q_{r_{\text{f}}}|q_{r_{\text{i}}})=i(q_{r_{\text{f}}}|\delta S|q_{r_{\text{i}}}),} ![{\\displaystyle \\delta \(q_{r_{\\text{f}}}|q_{r_{\\text{i}}}\)=i\(q_{r_{\\text{f}}}|\\delta S|q_{r_{\\text{i}}}\),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/268d771a4e71d90640ee023e84ca27c8734d0299)
where the action operator is       S = ∫ t i t f L d t . {\displaystyle S=\int _{t_{\text{i}}}^{t_{\text{f}}}L\,dt.} ![{\\displaystyle S=\\int _{t_{\\text{i}}}^{t_{\\text{f}}}L\\,dt.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c5bc2746625895c950b896d7bd0fb951bcd0ab6e)
The Schwinger form makes analysis of variation of the Lagrangian itself, for example, variation in potential source strength, especially transparent.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-YourgrauMandelstam1979-2): 138 
## Optico-mechanical analogy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Optico-mechanical analogy")]
Main article: [Hamilton's optico-mechanical analogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton%27s_optico-mechanical_analogy "Hamilton's optico-mechanical analogy")
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/Hamiltonian_Optics-Rays_and_Wavefronts.svg/250px-Hamiltonian_Optics-Rays_and_Wavefronts.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Hamiltonian_Optics-Rays_and_Wavefronts.svg)Surfaces of constant action shown as wavefronts perpendicular to paths for the case of light
For every path, the action integral builds in value from zero at the starting point to its final value at the end. Any nearby path has similar values at similar distances from the starting point. Lines or surfaces of constant partial action value can be drawn across the paths, creating a wave-like view of the action. Analysis like this connects particle-like rays of [geometrical optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrical_optics "Geometrical optics") with the wavefronts of [Huygens–Fresnel principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens%E2%80%93Fresnel_principle "Huygens–Fresnel principle"). 
> [[Maupertuis]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Louis_Maupertuis "Pierre Louis Maupertuis") … thus pointed to that remarkable analogy between optical and mechanical phenomena which was observed much earlier by [John Bernoulli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bernoulli "John Bernoulli") and which was later fully developed in Hamilton's ingenious optico-mechanical theory. This analogy played a fundamental role in the development of modern wave-mechanics. 
— [C. Lanczos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornelius_Lanczos "Cornelius Lanczos")[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Lanczos-5): 136 
## Applications
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Applications")]
See also: [Lagrangian (field theory) § Examples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_\(field_theory\)#Examples "Lagrangian \(field theory\)")
Action principles are applied to derive differential equations like the [Euler–Lagrange equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%E2%80%93Lagrange_equations "Euler–Lagrange equations")[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Goldstein3-9): 44  or as direct applications to physical problems. 
### Classical mechanics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Classical mechanics")]
Action principles can be directly applied to many problems in [classical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics "Classical mechanics"), such as the shape of elastic rods under load,[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Dittrich-23): 9  the shape of a liquid between two vertical plates (a [capillary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capillary "Capillary")),[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Dittrich-23): 22  or the motion of a pendulum when its support is in motion.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Dittrich-23): 39 
### Chemistry
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Chemistry")]
Quantum action principles are used in the quantum theory of atoms in molecules ([QTAIM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QTAIM "QTAIM")), a way of decomposing the computed electron density of molecules in to atoms as a way of gaining insight into chemical bonding.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-24)
### General relativity
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: General relativity")]
Inspired by Einstein's work on [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity"), the renowned mathematician [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert") applied the principle of least action to derive the field equations of general relativity.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-25): 186  His action, now known as the [Einstein–Hilbert action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein%E2%80%93Hilbert_action "Einstein–Hilbert action"),       S = 1 2 κ ∫ R − g d 4 x , {\displaystyle S={\frac {1}{2\kappa }}\int R{\sqrt {-g}}\,d^{4}x,} ![{\\displaystyle S={\\frac {1}{2\\kappa }}\\int R{\\sqrt {-g}}\\,d^{4}x,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b27c42a5fac5991016c1e08f2d8049ab1d93566)
contained a relativistically invariant volume element √− _g_ _d_ 4 _x_ and the Ricci [scalar curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_curvature "Scalar curvature") R. The scale factor  κ {\displaystyle \kappa } ![{\\displaystyle \\kappa }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/54ddec2e922c5caea4e47d04feef86e782dc8e6d) is the [Einstein gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_gravitational_constant "Einstein gravitational constant"). 
### Other applications
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Other applications")]
The action principle is so central in [modern physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_physics "Modern physics") and [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") that it is widely applied including in [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics"),[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-26)[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-27)[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-28) [fluid mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_mechanics "Fluid mechanics"),[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-29) the [theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity "Theory of relativity"), [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"),[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-30) [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"), and [string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory "String theory").[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-31)
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Action_principles&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: History")]
Main article: [History of variational principles in physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_variational_principles_in_physics "History of variational principles in physics")
The action principle is preceded by earlier ideas in [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics "Optics"). In [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"), [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid") wrote in his _Catoptrica_ that, for the path of light reflecting from a mirror, the [angle of incidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_incidence_\(optics\) "Angle of incidence \(optics\)") equals the [angle of reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_reflection "Angle of reflection").[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-32) [Hero of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_of_Alexandria "Hero of Alexandria") later showed that this path has the shortest length and least time.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-33)
Building on the early work of [Pierre Louis Maupertuis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Louis_Maupertuis "Pierre Louis Maupertuis"), [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler"), and [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange "Joseph-Louis Lagrange") defining versions of **principle of least action** ,[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-Kline1972-34): 580  [William Rowan Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rowan_Hamilton "William Rowan Hamilton") and in tandem [Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jacob_Jacobi "Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi") developed a variational form for classical mechanics known as the [Hamilton–Jacobi equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamilton%E2%80%93Jacobi_equation "Hamilton–Jacobi equation").[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-NakaneFraser-35): 201 
In 1915, [David Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hilbert "David Hilbert") applied the variational principle to derive [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein")'s equations of [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity").[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-MehraEinsteinHilbert-36)
In 1933, the physicist [Paul Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Dirac "Paul Dirac") demonstrated how this principle can be used in quantum calculations by discerning the [quantum mechanical underpinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation#Quantum_action_principle "Path integral formulation") of the principle in the [quantum interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interference_\(wave_propagation\)#Quantum_interference "Interference \(wave propagation\)") of amplitudes.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-37) Subsequently [Julian Schwinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Schwinger "Julian Schwinger") and [Richard Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman") independently applied this principle in quantum electrodynamics.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-38)[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principles#cite_note-39)
