The **Born rule** is a postulate of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") that gives the [probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability "Probability") that a [measurement of a quantum system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurement_in_quantum_mechanics "Measurement in quantum mechanics") will yield a given result. In one commonly used application, it states that the [probability density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_density_function "Probability density function") for finding a particle at a given position is proportional to the square of the amplitude of the system's [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction "Wavefunction") at that position. It was formulated and published by German physicist [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born "Max Born") in July 1926.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-1)
## Details
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Born_rule&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Details")]
The Born rule states that an [observable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable "Observable"), measured in a system with normalized [wave function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function "Wave function") | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) (see [Bra–ket notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bra%E2%80%93ket_notation "Bra–ket notation")), corresponds to a [self-adjoint operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint_operator "Self-adjoint operator") A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) whose [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_\(functional_analysis\) "Spectrum \(functional analysis\)") is discrete if: 
  * the measured result will be one of the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors "Eigenvalues and eigenvectors") λ {\displaystyle \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b43d0ea3c9c025af1be9128e62a18fa74bedda2a) of  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3), and
  * the probability of measuring a given eigenvalue  λ i {\displaystyle \lambda _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72fde940918edf84caf3d406cc7d31949166820f) will equal  ⟨ ψ | P i | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle \psi |P_{i}|\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\psi |P_{i}|\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d7d2128d494edf8e78cd3771bd3f3fc62f7b3503), where  P i {\displaystyle P_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle P_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3ba1396129f7be3c7f828a571b6649e6807d10d3) is the projection onto the [eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace "Eigenspace") of  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) corresponding to  λ i {\displaystyle \lambda _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72fde940918edf84caf3d406cc7d31949166820f).

    (In the case where the eigenspace of  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) corresponding to  λ i {\displaystyle \lambda _{i}} ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda _{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72fde940918edf84caf3d406cc7d31949166820f) is one-dimensional and spanned by the normalized eigenvector  | λ i ⟩ {\displaystyle |\lambda _{i}\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\lambda _{i}\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/abb47e462052ec3958087e048ef82eabba171d6c),  P i {\displaystyle P_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle P_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3ba1396129f7be3c7f828a571b6649e6807d10d3) is equal to  | λ i ⟩ ⟨ λ i | {\displaystyle |\lambda _{i}\rangle \langle \lambda _{i}|} ![{\\displaystyle |\\lambda _{i}\\rangle \\langle \\lambda _{i}|}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ea6ec1f6c146476065e03bbc59a80774a64ca74f), so the probability  ⟨ ψ | P i | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle \psi |P_{i}|\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\psi |P_{i}|\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d7d2128d494edf8e78cd3771bd3f3fc62f7b3503) is equal to  ⟨ ψ | λ i ⟩ ⟨ λ i | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle \psi |\lambda _{i}\rangle \langle \lambda _{i}|\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\psi |\\lambda _{i}\\rangle \\langle \\lambda _{i}|\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1fc48e9d6e20970b94822aa49174db0c07bb82d2). Since the [complex number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number "Complex number") ⟨ λ i | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle \lambda _{i}|\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\lambda _{i}|\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8434fce662caacbd47cbbe1e18338c679f22e200) is known as the _[probability amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude "Probability amplitude")_ that the state vector  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) assigns to the eigenvector  | λ i ⟩ {\displaystyle |\lambda _{i}\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\lambda _{i}\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/abb47e462052ec3958087e048ef82eabba171d6c), it is common to describe the Born rule as saying that probability is equal to the amplitude-squared (really the amplitude times its own [complex conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugate "Complex conjugate")). Equivalently, the probability can be written as  | ⟨ λ i | ψ ⟩ | 2 {\displaystyle {\big |}\langle \lambda _{i}|\psi \rangle {\big |}^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\big |}\\langle \\lambda _{i}|\\psi \\rangle {\\big |}^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/751d9e9eaebec8f4157934d43cd2282b8f5de0b2).)
In the case where the spectrum of  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3) is not wholly discrete, the [spectral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_theorem#Possible_absence_of_eigenvectors "Spectral theorem") proves the existence of a certain [projection-valued measure (PVM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection-valued_measure "Projection-valued measure") Q {\displaystyle Q} ![{\\displaystyle Q}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8752c7023b4b3286800fe3238271bbca681219ed), the spectral measure of  A {\displaystyle A} ![{\\displaystyle A}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7daff47fa58cdfd29dc333def748ff5fa4c923e3). In this case: 
  * the probability that the result of the measurement lies in a measurable set  M {\displaystyle M} ![{\\displaystyle M}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f82cade9898ced02fdd08712e5f0c0151758a0dd) is given by  ⟨ ψ | Q ( M ) | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle \psi |Q(M)|\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\psi |Q\(M\)|\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d64b5f2ec54e37e33fd663d4290d8b616014454b).


For example, a single structureless particle can be described by a wave function  ψ {\displaystyle \psi } ![{\\displaystyle \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45e5789e5d9c8f7c79744f43ecaaf8ba42a8553a) that depends upon position coordinates  ( x , y , z ) {\displaystyle (x,y,z)} ![{\\displaystyle \(x,y,z\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/22a8c93372e8f8b6e24d523bd5545aed3430baf4) and a time coordinate  t {\displaystyle t} ![{\\displaystyle t}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/65658b7b223af9e1acc877d848888ecdb4466560). The Born rule implies that the probability density function  p {\displaystyle p} ![{\\displaystyle p}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81eac1e205430d1f40810df36a0edffdc367af36) for the result of a measurement of the particle's position at time  t 0 {\displaystyle t_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle t_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/02d3006c4190b1939b04d9b9bb21006fb4e6fa4a) is:  p ( x , y , z , t 0 ) = | ψ ( x , y , z , t 0 ) | 2 . {\displaystyle p(x,y,z,t_{0})=|\psi (x,y,z,t_{0})|^{2}.} ![{\\displaystyle p\(x,y,z,t_{0}\)=|\\psi \(x,y,z,t_{0}\)|^{2}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/28264a9b904f707c57e420a5725f6b56985f60de) The Born rule can also be employed to calculate probabilities (for measurements with discrete sets of outcomes) or probability densities (for continuous-valued measurements) for other observables, like momentum, energy, and angular momentum. 
In some applications, this treatment of the Born rule is generalized using [positive-operator-valued measures (POVM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/POVM "POVM"). A POVM is a [measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_\(mathematics\) "Measure \(mathematics\)") whose values are [positive semi-definite operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definiteness_of_a_matrix "Definiteness of a matrix") on a [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space"). POVMs are a generalization of von Neumann measurements and, correspondingly, quantum measurements described by POVMs are a generalization of quantum measurements described by self-adjoint observables. In rough analogy, a POVM is to a PVM what a [mixed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state#Mixed_states "Quantum state") is to a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_state#Pure_states "Quantum state"). Mixed states are needed to specify the state of a subsystem of a larger system (see [purification of quantum state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purification_of_quantum_state "Purification of quantum state")); analogously, POVMs are necessary to describe the effect on a subsystem of a projective measurement performed on a larger system. POVMs are the most general kind of measurement in quantum mechanics and can also be used in [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-2) They are extensively used in the field of [quantum information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_information "Quantum information"). 
In the simplest case of a POVM with a finite number of elements acting on a finite-dimensional [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space"), a POVM is a set of [positive semi-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definiteness_of_a_matrix "Definiteness of a matrix") [matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_\(mathematics\) "Matrix \(mathematics\)") { F i } {\displaystyle \\{F_{i}\\}} ![{\\displaystyle \\{F_{i}\\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a16ebacac98f91296d70fbf635d86447a1acae86) on a Hilbert space  H {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {H}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/19ef4c7b923a5125ac91aa491838a95ee15b804f) that sum to the [identity matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_matrix "Identity matrix"),:[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-mike_ike-3): 90  ∑ i = 1 n F i = I . {\displaystyle \sum _{i=1}^{n}F_{i}=I.} ![{\\displaystyle \\sum _{i=1}^{n}F_{i}=I.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bf72e4661e1c49676a66fca5d6a3803f9b97b988)
The POVM element  F i {\displaystyle F_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle F_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/18f9a5ed62f131c8ad9acdf7f8539f103f1e0ec9) is associated with the measurement outcome  i {\displaystyle i} ![{\\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20), such that the probability of obtaining it when making a measurement on the quantum state  ρ {\displaystyle \rho } ![{\\displaystyle \\rho }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f7d439671d1289b6a816e6af7a304be40608d64) is given by: 
p ( i ) = tr ⁡ ( ρ F i ) , {\displaystyle p(i)=\operatorname {tr} (\rho F_{i}),} ![{\\displaystyle p\(i\)=\\operatorname {tr} \(\\rho F_{i}\),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7912529124276d7f6fae482d72f22623cfd9923e)
where  tr {\displaystyle \operatorname {tr} } ![{\\displaystyle \\operatorname {tr} }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7e779faa4557258ea25e8101f307317fc771a1ef) is the [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_\(linear_algebra\) "Trace \(linear algebra\)") operator. This is the POVM version of the Born rule. When the quantum state being measured is a pure state  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) this formula reduces to:  p ( i ) = tr ⁡ ( | ψ ⟩ ⟨ ψ | F i ) = ⟨ ψ | F i | ψ ⟩ . {\displaystyle p(i)=\operatorname {tr} {\big (}|\psi \rangle \langle \psi |F_{i}{\big )}=\langle \psi |F_{i}|\psi \rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle p\(i\)=\\operatorname {tr} {\\big \(}|\\psi \\rangle \\langle \\psi |F_{i}{\\big \)}=\\langle \\psi |F_{i}|\\psi \\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0e29b1538d39629005c3c63379df1fa1b8a5a2e5)
The Born rule, together with the [unitarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_operator "Unitary operator") of the time evolution operator  e − i H ^ t {\displaystyle e^{-i{\hat {H}}t}} ![{\\displaystyle e^{-i{\\hat {H}}t}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/04b91e5b73fff66a28729fccb9d67e3b001ea86e) (or, equivalently, the [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_\(quantum_mechanics\) "Hamiltonian \(quantum mechanics\)") H ^ {\displaystyle {\hat {H}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\hat {H}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6bb06de5217295d7fbdbf68fb9c5309a513fc99e) being [Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_matrix "Hermitian matrix")), implies the [unitarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitarity_\(physics\) "Unitarity \(physics\)") of the theory: a wave function that is time-evolved by a unitary operator will remain properly normalized. (In the more general case where one considers the time evolution of a [density matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_matrix "Density matrix"), proper normalization is ensured by requiring that the time evolution is a [trace-preserving, completely positive map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_channel "Quantum channel").) 
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Born_rule&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: History")]
The Born rule was formulated by Born in a 1926 paper.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-Zeitschrift-4) In this paper, Born solves the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger_equation "Schrödinger equation") for a scattering problem and, inspired by [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein") and [Einstein's probabilistic rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein%27s_thought_experiments#Background:_Einstein_and_the_quantum "Einstein's thought experiments") for the [photoelectric effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectric_effect "Photoelectric effect"),[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-Nobel-5) concludes, in a footnote, that the Born rule gives the only possible interpretation of the solution. (The main body of the article says that the amplitude "gives the probability" [_bestimmt die Wahrscheinlichkeit_], while the footnote added in proof says that the probability is proportional to the square of its magnitude.) In 1954, together with [Walther Bothe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Bothe "Walther Bothe"), Born was awarded the [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics "Nobel Prize in Physics") for this and other work.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-Nobel-5) [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann") discussed the application of [spectral theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_theory "Spectral theory") to Born's rule in his 1932 book.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-Grundlagen-6)
### Derivation from more basic principles
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Born_rule&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Derivation from more basic principles")]
[Gleason's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gleason%27s_theorem "Gleason's theorem") shows that the Born rule can be derived from the usual mathematical representation of measurements in quantum physics together with the assumption of [non-contextuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_contextuality "Quantum contextuality"). [Andrew M. Gleason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_M._Gleason "Andrew M. Gleason") first proved the theorem in 1957,[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-gleason1957-7) prompted by a question posed by [George W. Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey "George Mackey").[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-chernoff2009-9) This theorem was historically significant for the role it played in showing that wide classes of [hidden-variable theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden-variable_theory "Hidden-variable theory") are inconsistent with quantum physics.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-mermin1993-10)
Several other researchers have also tried to derive the Born rule from more basic principles. A number of derivations have been proposed in the context of the [many-worlds interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Many-worlds_interpretation "Many-worlds interpretation"). These include the decision-theory approach pioneered by [David Deutsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Deutsch "David Deutsch")[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-11) and later developed by [Hilary Greaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilary_Greaves "Hilary Greaves")[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-12) and David Wallace;[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-13) and an "envariance" approach by [Wojciech H. Zurek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wojciech_H._Zurek "Wojciech H. Zurek").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-14) These proofs have, however, been criticized as circular.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-15) In 2018, an approach based on self-locating uncertainty was suggested by Charles Sebens and [Sean M. Carroll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sean_M._Carroll "Sean M. Carroll");[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-16) this has also been criticized.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-17) In 2019, Lluís Masanes, Thomas Galley, and Markus Müller proposed a derivation based on postulates including the possibility of state estimation.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-18)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-19) In 2021, [Simon Saunders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Saunders "Simon Saunders") produced a branch counting derivation of the Born rule. The crucial feature of this approach is to define the branches so that they all have the same magnitude or [2-norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norm_\(mathematics\) "Norm \(mathematics\)"). The ratios of the numbers of branches thus defined give the probabilities of the various outcomes of a measurement, in accordance with the Born rule.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-BranchCounting-20)
It has also been claimed that [pilot-wave theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilot-wave_theory "Pilot-wave theory") can be used to statistically derive the Born rule, though this remains controversial.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-21)
Within the [QBist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Bayesianism "Quantum Bayesianism") interpretation of quantum theory, the Born rule is seen as an extension of the normative principle of [coherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherence_\(philosophical_gambling_strategy\) "Coherence \(philosophical gambling strategy\)"), which ensures self-consistency of probability assessments across a whole set of such assessments. It can be shown that an agent who thinks they are gambling on the outcomes of measurements on a sufficiently quantum-like system but refuses to use the Born rule when placing their bets is vulnerable to a [Dutch book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_book "Dutch book").[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Born_rule#cite_note-22)
