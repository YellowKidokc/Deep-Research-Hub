**Information theory** is the mathematical study of the [quantification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantification_\(science\) "Quantification \(science\)"), [storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_storage "Data storage"), and [communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications "Telecommunications") of a particular type of mathematically defined [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information"). The field was established and formalized by [Claude Shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Shannon "Claude Shannon") in the 1940s,[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-1) though early contributions were made in the 1920s through the works of [Harry Nyquist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Nyquist "Harry Nyquist") and [Ralph Hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Hartley "Ralph Hartley"). It is at the intersection of [electronic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_engineering "Electronic engineering"), [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics "Statistics"), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science "Computer science"), [neurobiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroscience "Neuroscience"), [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), and [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering "Electrical engineering").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-:2-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-:3-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-:0-4)
As a simple example, if one flips a fair coin and does not know the outcome (heads or tails), then they lack a certain amount of information. If one looks at the coin, they will know the outcome and gain that same amount of information. For a fair coin, the probability of either heads or tails is 1/2 and that amount of information can be expressed as  − log 2 ⁡ ( 1 / 2 ) {\displaystyle -\log _{2}(1/2)} ![{\\displaystyle -\\log _{2}\(1/2\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/33e01293d03ce1ddcb4450bab08b3b31ef4a0cf1) = 1 bit of information. 
A key measure in information theory is [information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy "Information entropy"). Entropy is equal to the lack of information about a [random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variable "Random variable") or the outcome of a [random process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_process "Random process"). In the above coin flip example, the entropy in the case where you don't know the outcome is 1 bit. In the case where you do know the outcome, the entropy is zero. As another example, knowing the outcome of a [fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_coin "Fair coin") [coin flip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coin_flip "Coin flip") (which has two equally likely outcomes) provides less information (lower entropy, less uncertainty) than identifying the outcome from a roll of a [die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice "Dice") (which has six equally likely outcomes). A simple way to look at information entropy in bits is approximately the average minimum number of yes/no questions you need to ask in order to recover complete information. (Exact for 2n possibilities and converges to exact for infinite possibilities). For the coin flip example, you only need to ask one question, e.g. "Is it heads?". "Yes" means its heads, "No" means it's tails, and you have your one bit of complete information. 
Some other important measures in information theory are [mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_information "Mutual information"), [channel capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_capacity "Channel capacity"), [error exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_exponent "Error exponent"), and [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy "Relative entropy"). Important sub-fields of information theory include [source coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_coding "Source coding"), [algorithmic complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_complexity_theory "Algorithmic complexity theory"), [algorithmic information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_information_theory "Algorithmic information theory") and [information-theoretic security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information-theoretic_security "Information-theoretic security"). 
Applications of fundamental topics of information theory include source coding/[data compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_compression "Data compression") (e.g. for [ZIP files](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZIP_\(file_format\) "ZIP \(file format\)")), and channel coding/[error detection and correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_detection_and_correction "Error detection and correction") (e.g. for [DSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_subscriber_line "Digital subscriber line")). ## Overview
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview")]
Information theory studies the transmission, processing, extraction, and utilization of [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information"). Abstractly, information can be thought of as the resolution of uncertainty. In the case of communication of information over a noisy channel, this abstract concept was formalized in 1948 by Claude Shannon in a paper entitled _[A Mathematical Theory of Communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mathematical_Theory_of_Communication "A Mathematical Theory of Communication")_ , in which information is thought of as a set of possible messages, and the goal is to send these messages over a noisy channel, and to have the receiver reconstruct the message with low probability of error, in spite of the channel noise. Shannon's main result, the [noisy-channel coding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noisy-channel_coding_theorem "Noisy-channel coding theorem"), showed that, in the limit of many channel uses, the rate of information that is asymptotically achievable is equal to the channel capacity, a quantity dependent merely on the statistics of the channel over which the messages are sent.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Spikes-9)
Coding theory is concerned with finding explicit methods, called _codes_ , for increasing the efficiency and reducing the error rate of data communication over noisy channels to near the channel capacity. These codes can be roughly subdivided into data compression (source coding) and [error-correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correction "Error-correction") (channel coding) techniques. In the latter case, it took many years to find the methods Shannon's work proved were possible.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-24)[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-25)
A third class of information theory codes are [cryptographic algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographic_algorithm "Cryptographic algorithm") (both [codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_\(cryptography\) "Code \(cryptography\)") and [ciphers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cipher "Cipher")). Concepts, methods and results from coding theory and information theory are widely used in cryptography and [cryptanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalysis "Cryptanalysis"),[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-26) such as the [unit ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_\(unit\) "Ban \(unit\)"). 
## Historical background
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Historical background")]
Main article: [History of information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_information_theory "History of information theory")
The landmark event _establishing_ the discipline of information theory and bringing it to immediate worldwide attention was the publication of Claude Shannon's classic paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" in the _[Bell System Technical Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_System_Technical_Journal "Bell System Technical Journal")_ in July and October 1948. Historian [James Gleick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gleick "James Gleick") rated the paper as the most important development of 1948, noting that the paper was "even more profound and more fundamental" than the [transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor "Transistor").[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-FOOTNOTEGleick20113%E2%80%934-27) He came to be known as the "father of information theory".[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-28)[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-29)[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-:1-30) Shannon outlined some of his initial ideas of information theory as early as 1939 in a letter to [Vannevar Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vannevar_Bush "Vannevar Bush").[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-:1-30)
Prior to this paper, limited information-theoretic ideas had been developed at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs "Bell Labs"), all implicitly assuming events of equal probability. Harry Nyquist's 1924 paper, _Certain Factors Affecting Telegraph Speed_ , contains a theoretical section quantifying "intelligence" and the "line speed" at which it can be transmitted by a communication system, giving the relation _W_ = _K_ log _m_ (recalling the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant "Boltzmann constant")), where _W_ is the speed of transmission of intelligence, _m_ is the number of different voltage levels to choose from at each time step, and _K_ is a constant. Ralph Hartley's 1928 paper, _Transmission of Information_ , uses the word _information_ as a measurable quantity, reflecting the receiver's ability to distinguish one [sequence of symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_of_symbols "Sequence of symbols") from any other, thus quantifying information as _H_ = log _S_ _n_ = _n_ log _S_ , where _S_ was the number of possible symbols, and _n_ the number of symbols in a transmission. The unit of information was therefore the [decimal digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_digit "Decimal digit"), which since has sometimes been called the [hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_\(unit\) "Hartley \(unit\)") in his honor as a unit or scale or measure of information. [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing "Alan Turing") in 1940 used similar ideas as part of the statistical analysis of the breaking of the German second world war [Enigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalysis_of_the_Enigma "Cryptanalysis of the Enigma") ciphers.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Much of the mathematics behind information theory with events of different probabilities were developed for the field of [thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamics "Thermodynamics") by [Ludwig Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Boltzmann "Ludwig Boltzmann") and [J. Willard Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Willard_Gibbs "J. Willard Gibbs"). Connections between information-theoretic entropy and thermodynamic entropy, including the important contributions by [Rolf Landauer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolf_Landauer "Rolf Landauer") in the 1960s, are explored in _[Entropy in thermodynamics and information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_in_thermodynamics_and_information_theory "Entropy in thermodynamics and information theory")_.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-31)
In Shannon's revolutionary and groundbreaking paper, the work for which had been substantially completed at Bell Labs by the end of 1944, Shannon for the first time introduced the qualitative and quantitative model of communication as a statistical process underlying information theory, opening with the assertion:[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-32)     "_The fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing at one point, either exactly or approximately, a message selected at another point._ "
With it came the ideas of: 
  * The [information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy "Information entropy") and [redundancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redundancy_\(information_theory\) "Redundancy \(information theory\)") of a source, and its relevance through the [source coding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_coding_theorem "Source coding theorem");
  * The [mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_information "Mutual information"), and the channel capacity of a noisy channel, including the promise of perfect loss-free communication given by the noisy-channel coding theorem;
  * The practical result of the [Shannon–Hartley law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon%E2%80%93Hartley_law "Shannon–Hartley law") for the channel capacity of a [Gaussian channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_channel "Gaussian channel"); as well as
  * The [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit "Bit")—a new way of seeing the most fundamental unit of information.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Information theory is based on [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") and statistics, where [quantified information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantities_of_information "Quantities of information") is usually described in terms of bits. Information theory often concerns itself with measures of information of the distributions associated with random variables. One of the most important measures is called [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_\(information_theory\) "Entropy \(information theory\)"), which forms the building block of many other measures. Entropy allows quantification of measure of information in a single random variable.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-33)
Another useful concept is mutual information defined on two random variables, which describes the measure of information in common between those variables, which can be used to describe their correlation. The former quantity is a property of the probability distribution of a random variable and gives a limit on the rate at which data generated by independent samples with the given distribution can be reliably compressed. The latter is a property of the joint distribution of two random variables, and is the maximum rate of reliable communication across a noisy [channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_channel "Communication channel") in the limit of long block lengths, when the channel statistics are determined by the joint distribution. 
The choice of logarithmic base in the following formulae determines the [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement "Units of measurement") of information entropy that is used. A common unit of information is the bit or [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_\(unit\) "Shannon \(unit\)"), based on the [binary logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_logarithm "Binary logarithm"). Other units include the [nat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nat_\(unit\) "Nat \(unit\)"), which is based on the [natural logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_logarithm "Natural logarithm"), and the [decimal digit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deciban "Deciban"), which is based on the [common logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_logarithm "Common logarithm"). 
In what follows, an expression of the form _p_ log _p_ is considered by convention to be equal to zero whenever _p_ = 0. This is justified because  lim p → 0 + p log ⁡ p = 0 {\displaystyle \lim _{p\rightarrow 0^{+}}p\log p=0} ![{\\displaystyle \\lim _{p\\rightarrow 0^{+}}p\\log p=0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/973eee89cf21743e7f29add330fdda601d23eb16) for any logarithmic base.

A less probable symbol has a larger surprisal, meaning its occurrence provides more information.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-MacKay_Ch2-36) The entropy  H {\displaystyle H} ![{\\displaystyle H}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/75a9edddcca2f782014371f75dca39d7e13a9c1b) is the weighted average of the surprisal of all possible symbols from the source's probability distribution:[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Reza_Ch3-38)[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Stone_InfoTheory-39)      H ( X ) = E X [ I ( x ) ] = ∑ i p i I ( p i ) = − ∑ i p i log 2 ⁡ ( p i ) {\displaystyle H(X)\ =\ \mathbb {E} _{X}[I(x)]\ =\ \sum _{i}p_{i}I(p_{i})\ =\ -\sum _{i}p_{i}\log _{2}(p_{i})} ![{\\displaystyle H\(X\)\\ =\\ \\mathbb {E} _{X}\[I\(x\)\]\\ =\\ \\sum _{i}p_{i}I\(p_{i}\)\\ =\\ -\\sum _{i}p_{i}\\log _{2}\(p_{i}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9e22bdeddcc03ace45a0db24c907bdafdac5a90c)
Intuitively, the entropy  H ( X ) {\displaystyle H(X)} ![{\\displaystyle H\(X\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bd232b6fb5ea803efc1154d2efb0c3fe00a4531b) of a [discrete random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_random_variable "Discrete random variable") _X_ is a measure of the amount of _uncertainty_ associated with the value of  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) when only its distribution is known.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-CoverThomas_Ch2-34) A high entropy indicates the outcomes are more evenly distributed, making the result harder to predict.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Schneider_InfoTheoryPrimer-40)
For example, if one transmits 1000 bits (0s and 1s), and the value of each of these bits is known to the receiver (has a specific value with certainty) ahead of transmission, no information is transmitted. If, however, each bit is independently and equally likely to be 0 or 1, 1000 shannons of information (more often called bits) have been transmitted.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-CoverThomas_Ch1-41)
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/22/Binary_entropy_plot.svg/250px-Binary_entropy_plot.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Binary_entropy_plot.svg)The entropy of a [Bernoulli trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_trial "Bernoulli trial") as a function of success probability, often called the _[binary entropy function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_entropy_function "Binary entropy function")_ , _H_ b(_p_). The entropy is maximized at 1 bit per trial when the two possible outcomes are equally probable, as in an unbiased coin toss.
#### Properties
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Properties")]
A key property of entropy is that it is maximized when all the messages in the message space are [equiprobable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equiprobable "Equiprobable"). For a source with n possible symbols, where  p i = 1 n {\textstyle p_{i}={\frac {1}{n}}} ![{\\textstyle p_{i}={\\frac {1}{n}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/07dd59ee1d01ed88696d468391a2e5b8c11987ad) for all  i {\displaystyle i} ![{\\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20), the entropy is given by:[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-CoverThomas_Ch2_Theorem2.6.2-42)      H ( X ) = log 2 ⁡ ( n ) {\displaystyle H(X)=\log _{2}(n)} ![{\\displaystyle H\(X\)=\\log _{2}\(n\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ed28a012d3148e39270c94f7ba1cd03cdd5bb178)
This maximum value represents the most unpredictable state.[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Reza_Ch3-38)
For a source that emits a sequence of  N {\displaystyle N} ![{\\displaystyle N}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f5e3890c981ae85503089652feb48b191b57aae3) symbols that are [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed "Independent and identically distributed") (i.i.d.), the total entropy of the message is  N ⋅ H {\displaystyle N\cdot H} ![{\\displaystyle N\\cdot H}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/502ae1621cc63638c9722afe67afa4370b068041) bits. If the source data symbols are identically distributed but not independent, the entropy of a message of length  N {\displaystyle N} ![{\\displaystyle N}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f5e3890c981ae85503089652feb48b191b57aae3) will be less than  N ⋅ H {\displaystyle N\cdot H} ![{\\displaystyle N\\cdot H}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/502ae1621cc63638c9722afe67afa4370b068041).[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-CoverThomas_Ch2_Asymptotic-43)[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-MacKay_Ch4-44)
#### Units
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Units")]
The choice of the logarithmic base in the entropy formula determines the unit of entropy used:[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-MacKay_Ch2-36)[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Reza_Ch3-38)
  * A **[base-2 logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base-2_logarithm "Base-2 logarithm")** (as shown in the main formula) measures entropy in **[bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit "Bit")** per symbol. This unit is also sometimes called the **[shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_\(unit\) "Shannon \(unit\)")** in honor of Claude Shannon.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-CoverThomas_Ch2-34)
  * A **[Natural logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_logarithm "Natural logarithm")** (base _[e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_\(mathematical_constant\) "E \(mathematical constant\)")_) measures entropy in **[nats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nat_\(unit\) "Nat \(unit\)")** per symbol. This is often used in theoretical analysis as it avoids the need for scaling constants (like ln 2) in derivations.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Leung_Nats-45)
  * Other bases are also possible. A **base-10 logarithm** measures entropy in decimal digits, or **[hartleys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_\(unit\) "Hartley \(unit\)")** , per symbol.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Arndt_InfoTheory-35) A **base-256 logarithm** measures entropy in **[bytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte "Byte")** per symbol, since 28 = 256.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-MacKay_Ch2_Bytes-46)


#### Binary Entropy Function
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Binary Entropy Function")]
The special case of information entropy for a random variable with two outcomes (a [Bernoulli trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_trial "Bernoulli trial")) is the **binary entropy function**. This is typically calculated using a base-2 logarithm, and its unit is the [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_\(unit\) "Shannon \(unit\)").[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-CoverThomas_Binary-47) If one outcome has probability p, the other has probability _1_ − _p_. The entropy is given by:[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-MacKay_Binary-48)      H b ( p ) = − p log 2 ⁡ p − ( 1 − p ) log 2 ⁡ ( 1 − p ) {\displaystyle H_{\mathrm {b} }(p)=-p\log _{2}p-(1-p)\log _{2}(1-p)} ![{\\displaystyle H_{\\mathrm {b} }\(p\)=-p\\log _{2}p-\(1-p\)\\log _{2}\(1-p\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/118d0a7ae01313db60949f1cea18711a934ea31e)
This function is depicted in the plot shown above, reaching its maximum of 1 bit when _p_ = 0.5, corresponding to the highest uncertainty. 
### Joint entropy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Joint entropy")]
The _[joint entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_entropy "Joint entropy")_ of two discrete random variables _X_ and _Y_ is merely the entropy of their pairing: (_X_ , _Y_). This implies that if _X_ and _Y_ are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence "Statistical independence"), then their joint entropy is the sum of their individual entropies. 
For example, if (_X_ , _Y_) represents the position of a chess piece— _X_ the row and _Y_ the column, then the joint entropy of the row of the piece and the column of the piece will be the entropy of the position of the piece.       H ( X , Y ) = E X , Y [ − log ⁡ p ( x , y ) ] = − ∑ x , y p ( x , y ) log ⁡ p ( x , y ) {\displaystyle H(X,Y)=\mathbb {E} _{X,Y}[-\log p(x,y)]=-\sum _{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x,y)\,} ![{\\displaystyle H\(X,Y\)=\\mathbb {E} _{X,Y}\[-\\log p\(x,y\)\]=-\\sum _{x,y}p\(x,y\)\\log p\(x,y\)\\,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a10572b1a21187833168e50a998f66856658674d)
Despite similar notation, joint entropy should not be confused with _[cross-entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-entropy "Cross-entropy")_. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Conditional entropy \(equivocation\)")]
The _[conditional entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_entropy "Conditional entropy")_ or _conditional uncertainty_ of _X_ given random variable _Y_ (also called the _equivocation_ of _X_ about _Y_) is the average conditional entropy over _Y_ :[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-FOOTNOTEAsh1990-49)      H ( X | Y ) = E Y [ H ( X | y ) ] = − ∑ y ∈ Y p ( y ) ∑ x ∈ X p ( x | y ) log ⁡ p ( x | y ) = − ∑ x , y p ( x , y ) log ⁡ p ( x | y ) . {\displaystyle H(X|Y)=\mathbb {E} _{Y}[H(X|y)]=-\sum _{y\in Y}p(y)\sum _{x\in X}p(x|y)\log p(x|y)=-\sum _{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x|y).} ![{\\displaystyle H\(X|Y\)=\\mathbb {E} _{Y}\[H\(X|y\)\]=-\\sum _{y\\in Y}p\(y\)\\sum _{x\\in X}p\(x|y\)\\log p\(x|y\)=-\\sum _{x,y}p\(x,y\)\\log p\(x|y\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7bdf0f6f2421309afb521010957e1e556669e479)
Because entropy can be conditioned on a random variable or on that random variable being a certain value, care should be taken not to confuse these two definitions of conditional entropy, the former of which is in more common use. A basic property of this form of conditional entropy is that:       H ( X | Y ) = H ( X , Y ) − H ( Y ) . {\displaystyle H(X|Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y).\,} ![{\\displaystyle H\(X|Y\)=H\(X,Y\)-H\(Y\).\\,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/55a800109119215c303f549c60dd667d707ff58c)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Mutual information \(transinformation\)")]
_[Mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_information "Mutual information")_ measures the amount of information that can be obtained about one random variable by observing another. It is important in communication where it can be used to maximize the amount of information shared between sent and received signals. The mutual information of _X_ relative to _Y_ is given by:       I ( X ; Y ) = E X , Y [ S I ( x , y ) ] = ∑ x , y p ( x , y ) log ⁡ p ( x , y ) p ( x ) p ( y ) {\displaystyle I(X;Y)=\mathbb {E} _{X,Y}[SI(x,y)]=\sum _{x,y}p(x,y)\log {\frac {p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}}} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X;Y\)=\\mathbb {E} _{X,Y}\[SI\(x,y\)\]=\\sum _{x,y}p\(x,y\)\\log {\\frac {p\(x,y\)}{p\(x\)\\,p\(y\)}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7d86fba391a52b75e1cf36dae6bfaa1ccbccf461)
where SI (_S_ pecific mutual Information) is the [pointwise mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointwise_mutual_information "Pointwise mutual information"). 
A basic property of the mutual information is that:       I ( X ; Y ) = H ( X ) − H ( X | Y ) . {\displaystyle I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X|Y).\,} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X;Y\)=H\(X\)-H\(X|Y\).\\,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ff968f71052c9abe1da3e3e414327077de6f2721)
That is, knowing  Y {\textstyle Y} ![{\\textstyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6222becc4f0c5effa012e5335b170575fdbbaad3), we can save an average of _I_(_X_ ; _Y_) bits in encoding _ X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab)_ compared to not knowing  Y {\displaystyle Y} ![{\\displaystyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/961d67d6b454b4df2301ac571808a3538b3a6d3f). 
Mutual information is [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_function "Symmetric function"):       I ( X ; Y ) = I ( Y ; X ) = H ( X ) + H ( Y ) − H ( X , Y ) . {\displaystyle I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y).\,} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X;Y\)=I\(Y;X\)=H\(X\)+H\(Y\)-H\(X,Y\).\\,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d8c8ca328d60dbd8eaab8833e9ec1e882daaa3b6)
Mutual information can be expressed as the average Kullback–Leibler divergence (information gain) between the [posterior probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_probability "Posterior probability") of _ X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab)_ given the value of  Y {\textstyle Y} ![{\\textstyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6222becc4f0c5effa012e5335b170575fdbbaad3) and the [prior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_probability "Prior probability") on  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab):       I ( X ; Y ) = E p ( y ) [ D K L ( p ( X | Y = y ) ‖ p ( X ) ) ] . {\displaystyle I(X;Y)=\mathbb {E} _{p(y)}[D_{\mathrm {KL} }(p(X|Y=y)\|p(X))].} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X;Y\)=\\mathbb {E} _{p\(y\)}\[D_{\\mathrm {KL} }\(p\(X|Y=y\)\\|p\(X\)\)\].}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d0d90fc72e685981072e77a3933c7ad6c4c83d13)
In other words, this is a measure of how much, on the average, the probability distribution on  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) will change if we are given the value of _ Y {\textstyle Y} ![{\\textstyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6222becc4f0c5effa012e5335b170575fdbbaad3)_. This is often recalculated as the divergence from the product of the marginal distributions to the actual joint distribution:       I ( X ; Y ) = D K L ( p ( X , Y ) ‖ p ( X ) p ( Y ) ) . {\displaystyle I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm {KL} }(p(X,Y)\|p(X)p(Y)).} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X;Y\)=D_{\\mathrm {KL} }\(p\(X,Y\)\\|p\(X\)p\(Y\)\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/03b108331b20bad2137992fa16f9fdd48906072d)
Mutual information is closely related to the [log-likelihood ratio test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood-ratio_test "Likelihood-ratio test") in the context of contingency tables and the [multinomial distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multinomial_distribution "Multinomial distribution") and to [Pearson's χ2 test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearson%27s_chi-squared_test "Pearson's chi-squared test"): mutual information can be considered a statistic for assessing independence between a pair of variables, and has a well-specified asymptotic distribution. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Kullback–Leibler divergence \(information gain\)")]
The _[Kullback–Leibler divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence "Kullback–Leibler divergence")_ (or _information divergence_ , _information gain_ , or _relative entropy_) is a way of comparing two distributions: a "true" [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") ⁠ p ( X ) {\displaystyle p(X)} ![{\\displaystyle p\(X\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f7425278aab7b6ceb8ddb54fa5ce71d2cf52d28f)⁠, and an arbitrary probability distribution ⁠ q ( X ) {\displaystyle q(X)} ![{\\displaystyle q\(X\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/352e294916cf0c2b425f097071daf0da1b47123b)⁠. If we compress data in a manner that assumes ⁠ q ( X ) {\displaystyle q(X)} ![{\\displaystyle q\(X\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/352e294916cf0c2b425f097071daf0da1b47123b)⁠ is the distribution underlying some data, when, in reality, ⁠ p ( X ) {\displaystyle p(X)} ![{\\displaystyle p\(X\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f7425278aab7b6ceb8ddb54fa5ce71d2cf52d28f)⁠ is the correct distribution, the Kullback–Leibler divergence is the number of average additional bits per datum necessary for compression. It is thus defined       D K L ( p ( X ) ‖ q ( X ) ) = ∑ x ∈ X − p ( x ) log ⁡ q ( x ) − ∑ x ∈ X − p ( x ) log ⁡ p ( x ) = ∑ x ∈ X p ( x ) log ⁡ p ( x ) q ( x ) . {\displaystyle D_{\mathrm {KL} }(p(X)\|q(X))=\sum _{x\in X}-p(x)\log {q(x)}\,-\,\sum _{x\in X}-p(x)\log {p(x)}=\sum _{x\in X}p(x)\log {\frac {p(x)}{q(x)}}.} ![{\\displaystyle D_{\\mathrm {KL} }\(p\(X\)\\|q\(X\)\)=\\sum _{x\\in X}-p\(x\)\\log {q\(x\)}\\,-\\,\\sum _{x\\in X}-p\(x\)\\log {p\(x\)}=\\sum _{x\\in X}p\(x\)\\log {\\frac {p\(x\)}{q\(x\)}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b6882199003743a8f6d6d0d4723b2bd2e496f98)
Although it is sometimes used as a 'distance metric', KL divergence is not a true [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_\(mathematics\) "Metric \(mathematics\)") since it is not symmetric and does not satisfy the [triangle inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_inequality "Triangle inequality") (making it a semi-quasimetric). 
Another interpretation of the KL divergence is the "unnecessary surprise" introduced by a prior from the truth: suppose a number _ X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab)_ is about to be drawn randomly from a discrete set with probability distribution ⁠ p ( x ) {\displaystyle p(x)} ![{\\displaystyle p\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8cb7afced134ef75572e5314a5d278c2d644f438)⁠. If Alice knows the true distribution ⁠ p ( x ) {\displaystyle p(x)} ![{\\displaystyle p\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8cb7afced134ef75572e5314a5d278c2d644f438)⁠, while Bob believes (has a [prior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_probability "Prior probability")) that the distribution is ⁠ q ( x ) {\displaystyle q(x)} ![{\\displaystyle q\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c38bbafe34a043d284f19231b946a76c0a4b16b4)⁠, then Bob will be more [surprised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_content "Information content") than Alice, on average, upon seeing the value of  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab). The KL divergence is the (objective) expected value of Bob's (subjective) [surprisal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_content "Information content") minus Alice's surprisal, measured in bits if the _log_ is in base 2. In this way, the extent to which Bob's prior is "wrong" can be quantified in terms of how "unnecessarily surprised" it is expected to make him. 
### Directed Information
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Directed Information")]
_[Directed information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_information "Directed information")_ ,  I ( X n → Y n ) {\displaystyle I(X^{n}\to Y^{n})} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X^{n}\\to Y^{n}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/779afffca11b308d71883749356d24da2f8e3659), is an information theory measure that quantifies the [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information") flow from the random process  X n = { X 1 , X 2 , … , X n } {\displaystyle X^{n}=\\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots ,X_{n}\\}} ![{\\displaystyle X^{n}=\\{X_{1},X_{2},\\dots ,X_{n}\\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8d2a01bc29253e3a34d2b4efc98b2ca157877660) to the random process  Y n = { Y 1 , Y 2 , … , Y n } {\displaystyle Y^{n}=\\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots ,Y_{n}\\}} ![{\\displaystyle Y^{n}=\\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\\dots ,Y_{n}\\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/34055d31664058ce10332b0576dadc2e5cbe779c). The term _directed information_ was coined by [James Massey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Massey "James Massey") and is defined as:       I ( X n → Y n ) ≜ ∑ i = 1 n I ( X i ; Y i | Y i − 1 ) {\displaystyle I(X^{n}\to Y^{n})\ \triangleq \ \sum _{i=1}^{n}I(X^{i};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X^{n}\\to Y^{n}\)\\ \\triangleq \\ \\sum _{i=1}^{n}I\(X^{i};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dd0b7450fe65a7dfc15b35b5baa9eeaf1749c3e2),
where  I ( X i ; Y i | Y i − 1 ) {\displaystyle I(X^{i};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X^{i};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/262d572ffcff0f5ccddb6e850c54d63abbfda28c) is the [conditional mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_mutual_information "Conditional mutual information") I ( X 1 , X 2 , . . . , X i ; Y i | Y 1 , Y 2 , . . . , Y i − 1 ) {\displaystyle I(X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{i};Y_{i}|Y_{1},Y_{2},...,Y_{i-1})} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{i};Y_{i}|Y_{1},Y_{2},...,Y_{i-1}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4d7743dc75d5d8ef30a1e1da83165a5bca2feef6). 
In contrast to _mutual_ information, _directed_ information is not symmetric. The  I ( X n → Y n ) {\displaystyle I(X^{n}\to Y^{n})} ![{\\displaystyle I\(X^{n}\\to Y^{n}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/779afffca11b308d71883749356d24da2f8e3659) measures the information bits that are transmitted causally[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] from  X n {\displaystyle X^{n}} ![{\\displaystyle X^{n}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/268db8293666fefd75cfb00513706171948edf09) to  Y n {\displaystyle Y^{n}} ![{\\displaystyle Y^{n}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a6e8fe1b832e2088d10ef7c91d815a0d21aad4ca). The Directed information has many applications in problems where [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality "Causality") plays an important role such as [capacity of channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_capacity "Channel capacity") with feedback,[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-massey-50)[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-51) capacity of discrete [memoryless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoryless "Memoryless") networks with feedback,[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-52) [gambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sports_gambling "Sports gambling") with causal side information,[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-53) [compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_compression "Data compression") with causal side information,[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-54) [real-time control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_control "Real-time control") communication settings,[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-55)[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-56) and in statistical physics.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-57)
### Other quantities
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Other quantities")]
Other important information theoretic quantities include the [Rényi entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9nyi_entropy "Rényi entropy") and the [Tsallis entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsallis_entropy "Tsallis entropy") (generalizations of the concept of entropy), [differential entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_entropy "Differential entropy") (a generalization of quantities of information to continuous distributions), and the [conditional mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_mutual_information "Conditional mutual information"). Also, [pragmatic information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatic_information "Pragmatic information") has been proposed as a measure of how much information has been used in making a decision.

Main article: [Coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coding_theory "Coding theory")
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5a/CDSCRATCHES.jpg/250px-CDSCRATCHES.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CDSCRATCHES.jpg)A picture showing scratches on the readable surface of a CD-R. Music and data CDs are coded using error correcting codes and thus can still be read even if they have minor scratches using [error detection and correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_detection_and_correction "Error detection and correction").
Coding theory is one of the most important and direct applications of information theory. It can be subdivided into source coding theory and channel coding theory. Using a statistical description for data, information theory quantifies the number of bits needed to describe the data, which is the information entropy of the source. 
  * [Data compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_compression "Data compression") (source coding): There are two formulations for the compression problem: 
    * [Lossless data compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossless_data_compression "Lossless data compression"): the data must be reconstructed exactly;
    * [Lossy data compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossy_data_compression "Lossy data compression"): allocates bits needed to reconstruct the data, within a specified fidelity level measured by a distortion function. This subset of information theory is called _[rate–distortion theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate%E2%80%93distortion_theory "Rate–distortion theory")_.
  * [Error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error-correcting_code "Error-correcting code") (channel coding): While data compression removes as much redundancy as possible, an error-correcting code adds just the right kind of redundancy (i.e., error correction) needed to transmit the data efficiently and faithfully across a noisy channel.


This division of coding theory into compression and transmission is justified by the information transmission theorems, or source–channel separation theorems that justify the use of bits as the universal currency for information in many contexts. However, these theorems only hold in the situation where one transmitting user wishes to communicate to one receiving user. In scenarios with more than one transmitter (the multiple-access channel), more than one receiver (the [broadcast channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_channel "Broadcast channel")) or intermediary "helpers" (the [relay channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relay_channel "Relay channel")), or more general [networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network "Computer network"), compression followed by transmission may no longer be optimal. 
### Source theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Source theory")]
Any process that generates successive messages can be considered a _[source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_source "Communication source")_ of information. A memoryless source is one in which each message is an [independent identically distributed random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_identically_distributed_random_variables "Independent identically distributed random variables"), whereas the properties of [ergodicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_theory "Ergodic theory") and [stationarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_process "Stationary process") impose less restrictive constraints. All such sources are [stochastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_process "Stochastic process"). These terms are well studied in their own right outside information theory. 
#### Rate
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Rate")]
Information _[rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_rate "Entropy rate")_ is the average entropy per symbol. For memoryless sources, this is merely the entropy of each symbol, while, in the case of a stationary stochastic process, it is:       r = lim n → ∞ H ( X n | X n − 1 , X n − 2 , X n − 3 , … ) ; {\displaystyle r=\lim _{n\to \infty }H(X_{n}|X_{n-1},X_{n-2},X_{n-3},\ldots );} ![{\\displaystyle r=\\lim _{n\\to \\infty }H\(X_{n}|X_{n-1},X_{n-2},X_{n-3},\\ldots \);}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cd2344c3096dbc21ce050a9b70a4ddf2062ff41e)
that is, the conditional entropy of a symbol given all the previous symbols generated. For the more general case of a process that is not necessarily stationary, the _average rate_ is:       r = lim n → ∞ 1 n H ( X 1 , X 2 , … X n ) ; {\displaystyle r=\lim _{n\to \infty }{\frac {1}{n}}H(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n});} ![{\\displaystyle r=\\lim _{n\\to \\infty }{\\frac {1}{n}}H\(X_{1},X_{2},\\dots X_{n}\);}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/324db7538097abf899fe20ce454b645c2e7ee5ba)
that is, the limit of the joint entropy per symbol. For stationary sources, these two expressions give the same result.[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-58)
The [information rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_rate "Information rate") is defined as:       r = lim n → ∞ 1 n I ( X 1 , X 2 , … X n ; Y 1 , Y 2 , … Y n ) ; {\displaystyle r=\lim _{n\to \infty }{\frac {1}{n}}I(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n};Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots Y_{n});} ![{\\displaystyle r=\\lim _{n\\to \\infty }{\\frac {1}{n}}I\(X_{1},X_{2},\\dots X_{n};Y_{1},Y_{2},\\dots Y_{n}\);}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a0b4dd61deebad94d93705b144f5350f604105b2)
It is common in information theory to speak of the "rate" or "entropy" of a language. This is appropriate, for example, when the source of information is English prose. The rate of a source of information is related to its redundancy and how well it can be compressed, the subject of _source coding_. 
### Channel capacity
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Channel capacity")]
Main article: [Channel capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_capacity "Channel capacity")
Communications over a channel is the primary motivation of information theory. However, channels often fail to produce exact reconstruction of a signal; noise, periods of silence, and other forms of signal corruption often degrade quality. 
Consider the communications process over a discrete channel. A simple model of the process is shown below:       → Message W Encoder f n → E n c o d e d s e q u e n c e X n Channel p ( y | x ) → R e c e i v e d s e q u e n c e Y n Decoder g n → E s t i m a t e d m e s s a g e W ^ {\displaystyle {\xrightarrow[{\text{Message}}]{W}}{\begin{array}{|c| }\hline {\text{Encoder}}\\\f_{n}\\\\\hline \end{array}}{\xrightarrow[{\mathrm {Encoded \atop sequence} }]{X^{n}}}{\begin{array}{|c| }\hline {\text{Channel}}\\\p(y|x)\\\\\hline \end{array}}{\xrightarrow[{\mathrm {Received \atop sequence} }]{Y^{n}}}{\begin{array}{|c| }\hline {\text{Decoder}}\\\g_{n}\\\\\hline \end{array}}{\xrightarrow[{\mathrm {Estimated \atop message} }]{\hat {W}}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\xrightarrow\[{\\text{Message}}\]{W}}{\\begin{array}{|c| }\\hline {\\text{Encoder}}\\\\f_{n}\\\\\\hline \\end{array}}{\\xrightarrow\[{\\mathrm {Encoded \\atop sequence} }\]{X^{n}}}{\\begin{array}{|c| }\\hline {\\text{Channel}}\\\\p\(y|x\)\\\\\\hline \\end{array}}{\\xrightarrow\[{\\mathrm {Received \\atop sequence} }\]{Y^{n}}}{\\begin{array}{|c| }\\hline {\\text{Decoder}}\\\\g_{n}\\\\\\hline \\end{array}}{\\xrightarrow\[{\\mathrm {Estimated \\atop message} }\]{\\hat {W}}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3e5366c809cb41ab57f4364b475895f13a9dd328)
Here  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) represents the space of messages transmitted, and  Y {\textstyle Y} ![{\\textstyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6222becc4f0c5effa012e5335b170575fdbbaad3) the space of messages received during a unit time over our channel. Let _p_(_y_ |_x_) be the [conditional probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_probability "Conditional probability") distribution function of _ Y {\textstyle Y} ![{\\textstyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6222becc4f0c5effa012e5335b170575fdbbaad3)_ given  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab). We will consider _p_(_y_ |_x_) to be an inherent fixed property of our communications channel (representing the nature of the _[noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_noise "Signal noise")_ of our channel). Then the joint distribution of _ X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab)_ and _ Y {\textstyle Y} ![{\\textstyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6222becc4f0c5effa012e5335b170575fdbbaad3)_ is completely determined by our channel and by our choice of _f_(_x_), the marginal distribution of messages we choose to send over the channel. Under these constraints, we would like to maximize the rate of information, or the _[signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_\(electrical_engineering\) "Signal \(electrical engineering\)")_ , we can communicate over the channel. The appropriate measure for this is the mutual information, and this maximum mutual information is called the _channel capacity_ and is given by:       C = max f I ( X ; Y ) . {\displaystyle C=\max _{f}I(X;Y).\\!} ![{\\displaystyle C=\\max _{f}I\(X;Y\).\\!}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6d6698ae5326003e7530add14e24f875f834fb68)
This capacity has the following property related to communicating at information rate _R_ (where _R_ is usually bits per symbol). For any information rate _R_ < _C_ and coding error _ε_ > 0, for large enough _N_ , there exists a code of length _N_ and rate ≥ R and a decoding algorithm, such that the maximal probability of block error is ≤ _ε_ ; that is, it is always possible to transmit with arbitrarily small block error. In addition, for any rate _R_ > _C_ , it is impossible to transmit with arbitrarily small block error. 
_[Channel coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_code "Channel code")_ is concerned with finding such nearly optimal codes that can be used to transmit data over a noisy channel with a small coding error at a rate near the channel capacity. 
#### Capacity of particular channel models
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Capacity of particular channel models")]
  * A continuous-time analog communications channel subject to [Gaussian noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_noise "Gaussian noise")—see [Shannon–Hartley theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon%E2%80%93Hartley_theorem "Shannon–Hartley theorem").
  * A [binary symmetric channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_symmetric_channel "Binary symmetric channel") (BSC) with crossover probability _p_ is a binary input, binary output channel that flips the input bit with probability _p_. The BSC has a capacity of 1 − _H_ b(_p_) bits per channel use, where _H_ b is the binary entropy function to the base-2 logarithm:

        [![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b5/Binary_symmetric_channel.svg/250px-Binary_symmetric_channel.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Binary_symmetric_channel.svg)
  * A [binary erasure channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_erasure_channel "Binary erasure channel") (BEC) with erasure probability _p_ is a binary input, ternary output channel. The possible channel outputs are 0, 1, and a third symbol 'e' called an erasure. The erasure represents complete loss of information about an input bit. The capacity of the BEC is 1 − _p_ bits per channel use.

#### Channels with memory and directed information
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Channels with memory and directed information")]
In practice many channels have memory. Namely, at time  i {\displaystyle i} ![{\\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20) the channel is given by the conditional probability P ( y i | x i , x i − 1 , x i − 2 , . . . , x 1 , y i − 1 , y i − 2 , . . . , y 1 ) {\displaystyle P(y_{i}|x_{i},x_{i-1},x_{i-2},...,x_{1},y_{i-1},y_{i-2},...,y_{1})} ![{\\displaystyle P\(y_{i}|x_{i},x_{i-1},x_{i-2},...,x_{1},y_{i-1},y_{i-2},...,y_{1}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a5485007c8ac5f922152674775f7c876c4bcb73d). It is often more comfortable to use the notation  x i = ( x i , x i − 1 , x i − 2 , . . . , x 1 ) {\displaystyle x^{i}=(x_{i},x_{i-1},x_{i-2},...,x_{1})} ![{\\displaystyle x^{i}=\(x_{i},x_{i-1},x_{i-2},...,x_{1}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9d94c204f6ab6c6678a43f8bff7b1f3c9091895a) and the channel become  P ( y i | x i , y i − 1 ) {\displaystyle P(y_{i}|x^{i},y^{i-1})} ![{\\displaystyle P\(y_{i}|x^{i},y^{i-1}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8789df9402aa82b9f55eb6cd52bccfef98ae3ce1). In such a case the capacity is given by the [mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_information "Mutual information") rate when there is no feedback available and the [Directed information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_information "Directed information") rate in the case that either there is feedback or not[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-massey-50)[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-59) (if there is no feedback the directed information equals the mutual information). 
### Fungible information
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Fungible information")]
**Fungible information** is the [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information") for which the means of [encoding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encoding "Encoding") is not important.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-60) Classical information theorists and computer scientists are mainly concerned with information of this sort. It is sometimes referred as speakable information.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-61)

### Network Physiology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Network Physiology")]
Information theory concepts, methods and approaches have broad applications in Network Physiology[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-62)[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-63)[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-64), a field which provides a quantitative framework, based on adaptive networks of dynamical systems, to investigate how physiological systems exchange, process, and integrate information as a network to (i) coordinate their functions across levels and scales (from sub-cellular to organs and organism level) and (ii) generate distinct physiological states in health and disease. Information theoretic concepts apply to cryptography and cryptanalysis. Turing's information unit, the [ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_\(unit\) "Ban \(unit\)"), was used in the [Ultra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultra_\(cryptography\) "Ultra \(cryptography\)") project, breaking the German [Enigma machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enigma_machine "Enigma machine") code and hastening the [end of World War II in Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victory_in_Europe_Day "Victory in Europe Day"). Shannon himself defined an important concept now called the [unicity distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicity_distance "Unicity distance"). Based on the redundancy of the [plaintext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaintext "Plaintext"), it attempts to give a minimum amount of [ciphertext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ciphertext "Ciphertext") necessary to ensure unique decipherability. 
Information theory leads us to believe it is much more difficult to keep secrets than it might first appear. A [brute force attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute_force_attack "Brute force attack") can break systems based on [asymmetric key algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_cryptography "Public-key cryptography") or on most commonly used methods of [symmetric key algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric-key_algorithm "Symmetric-key algorithm") (sometimes called secret key algorithms), such as [block ciphers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_cipher "Block cipher"). The security of all such methods comes from the assumption that no known attack can break them in a practical amount of time. 
[Information theoretic security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theoretic_security "Information theoretic security") refers to methods such as the [one-time pad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-time_pad "One-time pad") that are not vulnerable to such brute force attacks. In such cases, the positive conditional mutual information between the plaintext and ciphertext (conditioned on the [key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_\(cryptography\) "Key \(cryptography\)")) can ensure proper transmission, while the unconditional mutual information between the plaintext and ciphertext remains zero, resulting in absolutely secure communications. In other words, an eavesdropper would not be able to improve his or her guess of the plaintext by gaining knowledge of the ciphertext but not of the key. However, as in any other cryptographic system, care must be used to correctly apply even information-theoretically secure methods; the [Venona project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venona_project "Venona project") was able to crack the one-time pads of the Soviet Union due to their improper reuse of key material. 
### Pseudorandom number generation
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Pseudorandom number generation")]

[Pseudorandom number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generator "Pseudorandom number generator") are widely available in computer language libraries and application programs. They are, almost universally, unsuited to cryptographic use as they do not evade the deterministic nature of modern computer equipment and software. A class of improved random number generators is termed [cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographically_secure_pseudorandom_number_generator "Cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator"), but even they require [random seeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_seed "Random seed") external to the software to work as intended. These can be obtained via [extractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extractor_\(mathematics\) "Extractor \(mathematics\)"), if done carefully. The measure of sufficient randomness in extractors is [min-entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Min-entropy "Min-entropy"), a value related to Shannon entropy through [Rényi entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R%C3%A9nyi_entropy "Rényi entropy"); Rényi entropy is also used in evaluating randomness in cryptographic systems. Although related, the distinctions among these measures mean that a random variable with high Shannon entropy is not necessarily satisfactory for use in an extractor and so for cryptography uses. 
### Seismic exploration
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Seismic exploration")]
One early commercial application of information theory was in the field of seismic oil exploration. Work in this field made it possible to strip off and separate the unwanted noise from the desired seismic signal. Information theory and [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing "Digital signal processing") offer a major improvement of resolution and image clarity over previous analog methods.[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-80)
### Semiotics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Semiotics")]
[Semioticians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics "Semiotics") [Doede Nauta](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Doede_Nauta&action=edit&redlink=1 "Doede Nauta \(page does not exist\)") [[nl](https://nl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doede_Nauta "nl:Doede Nauta")] and [Winfried Nöth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winfried_N%C3%B6th "Winfried Nöth") both considered [Charles Sanders Peirce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Sanders_Peirce "Charles Sanders Peirce") as having created a theory of information in his works on semiotics.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Nauta_1972-81): 171 [[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-N%C3%B6th_2012-82): 137  Nauta defined semiotic information theory as the study of "_the internal processes of coding, filtering, and information processing._ "[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-Nauta_1972-81): 91 
Concepts from information theory such as redundancy and code control have been used by semioticians such as [Umberto Eco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umberto_Eco "Umberto Eco") and [Ferruccio Rossi-Landi](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Ferruccio_Rossi-Landi&action=edit&redlink=1 "Ferruccio Rossi-Landi \(page does not exist\)") [[it](https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferruccio_Rossi-Landi "it:Ferruccio Rossi-Landi")] to explain ideology as a form of message transmission whereby a dominant social class emits its message by using signs that exhibit a high degree of redundancy such that only one message is decoded among a selection of competing ones.[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-83)
### Integrated process organization of neural information
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Information_theory&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Integrated process organization of neural information")]
Quantitative information theoretic methods have been applied in [cognitive science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science "Cognitive science") to analyze the integrated process organization of neural information in the context of the [binding problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_problem "Binding problem") in [cognitive neuroscience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_neuroscience "Cognitive neuroscience").[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-84) In this context, either an information-theoretical measure, such as _functional clusters_ ([Gerald Edelman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Edelman "Gerald Edelman") and [Giulio Tononi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giulio_Tononi "Giulio Tononi")'s functional clustering model and dynamic core hypothesis (DCH)[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-85)) or _effective information_ (Tononi's [integrated information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_information_theory "Integrated information theory") (IIT) of consciousness[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-86)[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-87)[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-88)), is defined (on the basis of a reentrant process organization, i.e. the synchronization of neurophysiological activity between groups of neuronal populations), or the measure of the minimization of free energy on the basis of statistical methods ([Karl J. Friston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_J._Friston "Karl J. Friston")'s [free energy principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_energy_principle "Free energy principle") (FEP), an information-theoretical measure which states that every adaptive change in a self-organized system leads to a minimization of free energy, and the [Bayesian brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_brain "Bayesian brain") hypothesis[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-89)[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-90)[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-91)[[92]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-92)[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory#cite_note-93)).
