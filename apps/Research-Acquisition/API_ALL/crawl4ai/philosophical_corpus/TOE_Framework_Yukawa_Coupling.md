(Redirected from [Yukawa interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yukawa_interaction&redirect=no "Yukawa interaction"))
Scalar–fermion interaction
In [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"), the **Yukawa coupling** or **Yukawa interaction** , named after [Hideki Yukawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hideki_Yukawa "Hideki Yukawa"), is an interaction between particles according to the [Yukawa potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukawa_potential "Yukawa potential"). Specifically, it is between a [scalar field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field_\(quantum_field_theory\) "Scalar field \(quantum field theory\)") (or [pseudoscalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscalar "Pseudoscalar") field)  ϕ {\displaystyle \phi } ![{\\displaystyle \\phi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72b1f30316670aee6270a28334bdf4f5072cdde4) and a [Dirac field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_field "Dirac field") ψ {\displaystyle \psi } ![{\\displaystyle \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45e5789e5d9c8f7c79744f43ecaaf8ba42a8553a) of the type 
V = g ψ ¯ ϕ ψ {\displaystyle V=g\ {\bar {\psi }}\phi \psi } ![{\\displaystyle V=g\\ {\\bar {\\psi }}\\phi \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87d5815f41f4fbb2a44e7cc52e77ff1f26e0f932) g ψ ¯ i γ 5 ϕ ψ {\displaystyle g\ {\bar {\psi }}i\gamma ^{5}\phi \psi } ![{\\displaystyle g\\ {\\bar {\\psi }}i\\gamma ^{5}\\phi \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3f993aa1220bd6ad78d0466194a97f6e266d3ca5)
The Yukawa coupling was developed to model the [strong force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction "Strong interaction") between [hadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadrons "Hadrons"). Yukawa couplings are thus used to describe the [nuclear force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_force "Nuclear force") between [nucleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon "Nucleon") mediated by [pions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion") (which are pseudoscalar [mesons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson "Meson")). 
Yukawa couplings are also used in the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model") to describe the coupling between the [Higgs field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_field "Higgs field") and massless [quark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark "Quark") and [lepton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lepton "Lepton") fields (i.e., the fundamental [fermion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion "Fermion") particles). Through [spontaneous symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_symmetry_breaking "Spontaneous symmetry breaking"), these fermions acquire a mass proportional to the [vacuum expectation value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_expectation_value "Vacuum expectation value") of the Higgs field. This Higgs–fermion coupling was first described by [Steven Weinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Weinberg "Steven Weinberg") in 1967 to model lepton masses.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukawa_coupling#cite_note-1)
## Classical potential
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yukawa_coupling&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Classical potential")]
Main article: [Yukawa potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukawa_potential "Yukawa potential")
If two [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion "Fermion") interact through a Yukawa interaction mediated by a **Yukawa particle** of mass _μ_ , the potential between the two particles, known as the _Yukawa potential_ , will be:  V ( r ) = − g 2 4 π 1 r e − μ r {\displaystyle V(r)=-{\frac {g^{2}}{\,4\pi \,}}\,{\frac {1}{\,r\,}}\,e^{-\mu r}} ![{\\displaystyle V\(r\)=-{\\frac {g^{2}}{\\,4\\pi \\,}}\\,{\\frac {1}{\\,r\\,}}\\,e^{-\\mu r}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e33b2fa2b2c793ff64aab16a81b153e8fad5856f) which is the same as a [Coulomb potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_potential "Coulomb potential") except for the sign and the exponential factor. The sign will make the interaction attractive between all particles (the electromagnetic interaction is repulsive for same electrical charge sign particles). This is explained by the fact that the Yukawa particle has spin zero and even spin always results in an attractive potential. (It is a non-trivial result of [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory")[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukawa_coupling#cite_note-2) that the exchange of even-spin [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosons "Bosons") like the [pion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion "Pion") (spin 0, Yukawa force) or the [graviton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graviton "Graviton") (spin 2, [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity "Gravity")) results in forces always attractive, while odd-spin bosons like the [gluons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluons "Gluons") (spin 1, [strong interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction "Strong interaction")), the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon "Photon") (spin 1, [electromagnetic force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_force "Electromagnetic force")) or the [rho meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rho_meson "Rho meson") (spin 1, Yukawa-like interaction) yields a force that is attractive between opposite charge and repulsive between like-charge.) The negative sign in the exponential gives the interaction a finite effective range, so that particles at great distances will hardly interact any longer (interaction forces fall off exponentially with increasing separation). 
As for other forces, the form of the Yukawa potential has a geometrical interpretation in term of the [field line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_line "Field line") picture introduced by [Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday "Faraday"): The 1/_r_ part results from the dilution of the field line flux in space. The force is proportional to the number of field lines crossing an elementary surface. Since the field lines are emitted isotropically from the force source and since the distance r between the elementary surface and the source varies the apparent size of the surface (the [solid angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_angle "Solid angle")) as 1/_r_ 2 the force also follows the 1/_r_ 2 dependence. This is equivalent to the 1/_r_ part of the potential. In addition, the exchanged mesons are unstable and have a finite lifetime. The disappearance ([radioactive decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioactive_decay "Radioactive decay")) of the mesons causes a reduction of the flux through the surface that results in the additional exponential factor _e_ − _μr_ of the Yukawa potential. Massless particles such as [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photons "Photons") are stable and thus yield only 1/_r_ potentials. (Note however that other massless particles such as [gluons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluons "Gluons") or [gravitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitons "Gravitons") do not generally yield 1/_r_ potentials because they interact with each other, distorting their field pattern. When this self-interaction is negligible, such as in weak-field gravity ([Newtonian gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_gravitation "Newtonian gravitation")) or for very short distances for the [strong interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction "Strong interaction") ([asymptotic freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_freedom "Asymptotic freedom")), the 1/_r_ potential is restored.) 
## Action
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yukawa_coupling&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Action")]
The Yukawa interaction is an interaction between a [scalar field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field_\(quantum_field_theory\) "Scalar field \(quantum field theory\)") (or [pseudoscalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscalar "Pseudoscalar") field) ϕ and a [Dirac field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_field "Dirac field") ψ of the type 
V ≈ g ψ ¯ ϕ ψ {\displaystyle V\approx g\,{\bar {\psi }}\phi \psi } ![{\\displaystyle V\\approx g\\,{\\bar {\\psi }}\\phi \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/866867f9e28ab6e5942591ddeca3dc64b093ac1c) g ψ ¯ i γ 5 ϕ ψ {\displaystyle g\,{\bar {\psi }}i\gamma ^{5}\phi \psi } ![{\\displaystyle g\\,{\\bar {\\psi }}i\\gamma ^{5}\\phi \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/802b4d3894f22929a22a31b75bb68904972ab949)[pseudoscalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscalar "Pseudoscalar")).
The [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_\(physics\) "Action \(physics\)") for a [meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson "Meson") field  ϕ {\displaystyle \phi } ![{\\displaystyle \\phi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72b1f30316670aee6270a28334bdf4f5072cdde4) interacting with a [Dirac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_field "Dirac field") [baryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon "Baryon") field  ψ {\displaystyle \psi } ![{\\displaystyle \\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/45e5789e5d9c8f7c79744f43ecaaf8ba42a8553a) is  S [ ϕ , ψ ] = ∫ [ L m e s o n ( ϕ ) + L D i r a c ( ψ ) + L Y u k a w a ( ϕ , ψ ) ] d n x {\displaystyle S[\phi ,\psi ]=\int \left[\,{\mathcal {L}}_{\mathrm {meson} }(\phi )+{\mathcal {L}}_{\mathrm {Dirac} }(\psi )+{\mathcal {L}}_{\mathrm {Yukawa} }(\phi ,\psi )\,\right]\mathrm {d} ^{n}x} ![{\\displaystyle S\[\\phi ,\\psi \]=\\int \\left\[\\,{\\mathcal {L}}_{\\mathrm {meson} }\(\\phi \)+{\\mathcal {L}}_{\\mathrm {Dirac} }\(\\psi \)+{\\mathcal {L}}_{\\mathrm {Yukawa} }\(\\phi ,\\psi \)\\,\\right\]\\mathrm {d} ^{n}x}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7880b7627fc96f43375374adc834f47812511b8d) where the integration is performed over n dimensions; for typical four-dimensional spacetime _n_ = 4, and ⁠ d 4 x ≡ d x 1 d x 2 d x 3 d x 4 {\displaystyle \mathrm {d} ^{4}x\equiv \mathrm {d} x_{1}\,\mathrm {d} x_{2}\,\mathrm {d} x_{3}\,\mathrm {d} x_{4}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathrm {d} ^{4}x\\equiv \\mathrm {d} x_{1}\\,\\mathrm {d} x_{2}\\,\\mathrm {d} x_{3}\\,\\mathrm {d} x_{4}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7027881c0d5ee1f79b9ff98093587c7c16ce1e4f)⁠. 
The meson [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_\(field_theory\) "Lagrangian \(field theory\)") is given by  L m e s o n ( ϕ ) = 1 2 ∂ μ ϕ ∂ μ ϕ − V ( ϕ ) . {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{\mathrm {meson} }(\phi )={\frac {1}{2}}\partial ^{\mu }\phi \;\partial _{\mu }\phi -V(\phi )~.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {L}}_{\\mathrm {meson} }\(\\phi \)={\\frac {1}{2}}\\partial ^{\\mu }\\phi \\;\\partial _{\\mu }\\phi -V\(\\phi \)~.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e51fa52a4bc2b9b44489764702edba76c9d32c49)
Here,  V ( ϕ ) {\displaystyle V(\phi )} ![{\\displaystyle V\(\\phi \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/604c7510110c1f3d80638df3f720999eba78615c) is a self-interaction term. For a free-field massive meson, one would have ⁠ V ( ϕ ) = 1 2 μ 2 ϕ 2 {\displaystyle \textstyle V(\phi )={\frac {1}{2}}\mu ^{2}\phi ^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle \\textstyle V\(\\phi \)={\\frac {1}{2}}\\mu ^{2}\\phi ^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b300915973a418d93a333e022f873f7b4e8ca1d0)⁠, where  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161) is the mass for the meson. For a ([renormalizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renormalizable "Renormalizable"), polynomial) self-interacting field, one will have ⁠ V ( ϕ ) = 1 2 μ 2 ϕ 2 + λ ϕ 4 {\displaystyle \textstyle V(\phi )={\frac {1}{2}}\mu ^{2}\phi ^{2}+\lambda \phi ^{4}} ![{\\displaystyle \\textstyle V\(\\phi \)={\\frac {1}{2}}\\mu ^{2}\\phi ^{2}+\\lambda \\phi ^{4}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b618a2f59030c163bd8f3a852ac2f2863d4ae752)⁠, where λ is a coupling constant. This potential is explored in detail in the article on the [quartic interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartic_interaction "Quartic interaction"). 
The free-field Dirac Lagrangian is given by  L D i r a c ( ψ ) = ψ ¯ ( i ∂ / − m ) ψ , {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{\mathrm {Dirac} }(\psi )={\bar {\psi }}\,\left(i\,\partial \\!\\!\\!/-m\right)\,\psi ,} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {L}}_{\\mathrm {Dirac} }\(\\psi \)={\\bar {\\psi }}\\,\\left\(i\\,\\partial \\!\\!\\!/-m\\right\)\\,\\psi ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/40a583f2acab64d16b3a3d4d9a534b455d681a81) where m is the real-valued, positive mass of the fermion. 
The Yukawa interaction term is  L Y u k a w a ( ϕ , ψ ) = − g ψ ¯ ϕ ψ , {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{\mathrm {Yukawa} }(\phi ,\psi )=-g\,{\bar {\psi }}\,\phi \,\psi ,} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {L}}_{\\mathrm {Yukawa} }\(\\phi ,\\psi \)=-g\\,{\\bar {\\psi }}\\,\\phi \\,\\psi ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f1098db45a5b0acbcb620e96fabe9857fcedeca9) where g is the (real) [coupling constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_constant "Coupling constant") for scalar mesons and  L Y u k a w a ( ϕ , ψ ) = − g ψ ¯ i γ 5 ϕ ψ {\displaystyle {\mathcal {L}}_{\mathrm {Yukawa} }(\phi ,\psi )=-g\,{\bar {\psi }}\,i\,\gamma ^{5}\,\phi \,\psi } ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {L}}_{\\mathrm {Yukawa} }\(\\phi ,\\psi \)=-g\\,{\\bar {\\psi }}\\,i\\,\\gamma ^{5}\\,\\phi \\,\\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/318a4f71c9ac1be4b0c4e71fa54e0824dc4d014b) for pseudoscalar mesons. Putting it all together one can write the above more explicitly as  S [ ϕ , ψ ] = ∫ [ 1 2 ∂ μ ϕ ∂ μ ϕ − V ( ϕ ) + ψ ¯ ( i ∂ / − m ) ψ − g ψ ¯ ϕ ψ ] d n x . {\displaystyle S[\phi ,\psi ]=\int \left[{\tfrac {1}{2}}\,\partial ^{\mu }\phi \;\partial _{\mu }\phi -V(\phi )+{\bar {\psi }}\,\left(i\,\partial \\!\\!\\!/-m\right)\,\psi -g\,{\bar {\psi }}\,\phi \,\psi \,\right]\mathrm {d} ^{n}x~.} ![{\\displaystyle S\[\\phi ,\\psi \]=\\int \\left\[{\\tfrac {1}{2}}\\,\\partial ^{\\mu }\\phi \\;\\partial _{\\mu }\\phi -V\(\\phi \)+{\\bar {\\psi }}\\,\\left\(i\\,\\partial \\!\\!\\!/-m\\right\)\\,\\psi -g\\,{\\bar {\\psi }}\\,\\phi \\,\\psi \\,\\right\]\\mathrm {d} ^{n}x~.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f92857d28934853b2174b5fea977f5a876d7626d)
## Coupling to Higgs in the Standard Model
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yukawa_coupling&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Coupling to Higgs in the Standard Model")]
A Yukawa coupling term to the [Higgs field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_field "Higgs field") affecting [spontaneous symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_symmetry_breaking "Spontaneous symmetry breaking") in the Standard Model is responsible for fermion masses in a symmetric manner. 
Suppose that the potential  V ( ϕ ) {\displaystyle V(\phi )} ![{\\displaystyle V\(\\phi \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/604c7510110c1f3d80638df3f720999eba78615c) has its minimum, not at ⁠ ϕ = 0 {\displaystyle \phi =0} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi =0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2c96cdab103e6c884877c86d6e5db6e471a167d5)⁠, but at some non-zero value ⁠ ϕ 0 {\displaystyle \phi _{0}} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi _{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/085cd18f16ab03e701c109e6c68d16addab32c3a)⁠. This can happen, for example, with a potential form such as ⁠ V ( ϕ ) = λ ϕ 4 − μ 2 ϕ 2 {\displaystyle V(\phi )=\lambda \phi ^{4}-\mu ^{2}\phi ^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle V\(\\phi \)=\\lambda \\phi ^{4}-\\mu ^{2}\\phi ^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3b9d77da743599a976eddfa4d508ab8792d3d950)⁠. In this case, the Lagrangian exhibits [spontaneous symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_symmetry_breaking "Spontaneous symmetry breaking"). This is because the non-zero value of the  ϕ {\displaystyle \phi } ![{\\displaystyle \\phi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72b1f30316670aee6270a28334bdf4f5072cdde4) field, when operating on the vacuum, has a non-zero [vacuum expectation value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_expectation_value "Vacuum expectation value") of ⁠ ϕ {\displaystyle \phi } ![{\\displaystyle \\phi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/72b1f30316670aee6270a28334bdf4f5072cdde4)⁠. 
In the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model "Standard Model"), this non-zero expectation is responsible for the fermion masses despite the chiral symmetry of the model apparently excluding them. To exhibit the mass term, the action can be re-expressed in terms of the derived field ⁠ ϕ ′ = ϕ − ϕ 0 {\displaystyle \phi '=\phi -\phi _{0}} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi '=\\phi -\\phi _{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5d03fcf8fa3d154369c85bb95fc232f1ac4ebfd0)⁠, where  ϕ 0 {\displaystyle \phi _{0}} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi _{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/085cd18f16ab03e701c109e6c68d16addab32c3a) is constructed to be independent of position (a constant). This means that the Yukawa term includes a component  g ϕ 0 ψ ¯ ψ {\displaystyle g\,\phi _{0}\,{\bar {\psi }}\,\psi } ![{\\displaystyle g\\,\\phi _{0}\\,{\\bar {\\psi }}\\,\\psi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fd8ab730d710b1d6df06315a410a898c2a40af82) and, since both g and  ϕ 0 {\displaystyle \phi _{0}} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi _{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/085cd18f16ab03e701c109e6c68d16addab32c3a) are constants, the term presents as a mass term for the fermion with equivalent mass ⁠ g ϕ 0 {\displaystyle g\phi _{0}} ![{\\displaystyle g\\phi _{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/293b31352470f379ce3245903649a43f83499a4b)⁠. This mechanism is the means by which spontaneous symmetry breaking gives mass to fermions. The scalar field  ϕ ′ {\displaystyle \phi '} ![{\\displaystyle \\phi '}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ac5304c739c4deec1d259e3235a419e6177fe77a) is known as the [Higgs field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_field "Higgs field"). 
The Yukawa coupling for any given fermion in the Standard Model is an input to the theory. The ultimate reason for these couplings is not known: it would be something that a better, deeper theory should explain. 
## Majorana form
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Yukawa_coupling&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Majorana form")]
It is also possible to have a Yukawa interaction between a scalar and a [Majorana field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majorana_field "Majorana field"). In fact, the Yukawa interaction involving a scalar and a Dirac spinor can be thought of as a Yukawa interaction involving a scalar with two Majorana spinors of the same mass. Broken out in terms of the two [chiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chirality_\(physics\) "Chirality \(physics\)") Majorana spinors, one has  S [ ϕ , χ ] = ∫ [ 1 2 ∂ μ ϕ ∂ μ ϕ − V ( ϕ ) + χ † i σ ¯ ⋅ ∂ χ + i 2 ( m + g ϕ ) χ T σ 2 χ − i 2 ( m + g ϕ ) ∗ χ † σ 2 χ ∗ ] d n x , {\displaystyle S[\phi ,\chi ]=\int \left[\,{\frac {1}{2}}\,\partial ^{\mu }\phi \;\partial _{\mu }\phi -V(\phi )+\chi ^{\dagger }\,i\,{\bar {\sigma }}\,\cdot \,\partial \chi +{\frac {i}{2}}\,(m+g\,\phi )\,\chi ^{\mathrm {T} }\,\sigma ^{2}\,\chi -{\frac {i}{2}}\,(m+g\,\phi )^{*}\,\chi ^{\dagger }\,\sigma ^{2}\,\chi ^{*}\,\right]\mathrm {d} ^{n}x,} ![{\\displaystyle S\[\\phi ,\\chi \]=\\int \\left\[\\,{\\frac {1}{2}}\\,\\partial ^{\\mu }\\phi \\;\\partial _{\\mu }\\phi -V\(\\phi \)+\\chi ^{\\dagger }\\,i\\,{\\bar {\\sigma }}\\,\\cdot \\,\\partial \\chi +{\\frac {i}{2}}\\,\(m+g\\,\\phi \)\\,\\chi ^{\\mathrm {T} }\\,\\sigma ^{2}\\,\\chi -{\\frac {i}{2}}\\,\(m+g\\,\\phi \)^{*}\\,\\chi ^{\\dagger }\\,\\sigma ^{2}\\,\\chi ^{*}\\,\\right\]\\mathrm {d} ^{n}x,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dcbb44cdb157712251d42cfff532d62e24f7da9c) where g is a complex [coupling constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_constant "Coupling constant"), m is a [complex number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_number "Complex number"), and n is the number of dimensions, as above.
