Notion in statistics
In [mathematical statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_statistics "Mathematical statistics"), the **Fisher information** is a way of measuring the amount of [information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information "Information") that an observable [random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variable "Random variable") _X_ carries about an unknown parameter _θ_ of a distribution that models _X_. Formally, it is the [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance "Variance") of the [score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Score_\(statistics\) "Score \(statistics\)"), or the [expected value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_value "Expected value") of the [observed information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observed_information "Observed information"). 
The role of the Fisher information in the asymptotic theory of [maximum-likelihood estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum-likelihood_estimation "Maximum-likelihood estimation") was emphasized and explored by the statistician [Sir Ronald Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Fisher "Ronald Fisher") (following some initial results by [Francis Ysidro Edgeworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Ysidro_Edgeworth "Francis Ysidro Edgeworth")). The Fisher information matrix is used to calculate the [covariance matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance_matrices "Covariance matrices") associated with [maximum-likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood "Maximum likelihood") [estimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estimator "Estimator"). It can also be used in the formulation of test statistics, such as the [Wald test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wald_test "Wald test"). 
In [Bayesian statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_statistics "Bayesian statistics"), the Fisher information plays a role in the derivation of non-informative [prior distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_distribution "Prior distribution") according to [Jeffreys' rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffreys_prior "Jeffreys prior").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-1) It also appears as the large-sample covariance of the [posterior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_distribution "Posterior distribution"), provided that the prior is sufficiently smooth (a result known as [Bernstein–von Mises theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernstein%E2%80%93von_Mises_theorem "Bernstein–von Mises theorem"), which was anticipated by [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace "Laplace") for [exponential families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_families "Exponential families")).[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-2) The same result is used when approximating the posterior with [Laplace's approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace%27s_approximation "Laplace's approximation"), where the Fisher information appears as the covariance of the fitted Gaussian.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-3)
Statistical systems of a scientific nature (physical, biological, etc.) whose likelihood functions obey [shift invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shift-invariant_system "Shift-invariant system") have been shown to obey maximum Fisher information.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-FOOTNOTEFriedenGatenby2013-4) The level of the maximum depends upon the nature of the system constraints. 
## Definition
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Definition")]
The Fisher information is a way of measuring the amount of information that an observable [random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variable "Random variable") X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) carries about an unknown [parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter "Parameter") θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af) upon which the probability of  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) depends. Let  f ( X ; θ ) {\displaystyle f(X;\theta )} ![{\\displaystyle f\(X;\\theta \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/535b11f983144cd0bd04b458874af198fb51a82f) be the [probability density function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_density_function "Probability density function") (or [probability mass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_mass_function "Probability mass function")) for  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) conditioned on the value of  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af). It describes the probability that we observe a given outcome of  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab), _given_ a known value of  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af). If  f {\displaystyle f} ![{\\displaystyle f}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/132e57acb643253e7810ee9702d9581f159a1c61) is sharply peaked with respect to changes in  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af), it is easy to indicate the "correct" value of  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af) from the data, or equivalently, that the data  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) provides a lot of information about the parameter  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af). If  f {\displaystyle f} ![{\\displaystyle f}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/132e57acb643253e7810ee9702d9581f159a1c61) is flat and spread-out, then it would take many samples of  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) to estimate the actual "true" value of  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af) that _would_ be obtained using the entire population being sampled. This suggests studying some kind of variance with respect to  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af).

A random variable carrying high Fisher information implies that the absolute value of the score is often high. The Fisher information is not a function of a particular observation, as the random variable _X_ has been averaged out.

### Regularity conditions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Regularity conditions")]
The regularity conditions are as follows:[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-8)
  1. The partial derivative of _f_(_X_ ; _θ_) with respect to _θ_ exists [almost everywhere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_everywhere "Almost everywhere"). (It can fail to exist on a null set, as long as this set does not depend on _θ_.)
  2. The integral of _f_(_X_ ; _θ_) can be differentiated under the integral sign with respect to _θ_.
  3. The [support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_\(mathematics\) "Support \(mathematics\)") of _f_(_X_ ; _θ_) does not depend on _θ_.


If _θ_ is a vector then the regularity conditions must hold for every component of _θ_. It is easy to find an example of a density that does not satisfy the regularity conditions: The density of a Uniform(0, _θ_) variable fails to satisfy conditions 1 and 3. In this case, even though the Fisher information can be computed from the definition, it will not have the properties it is typically assumed to have. 
### In terms of likelihood
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: In terms of likelihood")]
Because the [likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood "Likelihood") of _θ_ given _X_ is always proportional to the probability _f_(_X_ ; _θ_), their logarithms necessarily differ by a constant that is independent of _θ_ , and the derivatives of these logarithms with respect to _θ_ are necessarily equal. Thus one can substitute in a log-likelihood _l_(_θ_ ; _X_) instead of log _f_(_X_ ; _θ_) in the definitions of Fisher Information. 
### Samples of any size
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Samples of any size")]
The value _X_ can represent a single sample drawn from a single distribution or can represent a collection of samples drawn from a collection of distributions. If there are _n_ samples and the corresponding _n_ distributions are [statistically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistically_independent "Statistically independent") then the Fisher information will necessarily be the sum of the single-sample Fisher information values, one for each single sample from its distribution. In particular, if the _n_ distributions are [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d. "I.i.d.") then the Fisher information will necessarily be _n_ times the Fisher information of a single sample from the common distribution. Stated in other words, the Fisher Information of i.i.d. observations of a sample of size _n_ from a population is equal to the product of _n_ and the Fisher Information of a single observation from the same population.

The sample mean of the _i_ th trial is  μ i = ( 1 / n ) ∑ j = 1 n x i j {\displaystyle \mu _{i}=(1/n)\sum _{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}} ![{\\displaystyle \\mu _{i}=\(1/n\)\\sum _{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/efb0010ae60f125b9fab7c2d9193e548e4c6b5b0). The expected value of the sample mean (over the [sampling distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_distribution "Sampling distribution")) is       E ( μ ) = ∑ x i μ i p ( x i , θ ) = θ , {\displaystyle E(\mu )=\sum _{x_{i}}\mu _{i}\,p(x_{i},\theta )=\theta ,} ![{\\displaystyle E\(\\mu \)=\\sum _{x_{i}}\\mu _{i}\\,p\(x_{i},\\theta \)=\\theta ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4b03b83bac8321559828e4d09710d07eeee2b7d7)
where the sum is over all  2 n {\displaystyle 2^{n}} ![{\\displaystyle 2^{n}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8226f30650ee4fe4e640c6d2798127e80e9c160d) possible trial outcomes. The expected value of the square of the sample mean is       E ( μ 2 ) = ∑ x i μ i 2 p ( x i , θ ) = ( 1 + ( n − 1 ) θ ) θ n {\displaystyle E(\mu ^{2})=\sum _{x_{i}}\mu _{i}^{2}\,p(x_{i},\theta )={\frac {(1+(n-1)\theta )\theta }{n}}} ![{\\displaystyle E\(\\mu ^{2}\)=\\sum _{x_{i}}\\mu _{i}^{2}\\,p\(x_{i},\\theta \)={\\frac {\(1+\(n-1\)\\theta \)\\theta }{n}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/718af4521bbc5978a1a421e707be1ba95fbe17e0)
so the variance in the value of the mean is       E ( μ 2 ) − E ( μ ) 2 = θ ( 1 − θ ) n {\displaystyle E(\mu ^{2})-E(\mu )^{2}={\frac {\theta (1-\theta )}{n}}} ![{\\displaystyle E\(\\mu ^{2}\)-E\(\\mu \)^{2}={\\frac {\\theta \(1-\\theta \)}{n}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/37335fa439546d9d03fc9fde5e5c57f111f83520)
It is seen that the Fisher information is the reciprocal of the [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance "Variance") of the mean number of successes in _n_ [Bernoulli trials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_trial "Bernoulli trial"). This is generally true. In this case, the Cramér–Rao bound is an equality.

## Matrix form
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Matrix form")]
When there are _N_ parameters, so that _θ_ is an _N_ × 1 [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector "Column vector") θ = [ θ 1 θ 2 … θ N ] T , {\displaystyle \theta ={\begin{bmatrix}\theta _{1}&\theta _{2}&\dots &\theta _{N}\end{bmatrix}}^{\textsf {T}},} ![{\\displaystyle \\theta ={\\begin{bmatrix}\\theta _{1}&\\theta _{2}&\\dots &\\theta _{N}\\end{bmatrix}}^{\\textsf {T}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4086b01fd43375bff350815851b0949330ce344e) the Fisher information takes the form of an _N_ × _N_ [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_\(mathematics\) "Matrix \(mathematics\)"). This matrix is called the **Fisher information matrix** (FIM) and has typical element       [ I ( θ ) ] i , j = E ⁡ [ ( ∂ ∂ θ i log ⁡ f ( X ; θ ) ) ( ∂ ∂ θ j log ⁡ f ( X ; θ ) ) | θ ] . {\displaystyle {\bigl [}{\mathcal {I}}(\theta ){\bigr ]}_{i,j}=\operatorname {E} \left[\left.\left({\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta _{i}}}\log f(X;\theta )\right)\left({\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta _{j}}}\log f(X;\theta )\right)\,\,\right|\,\,\theta \right].} ![{\\displaystyle {\\bigl \[}{\\mathcal {I}}\(\\theta \){\\bigr \]}_{i,j}=\\operatorname {E} \\left\[\\left.\\left\({\\frac {\\partial }{\\partial \\theta _{i}}}\\log f\(X;\\theta \)\\right\)\\left\({\\frac {\\partial }{\\partial \\theta _{j}}}\\log f\(X;\\theta \)\\right\)\\,\\,\\right|\\,\\,\\theta \\right\].}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ccbbe3a423ab34a3c9ac9989f134642de46c52c6)
The FIM is a _N_ × _N_ [positive semidefinite matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_semidefinite_matrix "Positive semidefinite matrix"). If it is positive definite, then it defines a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric "Riemannian metric")[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-11) on the _N_ -[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension "Dimension") [parameter space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter_space "Parameter space"). The topic [information geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_geometry "Information geometry") uses this to connect Fisher information to [differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry"), and in that context, this metric is known as the [Fisher information metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information_metric "Fisher information metric"). 
Under certain regularity conditions, the Fisher information matrix may also be written as       [ I ( θ ) ] i , j = − E ⁡ [ ∂ 2 ∂ θ i ∂ θ j log ⁡ f ( X ; θ ) | θ ] . {\displaystyle {\bigl [}{\mathcal {I}}(\theta ){\bigr ]}_{i,j}=-\operatorname {E} \left[\left.{\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial \theta _{i}\,\partial \theta _{j}}}\log f(X;\theta )\,\,\right|\,\,\theta \right]\,.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\bigl \[}{\\mathcal {I}}\(\\theta \){\\bigr \]}_{i,j}=-\\operatorname {E} \\left\[\\left.{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial \\theta _{i}\\,\\partial \\theta _{j}}}\\log f\(X;\\theta \)\\,\\,\\right|\\,\\,\\theta \\right\]\\,.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d7d2030360016af26f46a49480f7f8ddc4386837)
The result is interesting in several ways: 
  * It is equal to minus the _expected_ [Hessian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hessian_matrix "Hessian matrix") of the [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy "Relative entropy").
  * It can be used as a Riemannian metric for defining Fisher-Rao geometry when it is positive-definite.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-12)
  * It can be understood as a metric induced from the [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric "Euclidean metric"), after appropriate change of variable.
  * In its complex-valued form, it is the [Fubini–Study metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fubini%E2%80%93Study_metric "Fubini–Study metric").
  * It is the key part of the proof of [Wilks' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilks%27_theorem "Wilks' theorem"), which allows confidence region estimates for [maximum likelihood estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood_estimation "Maximum likelihood estimation") (for those conditions for which it applies) without needing the [Likelihood Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood_Principle "Likelihood Principle").
  * In cases where the analytical calculations of the FIM above are difficult, it is possible to form an average of easy [Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method "Monte Carlo method") estimates of the [Hessian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hessian_matrix "Hessian matrix") of the negative log-likelihood function as an estimate of the FIM.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-13)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-14)[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-15) The estimates may be based on values of the negative log-likelihood function or the gradient of the negative log-likelihood function; no analytical calculation of the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood function is needed.


### Information orthogonal parameters
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Information orthogonal parameters")]
We say that two parameter component vectors _θ 1_ and _θ 2_ are information orthogonal if the Fisher information matrix is block diagonal, with these components in separate blocks.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-16) Orthogonal parameters are easy to deal with in the sense that their [ maximum likelihood estimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood "Maximum likelihood") are asymptotically uncorrelated. When considering how to analyse a statistical model, the modeller is advised to invest some time searching for an orthogonal parametrization of the model, in particular when the parameter of interest is one-dimensional, but the [nuisance parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuisance_parameter "Nuisance parameter") can have any dimension.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-17)
### Singular statistical model
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Singular statistical model")]
See also: [Regular parametric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_parametric_model "Regular parametric model")
If the Fisher information matrix is positive definite for all θ, then the corresponding [statistical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_model "Statistical model") is said to be _regular_ ; otherwise, the statistical model is said to be _singular_.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-18) Examples of singular statistical models include the following: normal [mixtures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_model "Mixture model"), binomial mixtures, multinomial mixtures, [Bayesian networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_network "Bayesian network"), [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network_\(machine_learning\) "Neural network \(machine learning\)"), [radial basis functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_basis_function "Radial basis function"), [hidden Markov models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hidden_Markov_model "Hidden Markov model"), [stochastic context-free grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_context-free_grammar "Stochastic context-free grammar"), reduced rank regressions, [Boltzmann machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_machine "Boltzmann machine"). 
In [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning"), if a statistical model is devised so that it extracts hidden structure from a random phenomenon, then it naturally becomes singular.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-19)

## Properties
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Properties")]
### Chain rule
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Chain rule")]
Similar to the [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_\(information_theory\)#Further_properties "Entropy \(information theory\)") or [mutual information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_information#Conditional_mutual_information "Mutual information"), the Fisher information also possesses a **chain rule** decomposition. In particular, if _X_ and _Y_ are jointly distributed random variables, it follows that:[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-22)      I X , Y ( θ ) = I X ( θ ) + I Y ∣ X ( θ ) , {\displaystyle {\mathcal {I}}_{X,Y}(\theta )={\mathcal {I}}_{X}(\theta )+{\mathcal {I}}_{Y\mid X}(\theta ),} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {I}}_{X,Y}\(\\theta \)={\\mathcal {I}}_{X}\(\\theta \)+{\\mathcal {I}}_{Y\\mid X}\(\\theta \),}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f8d49a369f3c5e671d40157bd37a071600c41a40)
where  I Y ∣ X ( θ ) = E X ⁡ [ I Y ∣ X = x ( θ ) ] {\displaystyle {\mathcal {I}}_{Y\mid X}(\theta )=\operatorname {E} _{X}\left[{\mathcal {I}}_{Y\mid X=x}(\theta )\right]} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {I}}_{Y\\mid X}\(\\theta \)=\\operatorname {E} _{X}\\left\[{\\mathcal {I}}_{Y\\mid X=x}\(\\theta \)\\right\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0597b3e4e15806338f76e41e02af60263455c8d3) and  I Y ∣ X = x ( θ ) {\displaystyle {\mathcal {I}}_{Y\mid X=x}(\theta )} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {I}}_{Y\\mid X=x}\(\\theta \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/32d34878f1c6934fc9afdc8ac44872c8781c9a09) is the Fisher information of _Y_ relative to  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af) calculated with respect to the conditional density of _Y_ given a specific value  _X_ =  _x_. 
As a special case, if the two random variables are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence "Statistical independence"), the information yielded by the two random variables is the sum of the information from each random variable separately:       I X , Y ( θ ) = I X ( θ ) + I Y ( θ ) . {\displaystyle {\mathcal {I}}_{X,Y}(\theta )={\mathcal {I}}_{X}(\theta )+{\mathcal {I}}_{Y}(\theta ).} ![{\\displaystyle {\\mathcal {I}}_{X,Y}\(\\theta \)={\\mathcal {I}}_{X}\(\\theta \)+{\\mathcal {I}}_{Y}\(\\theta \).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d2f877785d107d6e679e2feaf98851b414b37674)
Consequently, the information in a random sample of _n_ [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed_random_variables "Independent and identically distributed random variables") observations is _n_ times the information in a sample of size 1.

### Isoperimetric inequality
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Isoperimetric inequality")]
The Fisher information matrix plays a role in an inequality like the [isoperimetric inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isoperimetric_inequality "Isoperimetric inequality").[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-29) Of all probability distributions with a given entropy, the one whose Fisher information matrix has the smallest trace is the Gaussian distribution. This is like how, of all bounded sets with a given volume, the sphere has the smallest surface area. 
The proof involves taking a multivariate random variable  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) with density function  f {\displaystyle f} ![{\\displaystyle f}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/132e57acb643253e7810ee9702d9581f159a1c61) and adding a location parameter to form a family of densities  { f ( x − θ ) ∣ θ ∈ R n } {\displaystyle \\{f(x-\theta )\mid \theta \in \mathbb {R} ^{n}\\}} ![{\\displaystyle \\{f\(x-\\theta \)\\mid \\theta \\in \\mathbb {R} ^{n}\\}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c28d75854a5141c527c3a2ffded9770c3f0c3428). Then, by analogy with the [Minkowski–Steiner formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski%E2%80%93Steiner_formula "Minkowski–Steiner formula"), the "surface area" of  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) is defined to be       S ( X ) = lim ε → 0 e H ( X + Z ε ) − e H ( X ) ε {\displaystyle S(X)=\lim _{\varepsilon \to 0}{\frac {e^{H(X+Z_{\varepsilon })}-e^{H(X)}}{\varepsilon }}} ![{\\displaystyle S\(X\)=\\lim _{\\varepsilon \\to 0}{\\frac {e^{H\(X+Z_{\\varepsilon }\)}-e^{H\(X\)}}{\\varepsilon }}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/77532cbf846f9c21811b19337b2c2ba1c20ceb30)
where  Z ε {\displaystyle Z_{\varepsilon }} ![{\\displaystyle Z_{\\varepsilon }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a42c478078f5f37926b3ca0663b9ab9301d01b87) is a Gaussian variable with covariance matrix  ε I {\displaystyle \varepsilon I} ![{\\displaystyle \\varepsilon I}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/35bc78c234f5e608f23e424519989b9bc4a9af9f). The trace of the Fisher information matrix is found to be a factor of  S ( X ) {\displaystyle S(X)} ![{\\displaystyle S\(X\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f9a8ce88a4734545697e3dcbc9ec1026a8ed35e2). 
## Applications
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Applications")]
### Optimal design of experiments
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Optimal design of experiments")]
Fisher information is widely used in [optimal experimental design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_design "Optimal design"). Because of the reciprocity of estimator-variance and Fisher information, _minimizing_ the _variance_ corresponds to _maximizing_ the _information_. 
When the [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_model "Linear model") (or [linearized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_regression "Nonlinear regression")) [statistical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_model "Statistical model") has several [parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter "Parameter"), the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_value "Expected value") of the parameter estimator is a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector "Column vector") and its [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance_matrix "Covariance matrix") is a [matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_\(mathematics\) "Matrix \(mathematics\)"). The inverse of the variance matrix is called the "information matrix". Because the variance of the estimator of a parameter vector is a matrix, the problem of "minimizing the variance" is complicated. Using [statistical theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_theory "Statistical theory"), statisticians compress the information-matrix using real-valued [summary statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_statistics "Summary statistics"); being real-valued functions, these "information criteria" can be maximized. 
Traditionally, statisticians have evaluated estimators and designs by considering some [summary statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_statistics "Summary statistics") of the covariance matrix (of an unbiased estimator), usually with positive real values (like the [determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinant "Determinant") or [matrix trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_trace "Matrix trace")). Working with positive real numbers brings several advantages: If the estimator of a single parameter has a positive variance, then the variance and the Fisher information are both positive real numbers; hence they are members of the convex cone of nonnegative real numbers (whose nonzero members have reciprocals in this same cone). 
For several parameters, the covariance matrices and information matrices are elements of the convex cone of [nonnegative-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative-definite_matrix "Nonnegative-definite matrix") symmetric matrices in a [partially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order "Partial order") [ordered vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_vector_space "Ordered vector space"), under the [Loewner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loewner_order "Loewner order") (Löwner) order. This cone is closed under matrix addition and inversion, as well as under the multiplication of positive real numbers and matrices. An exposition of matrix theory and Loewner order appears in Pukelsheim.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-31)
The traditional optimality criteria are the information matrix's invariants, in the sense of [invariant theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_theory "Invariant theory"); algebraically, the traditional optimality criteria are [functionals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_\(mathematics\) "Functional \(mathematics\)") of the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue "Eigenvalue") of the (Fisher) information matrix (see [optimal design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_design "Optimal design")). 
### Jeffreys prior in Bayesian statistics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Jeffreys prior in Bayesian statistics")]
In [Bayesian statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_statistics "Bayesian statistics"), the Fisher information is used to calculate the [Jeffreys prior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffreys_prior "Jeffreys prior"), which is a standard, non-informative prior for continuous distribution parameters.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-32)
### Computational neuroscience
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Computational neuroscience")]
The Fisher information has been used to find bounds on the accuracy of [neural codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_coding "Neural coding"). In that case, _X_ is typically the joint responses of many neurons representing a low dimensional variable _θ_ (such as a [stimulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimulus_\(physiology\) "Stimulus \(physiology\)") parameter). In particular the role of correlations in the noise of the neural responses has been studied.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-33)
### Epidemiology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Epidemiology")]
Fisher information was used to study how informative different data sources are for estimation of the [reproduction number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproduction_number "Reproduction number") of SARS-CoV-2.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-34)
### Machine learning
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Machine learning")]
The Fisher information is used in machine learning techniques such as [elastic weight consolidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_weight_consolidation "Elastic weight consolidation"),[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-35) which reduces [catastrophic forgetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophic_interference "Catastrophic interference") in [artificial neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_networks "Artificial neural networks"). 
Fisher information can be used as an alternative to the Hessian of the loss function in second-order gradient descent network training.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-Martens2020-36)
### Color discrimination
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Color discrimination")]
Using a [Fisher information metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information_metric "Fisher information metric"), da Fonseca et. al [[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-Fonseca2016-37) investigated the degree to which [MacAdam ellipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacAdam_ellipse "MacAdam ellipse") (color discrimination ellipses) can be derived from the [response functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LMS_color_space "LMS color space") of the retinal photoreceptors. 
## Relation to relative entropy
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Relation to relative entropy")]
See also: [Fisher information metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information_metric "Fisher information metric")
Fisher information is related to [relative entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_entropy "Relative entropy").[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-38) The relative entropy, or [Kullback–Leibler divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence "Kullback–Leibler divergence"), between two distributions  p {\displaystyle p} ![{\\displaystyle p}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81eac1e205430d1f40810df36a0edffdc367af36) and  q {\displaystyle q} ![{\\displaystyle q}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/06809d64fa7c817ffc7e323f85997f783dbdf71d) can be written as       K L ( p : q ) = ∫ p ( x ) log ⁡ p ( x ) q ( x ) d x . {\displaystyle KL(p:q)=\int p(x)\log {\frac {p(x)}{q(x)}}\,dx.} ![{\\displaystyle KL\(p:q\)=\\int p\(x\)\\log {\\frac {p\(x\)}{q\(x\)}}\\,dx.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1a35dc7646ec3bd3ce7f26ae551fcb9e754ab6f4)
Now, consider a family of probability distributions  f ( x ; θ ) {\displaystyle f(x;\theta )} ![{\\displaystyle f\(x;\\theta \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/79480c3540803bdda2613d69277692e1061ad7d5) parametrized by  θ ∈ Θ {\displaystyle \theta \in \Theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta \\in \\Theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bc76a89f6f175a02e371f31f86a8906fa3fd5e7e). Then the [Kullback–Leibler divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback%E2%80%93Leibler_divergence "Kullback–Leibler divergence"), between two distributions in the family can be written as       D ( θ , θ ′ ) = K L ( p ( ⋅ ; θ ) : p ( ⋅ ; θ ′ ) ) = ∫ f ( x ; θ ) log ⁡ f ( x ; θ ) f ( x ; θ ′ ) d x . {\displaystyle D(\theta ,\theta ')=KL(p({}\cdot {};\theta ):p({}\cdot {};\theta '))=\int f(x;\theta )\log {\frac {f(x;\theta )}{f(x;\theta ')}}\,dx.} ![{\\displaystyle D\(\\theta ,\\theta '\)=KL\(p\({}\\cdot {};\\theta \):p\({}\\cdot {};\\theta '\)\)=\\int f\(x;\\theta \)\\log {\\frac {f\(x;\\theta \)}{f\(x;\\theta '\)}}\\,dx.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8120bdecf0599889fac8a0bcec31cccdce6b6aa0)
If  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af) is fixed, then the relative entropy between two distributions of the same family is minimized at  θ ′ = θ {\displaystyle \theta '=\theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta '=\\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a2c3afb55e9f83bcd762c9e09456a5fec315a8a8). For  θ ′ {\displaystyle \theta '} ![{\\displaystyle \\theta '}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c02a68dba2972e9cf4faf9656f58da0df8be6ae9) close to  θ {\displaystyle \theta } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6e5ab2664b422d53eb0c7df3b87e1360d75ad9af), one may expand the previous expression in a series up to second order:       D ( θ , θ ′ ) = 1 2 ( θ ′ − θ ) T ( ∂ 2 ∂ θ i ′ ∂ θ j ′ D ( θ , θ ′ ) ) θ ′ = θ ( θ ′ − θ ) + o ( ( θ ′ − θ ) 2 ) {\displaystyle D(\theta ,\theta ')={\frac {1}{2}}(\theta '-\theta )^{\textsf {T}}\left({\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial \theta '_{i}\,\partial \theta '_{j}}}D(\theta ,\theta ')\right)_{\theta '=\theta }(\theta '-\theta )+o\left((\theta '-\theta )^{2}\right)} ![{\\displaystyle D\(\\theta ,\\theta '\)={\\frac {1}{2}}\(\\theta '-\\theta \)^{\\textsf {T}}\\left\({\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial \\theta '_{i}\\,\\partial \\theta '_{j}}}D\(\\theta ,\\theta '\)\\right\)_{\\theta '=\\theta }\(\\theta '-\\theta \)+o\\left\(\(\\theta '-\\theta \)^{2}\\right\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c861740a89e0e210ec11ecb124945bfd6be50635)
But the second order derivative can be written as       ( ∂ 2 ∂ θ i ′ ∂ θ j ′ D ( θ , θ ′ ) ) θ ′ = θ = − ∫ f ( x ; θ ) ( ∂ 2 ∂ θ i ′ ∂ θ j ′ log ⁡ ( f ( x ; θ ′ ) ) ) θ ′ = θ d x = [ I ( θ ) ] i , j . {\displaystyle \left({\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial \theta '_{i}\,\partial \theta '_{j}}}D(\theta ,\theta ')\right)_{\theta '=\theta }=-\int f(x;\theta )\left({\frac {\partial ^{2}}{\partial \theta '_{i}\,\partial \theta '_{j}}}\log(f(x;\theta '))\right)_{\theta '=\theta }\,dx=[{\mathcal {I}}(\theta )]_{i,j}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\left\({\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial \\theta '_{i}\\,\\partial \\theta '_{j}}}D\(\\theta ,\\theta '\)\\right\)_{\\theta '=\\theta }=-\\int f\(x;\\theta \)\\left\({\\frac {\\partial ^{2}}{\\partial \\theta '_{i}\\,\\partial \\theta '_{j}}}\\log\(f\(x;\\theta '\)\)\\right\)_{\\theta '=\\theta }\\,dx=\[{\\mathcal {I}}\(\\theta \)\]_{i,j}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/02bab191b23a6d1a0812342f41d4e4eeec455bcb)
Thus the Fisher information represents the [curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature "Curvature") of the relative entropy of a conditional distribution with respect to its parameters. 
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Fisher_information&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: History")]
The Fisher information was discussed by several early statisticians, notably [F. Y. Edgeworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Ysidro_Edgeworth "Francis Ysidro Edgeworth").[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-FOOTNOTESavage1976-39) For example, Savage[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-FOOTNOTESavage1976156-40) says: "In it [Fisher information], he [Fisher] was to some extent anticipated (Edgeworth 1908–9 esp. 502, 507–8, 662, 677–8, 82–5 and references he [Edgeworth] cites including Pearson and Filon 1898 [. . .])." There are a number of early historical sources[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEdgeworth1908bEdgeworth1908c-41) and a number of reviews of this early work.[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-FOOTNOTEPratt1976-42)[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-FOOTNOTEStigler1978Stigler1986Stigler1999-43)[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information#cite_note-FOOTNOTEHald1998Hald1999-44)
