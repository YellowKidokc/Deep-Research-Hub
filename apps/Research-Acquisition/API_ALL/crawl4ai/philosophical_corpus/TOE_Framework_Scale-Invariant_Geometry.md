From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Features that do not change if length or energy scales are multiplied by a common factor
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2a/Wiener_process_animated.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Wiener_process_animated.gif)The [Wiener process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiener_process "Wiener process") is scale-invariant
In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") and [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics "Statistics"), **scale invariance** is a feature of objects or laws that do not change if scales of length, energy, or other variables, are multiplied by a common factor, and thus represent a universality. 
The technical term for this [transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_\(mathematics\) "Transformation \(mathematics\)") is a **dilatation** (also known as **dilation**). Dilatations can form part of a larger [conformal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_symmetry "Conformal symmetry"). 
  * In mathematics, scale invariance usually refers to an invariance of individual [functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_\(mathematics\) "Function \(mathematics\)") or [curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve "Curve"). A closely related concept is [self-similarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-similarity "Self-similarity"), where a function or curve is invariant under a discrete subset of the dilations. It is also possible for the [probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") of [random processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_process "Random process") to display this kind of scale invariance or self-similarity.
  * In [classical field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_field_theory "Classical field theory"), scale invariance most commonly applies to the invariance of a whole theory under dilatations. Such theories typically describe classical physical processes with no characteristic length scale.
  * In [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"), scale invariance has an interpretation in terms of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics "Particle physics"). In a scale-invariant theory, the strength of particle interactions does not depend on the energy of the particles involved.
  * In [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics"), scale invariance is a feature of [phase transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition "Phase transition"). The key observation is that near a phase transition or [critical point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_point_\(thermodynamics\) "Critical point \(thermodynamics\)"), fluctuations occur at all length scales, and thus one should look for an explicitly scale-invariant theory to describe the phenomena. Such theories are scale-invariant [statistical field theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_field_theory "Statistical field theory"), and are formally very similar to scale-invariant quantum field theories.
  * [Universality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universality_\(dynamical_systems\) "Universality \(dynamical systems\)") is the observation that widely different microscopic systems can display the same behaviour at a phase transition. Thus phase transitions in many different systems may be described by the same underlying scale-invariant theory.
  * In general, [dimensionless quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantities "Dimensionless quantities") are scale-invariant. The analogous concept in [statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics "Statistics") are [standardized moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardized_moment "Standardized moment"), which are scale-invariant statistics of a variable, while the unstandardized moments are not.


## Scale-invariant curves and self-similarity
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Scale-invariant curves and self-similarity")]
In mathematics, one can consider the scaling properties of a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_\(mathematics\) "Function \(mathematics\)") or [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve "Curve") _f_ (_x_) under rescalings of the variable x. That is, one is interested in the shape of _f_ (_λx_) for some scale factor λ, which can be taken to be a length or size rescaling. The requirement for _f_ (_x_) to be invariant under all rescalings is usually taken to be       f ( λ x ) = λ Δ f ( x ) {\displaystyle f(\lambda x)=\lambda ^{\Delta }f(x)} ![{\\displaystyle f\(\\lambda x\)=\\lambda ^{\\Delta }f\(x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/eb8ce3e8e109120526ac15af5ed4b916810e212c)
for some choice of exponent Δ, and for all dilations λ. This is equivalent to f being a [homogeneous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_function "Homogeneous function") of degree Δ. 
Examples of scale-invariant functions are the [monomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomial "Monomial") f ( x ) = x n {\displaystyle f(x)=x^{n}} ![{\\displaystyle f\(x\)=x^{n}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8d09a7e3b534fe963d72c17a19e5bdd8b09eebc1), for which Δ = _n_ , in that clearly       f ( λ x ) = ( λ x ) n = λ n f ( x ) . {\displaystyle f(\lambda x)=(\lambda x)^{n}=\lambda ^{n}f(x)~.} ![{\\displaystyle f\(\\lambda x\)=\(\\lambda x\)^{n}=\\lambda ^{n}f\(x\)~.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/aec37a3d720a643f53e436748a10797c176924a0)
An example of a scale-invariant curve is the [logarithmic spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_spiral "Logarithmic spiral"), a kind of curve that often appears in nature. In [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates "Polar coordinates") (_r_ , _θ_), the spiral can be written as       θ = 1 b ln ⁡ ( r / a ) . {\displaystyle \theta ={\frac {1}{b}}\ln(r/a)~.} ![{\\displaystyle \\theta ={\\frac {1}{b}}\\ln\(r/a\)~.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ab311acd9649bf6fdecafd5f39edb75b4a90fe9e)
Allowing for rotations of the curve, it is invariant under all rescalings λ; that is, _θ_(_λr_) is identical to a rotated version  θ ( r ) + 1 b ln ⁡ λ {\displaystyle \theta (r)+{\frac {1}{b}}\ln \lambda } ![{\\displaystyle \\theta \(r\)+{\\frac {1}{b}}\\ln \\lambda }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ca88f4f8b05493006d78436a38f562d7b56917f5). 
### Projective geometry
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Projective geometry")]
The idea of scale invariance of a monomial generalizes in higher dimensions to the idea of a [homogeneous polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_polynomial "Homogeneous polynomial"), and more generally to a [homogeneous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_function "Homogeneous function"). Homogeneous functions are the natural denizens of [projective space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_space "Projective space"), and homogeneous polynomials are studied as [projective varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_varieties "Projective varieties") in [projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry "Projective geometry"). Projective geometry is a particularly rich field of mathematics; in its most abstract forms, the geometry of [schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_\(mathematics\) "Scheme \(mathematics\)"), it has connections to various topics in [string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory "String theory"). 
### Fractals
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Fractals")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/65/Kochsim.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Kochsim.gif)A [Koch curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch_curve "Koch curve") is [self-similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-similar "Self-similar").
It is sometimes said that [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal "Fractal") are scale-invariant, although more precisely, one should say that they are [self-similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-similar "Self-similar"). A fractal is equal to itself typically for only a discrete set of values λ, and even then a translation and rotation may have to be applied to match the fractal up to itself. 
Thus, for example, the [Koch curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koch_curve "Koch curve") scales with ∆ = 1, but the scaling holds only for values of _λ_ = 1/3 _n_ for integer n. In addition, the Koch curve scales not only at the origin, but, in a certain sense, "everywhere": miniature copies of itself can be found all along the curve. 
Some fractals may have multiple scaling factors at play at once; such scaling is studied with [multi-fractal analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-fractal_analysis "Multi-fractal analysis"). 
Periodic [external and internal rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/External_ray "External ray") are invariant curves . 
## Scale invariance in stochastic processes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Scale invariance in stochastic processes")]
If _P_(_f_ ) is the [average (expected)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_value "Expectation value") power at frequency f , then noise scales as       P ( f ) = λ Δ P ( λ f ) {\displaystyle P(f)=\lambda ^{\Delta }P(\lambda f)} ![{\\displaystyle P\(f\)=\\lambda ^{\\Delta }P\(\\lambda f\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/37ddce374ba5afbdedb24fd2faad8f55df22657f)
with Δ = 0 for [white noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_noise "White noise"), Δ = −1 for [pink noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_noise "Pink noise"), and Δ = −2 for [Brownian noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_noise "Brownian noise") (and more generally, [Brownian motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_motion "Brownian motion")). 
More precisely, scaling in stochastic systems concerns itself with the likelihood of choosing a particular configuration out of the set of all possible random configurations. This likelihood is given by the [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"). 
Examples of scale-invariant distributions are the [Pareto distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pareto_distribution "Pareto distribution") and the [Zipfian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipfian_distribution "Zipfian distribution"). 
### Scale-invariant Tweedie distributions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Scale-invariant Tweedie distributions")]
**[Tweedie distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweedie_distributions "Tweedie distributions")** are a special case of **[exponential dispersion models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_dispersion_model "Exponential dispersion model")** , a class of statistical models used to describe error distributions for the [generalized linear model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_linear_model "Generalized linear model") and characterized by [closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_\(mathematics\) "Closure \(mathematics\)") under additive and reproductive convolution as well as under scale transformation.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-J%C3%B8rgensen1997-1) These include a number of common distributions: the [normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution"), [Poisson distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_distribution "Poisson distribution") and [gamma distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_distribution "Gamma distribution"), as well as more unusual distributions like the compound Poisson-gamma distribution, positive [stable distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_distribution "Stable distribution"), and extreme stable distributions. Consequent to their inherent scale invariance Tweedie [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variable "Random variable") _Y_ demonstrate a [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance "Variance") var(_Y_) to [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean "Mean") E(_Y_) power law:       var ( Y ) = a [ E ( Y ) ] p {\displaystyle {\text{var}}\,(Y)=a[{\text{E}}\,(Y)]^{p}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\text{var}}\\,\(Y\)=a\[{\\text{E}}\\,\(Y\)\]^{p}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4940e0d134bf31f4009cc68ea9e66a87bd0c16a7),
where _a_ and _p_ are positive constants. This variance to mean power law is known in the physics literature as **fluctuation scaling** ,[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Eisler2008-2) and in the ecology literature as [Taylor's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor%27s_law "Taylor's law").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Kendal2011a-3)
Random sequences, governed by the Tweedie distributions and evaluated by the [method of expanding bins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweedie_distributions "Tweedie distributions") exhibit a [biconditional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_biconditional "Logical biconditional") relationship between the variance to mean power law and power law [autocorrelations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autocorrelation "Autocorrelation"). The [Wiener–Khinchin theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiener%E2%80%93Khinchin_theorem "Wiener–Khinchin theorem") further implies that for any sequence that exhibits a variance to mean power law under these conditions will also manifest [_1/f_ noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pink_noise "Pink noise").[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Kendal2011-4)
The [**Tweedie convergence theorem**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweedie_distributions "Tweedie distributions") provides a hypothetical explanation for the wide manifestation of fluctuation scaling and _1/f_ noise.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-J%C3%B8rgensen1994-5) It requires, in essence, that any exponential dispersion model that asymptotically manifests a variance to mean power law will be required express a [variance function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_exponential_family "Natural exponential family") that comes within the [domain of attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attractor "Attractor") of a Tweedie model. Almost all distribution functions with finite [cumulant generating functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulant "Cumulant") qualify as exponential dispersion models and most exponential dispersion models manifest variance functions of this form. Hence many probability distributions have variance functions that express this [asymptotic behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_expansion "Asymptotic expansion"), and the Tweedie distributions become foci of convergence for a wide range of data types.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Kendal2011-4)
Much as the [central limit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_limit_theorem "Central limit theorem") requires certain kinds of random variables to have as a focus of convergence the [Gaussian distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution") and express [white noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_noise "White noise"), the Tweedie convergence theorem requires certain non-Gaussian random variables to express _1/f_ noise and fluctuation scaling.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Kendal2011-4)
### Cosmology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Cosmology")]
In [physical cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_cosmology "Physical cosmology"), the power spectrum of the spatial distribution of the [cosmic microwave background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_microwave_background "Cosmic microwave background") is near to being a scale-invariant function. Although in mathematics this means that the spectrum is a power-law, in cosmology the term "scale-invariant" indicates that the amplitude, _P_(_k_), of [primordial fluctuations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primordial_fluctuations "Primordial fluctuations") as a function of [wave number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_number "Wave number"), k, is approximately constant, i.e. a flat spectrum. This pattern is consistent with the proposal of [cosmic inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_inflation "Cosmic inflation"). 
## Scale invariance in classical field theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Scale invariance in classical field theory")]
[Classical field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_field_theory "Classical field theory") is generically described by a field, or set of fields, _φ_ , that depend on coordinates, _x_. Valid field configurations are then determined by solving [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations "Differential equations") for _φ_ , and these equations are known as [field equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_equation "Field equation"). 
For a theory to be scale-invariant, its field equations should be invariant under a rescaling of the coordinates, combined with some specified rescaling of the fields,       x → λ x , {\displaystyle x\rightarrow \lambda x~,} ![{\\displaystyle x\\rightarrow \\lambda x~,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3c81cc1483b458884e79c09a92642e16c318c789)      φ → λ − Δ φ . {\displaystyle \varphi \rightarrow \lambda ^{-\Delta }\varphi ~.} ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi \\rightarrow \\lambda ^{-\\Delta }\\varphi ~.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/07d79783057f9f2f91c97f8f21aee440d4f54e9a)
The parameter Δ is known as the [scaling dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_dimension "Scaling dimension") of the field, and its value depends on the theory under consideration. Scale invariance will typically hold provided that no fixed length scale appears in the theory. Conversely, the presence of a fixed length scale indicates that a theory is **not** scale-invariant. 
A consequence of scale invariance is that given a solution of a scale-invariant field equation, we can automatically find other solutions by rescaling both the coordinates and the fields appropriately. In technical terms, given a solution, _φ_(_x_), one always has other solutions of the form       λ Δ φ ( λ x ) . {\displaystyle \lambda ^{\Delta }\varphi (\lambda x).} ![{\\displaystyle \\lambda ^{\\Delta }\\varphi \(\\lambda x\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ee823da2fc76db5b395810f21a060bc9af8b2d9c)
### Scale invariance of field configurations
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Scale invariance of field configurations")]
For a particular field configuration, _φ_(_x_), to be scale-invariant, we require that       φ ( x ) = λ − Δ φ ( λ x ) {\displaystyle \varphi (x)=\lambda ^{-\Delta }\varphi (\lambda x)} ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi \(x\)=\\lambda ^{-\\Delta }\\varphi \(\\lambda x\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0bcd17b395917b7a48e1405cc0261153138ad52a)
where Δ is, again, the [scaling dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_dimension "Scaling dimension") of the field. 
We note that this condition is rather restrictive. In general, solutions even of scale-invariant field equations will **not** be scale-invariant, and in such cases the symmetry is said to be [spontaneously broken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneously_broken "Spontaneously broken"). 
### Classical electromagnetism
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Classical electromagnetism")]
An example of a scale-invariant classical field theory is [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field "Electromagnetic field") with no charges or currents. The fields are the electric and magnetic fields, **E**(**x** ,_t_) and **B**(**x** ,_t_), while their field equations are [Maxwell's equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%27s_equations "Maxwell's equations"). 
With no charges or currents, [these field equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field#Light_as_an_electromagnetic_disturbance "Electromagnetic field") take the form of [wave equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_equation "Wave equation")      ∇ 2 E = 1 c 2 ∂ 2 E ∂ t 2 ∇ 2 B = 1 c 2 ∂ 2 B ∂ t 2 {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\nabla ^{2}\mathbf {E} ={\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\frac {\partial ^{2}\mathbf {E} }{\partial t^{2}}}\\\\[6pt]\nabla ^{2}\mathbf {B} ={\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\frac {\partial ^{2}\mathbf {B} }{\partial t^{2}}}\end{aligned}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\begin{aligned}\\nabla ^{2}\\mathbf {E} ={\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}\\mathbf {E} }{\\partial t^{2}}}\\\\\[6pt\]\\nabla ^{2}\\mathbf {B} ={\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}\\mathbf {B} }{\\partial t^{2}}}\\end{aligned}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/327625d8635404f3b471851c4fa51128477d3e7b)
where _c_ is the speed of light. 
These field equations are invariant under the transformation       x → λ x , t → λ t . {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}x\rightarrow \lambda x,\\\\[6pt]t\rightarrow \lambda t.\end{aligned}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\begin{aligned}x\\rightarrow \\lambda x,\\\\\[6pt\]t\\rightarrow \\lambda t.\\end{aligned}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4d78f42ef8c3ab073cf341bdb11fc014644fa28f)
Moreover, given solutions of Maxwell's equations, **E**(**x** , _t_) and **B**(**x** , _t_), it holds that **E**(_λ_**x** , _λt_) and **B**(_λ_**x** , _λt_) are also solutions. 
### Massless scalar field theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Massless scalar field theory")]
Another example of a scale-invariant classical field theory is the massless [scalar field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field_theory "Scalar field theory") (note that the name [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_\(physics\) "Scalar \(physics\)") is unrelated to scale invariance). The scalar field, _φ_(_**x**_ , _t_) is a function of a set of spatial variables, _**x**_ , and a time variable, t. 
Consider first the linear theory. Like the electromagnetic field equations above, the equation of motion for this theory is also a wave equation,       1 c 2 ∂ 2 φ ∂ t 2 − ∇ 2 φ = 0 , {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\frac {\partial ^{2}\varphi }{\partial t^{2}}}-\nabla ^{2}\varphi =0,} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}\\varphi }{\\partial t^{2}}}-\\nabla ^{2}\\varphi =0,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fdb8b3dbeedb115902a178090d6a232e5971cbcc)
and is invariant under the transformation       x → λ x , {\displaystyle x\rightarrow \lambda x,} ![{\\displaystyle x\\rightarrow \\lambda x,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f6988a6144338a45f2aeafcd168a1ccb0f96e64f)      t → λ t . {\displaystyle t\rightarrow \lambda t.} ![{\\displaystyle t\\rightarrow \\lambda t.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c60d215ab03ec3f21f30083a586b395877b0e387)
The name massless refers to the absence of a term  ∝ m 2 φ {\displaystyle \propto m^{2}\varphi } ![{\\displaystyle \\propto m^{2}\\varphi }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e7a8ed8b0812b54ce56ea2a4f0855db68c1aa65a) in the field equation. Such a term is often referred to as a `mass' term, and would break the invariance under the above transformation. In [relativistic field theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_field_theory "Relativistic field theory"), a mass-scale, m is physically equivalent to a fixed length scale through       L = ℏ m c , {\displaystyle L={\frac {\hbar }{mc}},} ![{\\displaystyle L={\\frac {\\hbar }{mc}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cbe487b4de4b15ead04824295ab0e9a5774e8604)
and so it should not be surprising that massive scalar field theory is _not_ scale-invariant. 
####  _φ_ 4 theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: φ4 theory")]
The field equations in the examples above are all [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear "Linear") in the fields, which has meant that the [scaling dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_dimension "Scaling dimension"), Δ, has not been so important. However, one usually requires that the scalar field [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_\(physics\) "Action \(physics\)") is dimensionless, and this fixes the [scaling dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_dimension "Scaling dimension") of φ. In particular,       Δ = D − 2 2 , {\displaystyle \Delta ={\frac {D-2}{2}},} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta ={\\frac {D-2}{2}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/191c7da5e26277e6f1e4f9ada05feadf1b46e06e)
where D is the combined number of spatial and time dimensions. 
Given this scaling dimension for φ, there are certain nonlinear modifications of massless scalar field theory which are also scale-invariant. One example is massless [φ4 theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_to_the_fourth "Phi to the fourth") for D = 4. The field equation is       1 c 2 ∂ 2 φ ∂ t 2 − ∇ 2 φ + g φ 3 = 0. {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\frac {\partial ^{2}\varphi }{\partial t^{2}}}-\nabla ^{2}\varphi +g\varphi ^{3}=0.} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {1}{c^{2}}}{\\frac {\\partial ^{2}\\varphi }{\\partial t^{2}}}-\\nabla ^{2}\\varphi +g\\varphi ^{3}=0.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d5d676c1dcd7ecd3a421aab5d5376a4054cc5726)
(Note that the name φ4 derives from the form of the [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phi_to_the_fourth#The_Lagrangian "Phi to the fourth"), which contains the fourth power of φ.) 
When D = 4 (e.g. three spatial dimensions and one time dimension), the scalar field scaling dimension is Δ = 1. The field equation is then invariant under the transformation       x → λ x , {\displaystyle x\rightarrow \lambda x,} ![{\\displaystyle x\\rightarrow \\lambda x,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f6988a6144338a45f2aeafcd168a1ccb0f96e64f)      t → λ t , {\displaystyle t\rightarrow \lambda t,} ![{\\displaystyle t\\rightarrow \\lambda t,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d1e3b9272f81555f3690e782946704c8b3455f98)      φ ( x ) → λ − 1 φ ( x ) . {\displaystyle \varphi (x)\rightarrow \lambda ^{-1}\varphi (x).} ![{\\displaystyle \\varphi \(x\)\\rightarrow \\lambda ^{-1}\\varphi \(x\).}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fced99a9b2591290695358cab2d402e57b1bd8cc)
The key point is that the parameter g must be dimensionless, otherwise one introduces a fixed length scale into the theory: For φ4 theory, this is only the case in D = 4. Note that under these transformations the argument of the function φ is unchanged. 
## Scale invariance in quantum field theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Scale invariance in quantum field theory")]
The scale-dependence of a [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory") (QFT) is characterised by the way its [coupling parameters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_constant "Coupling constant") depend on the energy-scale of a given physical process. This energy dependence is described by the [renormalization group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renormalization_group "Renormalization group"), and is encoded in the [beta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_function_\(physics\) "Beta function \(physics\)") of the theory. 
For a QFT to be scale-invariant, its coupling parameters must be independent of the energy-scale, and this is indicated by the vanishing of the beta-functions of the theory. Such theories are also known as [fixed points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renormalization_group "Renormalization group") of the corresponding renormalization group flow.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-6)
### Quantum electrodynamics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Quantum electrodynamics")]
A simple example of a scale-invariant QFT is the quantized electromagnetic field without charged particles. This theory actually has no coupling parameters (since [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon "Photon") are massless and non-interacting) and is therefore scale-invariant, much like the classical theory. 
However, in nature the electromagnetic field is coupled to charged particles, such as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron "Electron"). The QFT describing the interactions of photons and charged particles is [quantum electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_electrodynamics "Quantum electrodynamics") (QED), and this theory is not scale-invariant. We can see this from the [QED beta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_function_\(physics\)#Quantum_electrodynamics "Beta function \(physics\)"). This tells us that the [electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge "Electric charge") (which is the coupling parameter in the theory) increases with increasing energy. Therefore, while the quantized electromagnetic field without charged particles **is** scale-invariant, QED is **not** scale-invariant. 
### Massless scalar field theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Massless scalar field theory")]
Free, massless [quantized scalar field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field_\(quantum_field_theory\) "Scalar field \(quantum field theory\)") has no coupling parameters. Therefore, like the classical version, it is scale-invariant. In the language of the renormalization group, this theory is known as the [Gaussian fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_fixed_point "Gaussian fixed point"). 
However, even though the classical massless _φ_ 4 theory is scale-invariant in _D_ = 4, the quantized version is **not** scale-invariant. We can see this from the [beta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_function_\(physics\) "Beta function \(physics\)") for the coupling parameter, _g_. 
Even though the quantized massless _φ_ 4 is not scale-invariant, there do exist scale-invariant quantized scalar field theories other than the Gaussian fixed point. One example is the **Wilson–Fisher fixed point** , below. 
### Conformal field theory
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Conformal field theory")]
Scale-invariant QFTs are almost always invariant under the full [conformal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_symmetry "Conformal symmetry"), and the study of such QFTs is [conformal field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_field_theory "Conformal field theory") (CFT). [Operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_\(physics\) "Operator \(physics\)") in a CFT have a well-defined [scaling dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_dimension "Scaling dimension"), analogous to the [scaling dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_dimension "Scaling dimension"), _∆_ , of a classical field discussed above. However, the scaling dimensions of operators in a CFT typically differ from those of the fields in the corresponding classical theory. The additional contributions appearing in the CFT are known as [anomalous scaling dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalous_scaling_dimension "Anomalous scaling dimension"). 
### Scale and conformal anomalies
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Scale and conformal anomalies")]
The φ4 theory example above demonstrates that the coupling parameters of a quantum field theory can be scale-dependent even if the corresponding classical field theory is scale-invariant (or conformally invariant). If this is the case, the classical scale (or conformal) invariance is said to be [anomalous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_anomaly "Conformal anomaly"). A classically scale-invariant field theory, where scale invariance is broken by quantum effects, provides an explication of the nearly exponential expansion of the early universe called [cosmic inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_\(cosmology\) "Inflation \(cosmology\)"), as long as the theory can be studied through [perturbation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perturbation_theory "Perturbation theory").[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-7)
## Phase transitions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Phase transitions")]
In [statistical mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics "Statistical mechanics"), as a system undergoes a [phase transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition "Phase transition"), its fluctuations are described by a scale-invariant [statistical field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_field_theory "Statistical field theory"). For a system in equilibrium (i.e. time-independent) in D spatial dimensions, the corresponding statistical field theory is formally similar to a D-dimensional CFT. The scaling dimensions in such problems are usually referred to as [critical exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_exponent "Critical exponent"), and one can in principle compute these exponents in the appropriate CFT. 
### The Ising model
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: The Ising model")]
An example that links together many of the ideas in this article is the phase transition of the [Ising model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ising_model "Ising model"), a simple model of [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnet "Ferromagnet") substances. This is a statistical mechanics model, which also has a description in terms of conformal field theory. The system consists of an array of lattice sites, which form a D-dimensional periodic lattice. Associated with each lattice site is a [magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_moment "Magnetic moment"), or [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_\(physics\) "Spin \(physics\)"), and this spin can take either the value +1 or −1. (These states are also called up and down, respectively.) 
The key point is that the Ising model has a spin-spin interaction, making it energetically favourable for two adjacent spins to be aligned. On the other hand, thermal fluctuations typically introduce a randomness into the alignment of spins. At some critical temperature, _T c_ , [spontaneous magnetization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_magnetization "Spontaneous magnetization") is said to occur. This means that below _T c_ the spin-spin interaction will begin to dominate, and there is some net alignment of spins in one of the two directions. 
An example of the kind of physical quantities one would like to calculate at this critical temperature is the correlation between spins separated by a distance r. This has the generic behaviour:       G ( r ) ∝ 1 r D − 2 + η , {\displaystyle G(r)\propto {\frac {1}{r^{D-2+\eta }}},} ![{\\displaystyle G\(r\)\\propto {\\frac {1}{r^{D-2+\\eta }}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f9e4a2d21da036d2f4dbb68f83057599310365ed)
for some particular value of  η {\displaystyle \eta } ![{\\displaystyle \\eta }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e4d701857cf5fbec133eebaf94deadf722537f64), which is an example of a critical exponent. 
#### CFT description
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: CFT description")]
The fluctuations at temperature _T c_ are scale-invariant, and so the Ising model at this phase transition is expected to be described by a scale-invariant statistical field theory. In fact, this theory is the **Wilson–Fisher fixed point** , a particular scale-invariant [scalar field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field_\(quantum_field_theory\) "Scalar field \(quantum field theory\)"). 
In this context, _G_(_r_) is understood as a [correlation function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_function "Correlation function") of scalar fields,       ⟨ ϕ ( 0 ) ϕ ( r ) ⟩ ∝ 1 r D − 2 + η . {\displaystyle \langle \phi (0)\phi (r)\rangle \propto {\frac {1}{r^{D-2+\eta }}}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\phi \(0\)\\phi \(r\)\\rangle \\propto {\\frac {1}{r^{D-2+\\eta }}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8758007ead92ba3fd7616a6987ea6b9b5f48b080)
Now we can fit together a number of the ideas seen already. 
From the above, one sees that the critical exponent, η, for this phase transition, is also an **anomalous dimension**. This is because the classical dimension of the scalar field,       Δ = D − 2 2 {\displaystyle \Delta ={\frac {D-2}{2}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta ={\\frac {D-2}{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/760abf62a31f0028dff426098a2f775b43c397ca)
is modified to become       Δ = D − 2 + η 2 , {\displaystyle \Delta ={\frac {D-2+\eta }{2}},} ![{\\displaystyle \\Delta ={\\frac {D-2+\\eta }{2}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ed178edba8fe63da485e05ba6a49faa4ca24de11)
where D is the number of dimensions of the Ising model lattice. 
So this **anomalous dimension** in the conformal field theory is the _same_ as a particular critical exponent of the Ising model phase transition. 
Note that for dimension _D_ ≡ 4− _ε_ , η can be calculated approximately, using the **epsilon expansion** , and one finds that       η = ϵ 2 54 + O ( ϵ 3 ) {\displaystyle \eta ={\frac {\epsilon ^{2}}{54}}+O(\epsilon ^{3})} ![{\\displaystyle \\eta ={\\frac {\\epsilon ^{2}}{54}}+O\(\\epsilon ^{3}\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/01bf4dc8bc1ffd39b51e8ed3e67c43936ccc33d7).
In the physically interesting case of three spatial dimensions, we have ε=1, and so this expansion is not strictly reliable. However, a semi-quantitative prediction is that η is numerically small in three dimensions. 
On the other hand, in the two-dimensional case the Ising model is exactly soluble. In particular, it is equivalent to one of the [minimal models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_model_\(physics\) "Minimal model \(physics\)"), a family of well-understood CFTs, and it is possible to compute η (and the other critical exponents) exactly,       η D = 2 = 1 4 {\displaystyle \eta _{_{D=2}}={\frac {1}{4}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\eta _{_{D=2}}={\\frac {1}{4}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/354445ed276c5df1983fd6b5a732184e0adca0a9).
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Schramm–Loewner evolution")]
The anomalous dimensions in certain two-dimensional CFTs can be related to the typical [fractal dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_dimension "Fractal dimension") of random walks, where the random walks are defined via [Schramm–Loewner evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schramm%E2%80%93Loewner_evolution "Schramm–Loewner evolution") (SLE). As we have seen above, CFTs describe the physics of phase transitions, and so one can relate the critical exponents of certain phase transitions to these fractal dimensions. Examples include the 2 _d_ critical Ising model and the more general 2 _d_ critical [Potts model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potts_model "Potts model"). Relating other 2 _d_ CFTs to SLE is an active area of research. 
## Universality
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Universality")]
A phenomenon known as [universality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universality_\(dynamical_systems\) "Universality \(dynamical systems\)") is seen in a large variety of physical systems. It expresses the idea that different microscopic physics can give rise to the same scaling behaviour at a phase transition. A canonical example of universality involves the following two systems: 
  * The [Ising model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ising_model "Ising model") phase transition, described above.
  * The [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid "Liquid")-[vapour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapour "Vapour") transition in classical fluids.


Even though the microscopic physics of these two systems is completely different, their critical exponents turn out to be the same. Moreover, one can calculate these exponents using the same statistical field theory. The key observation is that at a phase transition or [critical point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_point_\(thermodynamics\) "Critical point \(thermodynamics\)"), fluctuations occur at all length scales, and thus one should look for a scale-invariant statistical field theory to describe the phenomena. In a sense, universality is the observation that there are relatively few such scale-invariant theories. 
The set of different microscopic theories described by the same scale-invariant theory is known as a [universality class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universality_class "Universality class"). Other examples of systems which belong to a universality class are: 
  * [Avalanches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avalanche "Avalanche") in piles of sand. The likelihood of an avalanche is in power-law proportion to the size of the avalanche, and avalanches are seen to occur at all size scales.
  * The frequency of [network outages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_outage "Network outage") on the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet "Internet"), as a function of size and duration.
  * The frequency of citations of journal articles, considered in the network of all citations amongst all papers, as a function of the number of citations in a given paper.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
  * The formation and propagation of cracks and tears in materials ranging from steel to rock to paper. The variations of the direction of the tear, or the roughness of a fractured surface, are in power-law proportion to the size scale.
  * The [electrical breakdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_breakdown "Electrical breakdown") of [dielectrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric "Dielectric"), which resemble cracks and tears.
  * The [percolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percolation "Percolation") of fluids through disordered media, such as [petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum "Petroleum") through fractured rock beds, or water through filter paper, such as in [chromatography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatography "Chromatography"). Power-law scaling connects the rate of flow to the distribution of fractures.
  * The [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion "Diffusion") of [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule "Molecule") in [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_\(chemistry\) "Solution \(chemistry\)"), and the phenomenon of [diffusion-limited aggregation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion-limited_aggregation "Diffusion-limited aggregation").
  * The distribution of rocks of different sizes in an aggregate mixture that is being shaken (with gravity acting on the rocks).


The key observation is that, for all of these different systems, the behaviour resembles a [phase transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition "Phase transition"), and that the language of statistical mechanics and scale-invariant [statistical field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_field_theory "Statistical field theory") may be applied to describe them.

### Newtonian fluid mechanics with no applied forces
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Newtonian fluid mechanics with no applied forces")]
Under certain circumstances, [fluid mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_mechanics "Fluid mechanics") is a scale-invariant classical field theory. The fields are the velocity of the fluid flow,  u ( x , t ) {\displaystyle \mathbf {u} (\mathbf {x} ,t)} ![{\\displaystyle \\mathbf {u} \(\\mathbf {x} ,t\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/07e81e5fe0eaa47b53063aac6aec1e68652845fd), the fluid density,  ρ ( x , t ) {\displaystyle \rho (\mathbf {x} ,t)} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho \(\\mathbf {x} ,t\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b1d439aa2bce46ee391a6b495d772aeec11eebe1), and the fluid pressure,  P ( x , t ) {\displaystyle P(\mathbf {x} ,t)} ![{\\displaystyle P\(\\mathbf {x} ,t\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ae1abfafb8269770ffd56b37048ee421a8682e28). ### Computer vision
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Scale_invariance&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Computer vision")]
Main article: [Scale space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_space "Scale space")
In [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision "Computer vision") and [biological vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_vision "Biological vision"), scaling transformations arise because of the perspective image mapping and because of objects having different physical size in the world. In these areas, scale invariance refers to local image descriptors or visual representations of the image data that remain invariant when the local scale in the image domain is changed.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Lin13PONE-8) Detecting local maxima over scales of normalized derivative responses provides a general framework for obtaining scale invariance from image data.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Lindeberg1998-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance#cite_note-Lin14CompVis-10) Examples of applications include [blob detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blob_detection "Blob detection"), [corner detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corner_detection "Corner detection"), [ridge detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridge_detection "Ridge detection"), and object recognition via the [scale-invariant feature transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale-invariant_feature_transform "Scale-invariant feature transform").
