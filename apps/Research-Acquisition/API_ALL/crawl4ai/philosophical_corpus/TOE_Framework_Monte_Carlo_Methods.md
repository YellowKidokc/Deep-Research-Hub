From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Probabilistic problem-solving algorithm
Not to be confused with [Monte Carlo algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_algorithm "Monte Carlo algorithm").
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/ff/Approximation_d%27une_distribution_normale.gif/330px-Approximation_d%27une_distribution_normale.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Approximation_d%27une_distribution_normale.gif)The approximation of a [normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_distribution "Normal distribution") with a Monte Carlo method
**Monte Carlo methods** , also called the **Monte Carlo experiments** or **Monte Carlo simulations** , are a broad class of [computational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computation "Computation") [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm "Algorithm") based on [repeated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resampling_\(statistics\) "Resampling \(statistics\)") [random sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sampling "Random sampling") for obtaining numerical results. The underlying concept is to use [randomness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomness "Randomness") to solve [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_system "Deterministic system") problems. 
Monte Carlo methods are mainly used in three distinct problem classes: [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization "Optimization"), [numerical integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_integration "Numerical integration"), and [non-uniform random variate generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-uniform_random_variate_generation "Non-uniform random variate generation"), available for modeling [phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon "Phenomenon") with significant input [uncertainties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_quantification "Uncertainty quantification"), e.g. risk assessments for nuclear power plants. Monte Carlo methods are often implemented using computer simulations. They can provide approximate solutions to problems too complex for [mathematical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_analysis "Mathematical analysis"). 
## Overview
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview")]
The name comes from the [Monte Carlo Casino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_Casino "Monte Carlo Casino") in [Monaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monaco "Monaco"), where the primary developer of the method, [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician "Mathematician") [Stanisław Ulam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Ulam "Stanisław Ulam"), was inspired by his uncle's [gambling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gambling "Gambling") habits. Monte Carlo methods are widely used in various fields of science, engineering, and mathematics, such as physics, chemistry, biology, statistics, artificial intelligence, finance, and cryptography. They have also been applied to social sciences, such as sociology, psychology, and political science. Monte Carlo methods have been recognized as one of the most important and influential ideas of the 20th century, and they have enabled many scientific and technological breakthroughs.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Monte Carlo methods also have some limitations and challenges, such as the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost, the [curse of dimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_dimensionality "Curse of dimensionality"), the reliability of random number generators, and the verification and validation of the results. Monte Carlo methods vary, but tend to follow a particular pattern: 
  1. Define a domain of possible inputs.
  2. Generate inputs randomly from a [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") over the domain.
  3. Perform a [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_algorithm "Deterministic algorithm") computation of the outputs.
  4. Aggregate the results.

[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/d4/Pi_monte_carlo_all.gif/330px-Pi_monte_carlo_all.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pi_monte_carlo_all.gif) Monte Carlo method applied to approximating the value of π
For example, consider a [quadrant (circular sector)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_sector#Quadrant "Circular sector") inscribed in a [unit square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_square "Unit square"). Given that the ratio of their areas is ⁠π/4⁠, the value of [π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi "Pi") can be approximated using the Monte Carlo method:[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEKalosWhitlock2008-1)
  1. Draw a square, then [inscribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_figure "Inscribed figure") a quadrant within it.
  2. [Uniformly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_\(continuous\) "Uniform distribution \(continuous\)") scatter a given number of points over the square.
  3. Count the number of points inside the quadrant, i.e. having a distance from the origin of less than 1.
  4. The ratio of the inside-count and the total-sample-count is an estimate of the ratio of the two areas, ⁠π/4⁠. Multiply the result by 4 to estimate π.


In this procedure, the domain of inputs is the square that circumscribes the quadrant. One can generate random inputs by scattering grains over the square, then performing a computation on each input to test whether it falls within the quadrant. Aggregating the results yields our final result, the approximation of π.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] There are two important considerations: 
  1. If the points are not uniformly distributed, the approximation will be poor.
  2. The approximation improves as more points are randomly placed in the whole square.


Uses of Monte Carlo methods require large amounts of random numbers, and their use benefitted greatly from [pseudorandom number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generators "Pseudorandom number generators"), which are far quicker to use than the tables of random numbers that had been previously employed. 
## Applications
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Applications")]
Monte Carlo methods are often used in [physical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics") and [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") problems and are most useful when it is difficult or impossible to use other approaches. Monte Carlo methods are mainly used in three problem classes:[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-2) [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization "Optimization"), [numerical integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_integration "Numerical integration"), and generating draws from a [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"). 
In physics-related problems, Monte Carlo methods are useful for simulating systems with many [coupled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_\(physics\) "Coupling \(physics\)") [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom "Degrees of freedom"), such as fluids, disordered materials, strongly coupled solids, and cellular structures, e.g. [cellular Potts model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_Potts_model "Cellular Potts model"), [interacting particle systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interacting_particle_systems "Interacting particle systems"), [McKean–Vlasov processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McKean%E2%80%93Vlasov_process "McKean–Vlasov process"), [kinetic models of gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_theory_of_gases "Kinetic theory of gases").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Other examples include modeling phenomena with significant [uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty "Uncertainty") in inputs such as the calculation of [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk "Risk") in business and, in mathematics, evaluation of multidimensional [definite integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral "Integral") with complicated [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions "Boundary conditions"). In application to systems engineering problems (space, [oil exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oil_exploration "Oil exploration"), aircraft design, etc.), Monte Carlo–based predictions of failure, [cost overruns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_overrun "Cost overrun") and schedule overruns are routinely better than human intuition or alternative "soft" methods.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-3)
In principle, Monte Carlo methods can be used to solve any problem having a probabilistic interpretation. By the [law of large numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_large_numbers "Law of large numbers"), integrals described by the [expected value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_value "Expected value") of some random variable can be approximated by taking the [empirical mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean_and_sample_covariance "Sample mean and sample covariance") (a.k.a. the 'sample mean') of independent samples of the variable. When the [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") of the variable is parameterized, mathematicians often use a [Markov chain Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain_Monte_Carlo "Markov chain Monte Carlo") (MCMC) sampler.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-6) The central idea is to design a judicious [Markov chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain "Markov chain") model with a prescribed [stationary probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_probability_distribution "Stationary probability distribution"). That is, in the limit, the samples being generated by the MCMC method will be samples from the desired (target) distribution.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-7)[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-8) By the [ergodic theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic_theorem "Ergodic theorem"), the stationary distribution is approximated by the [empirical measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_measure "Empirical measure") of the random states of the MCMC sampler.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
In other problems, the objective is generating draws from a sequence of probability distributions satisfying a nonlinear evolution equation. These flows of probability distributions can always be interpreted as the distributions of the random states of a [Markov process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process "Markov process") whose transition probabilities depend on the distributions of the current random states (see [McKean–Vlasov processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McKean%E2%80%93Vlasov_process "McKean–Vlasov process"), [nonlinear filtering equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_filter "Particle filter")).[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-kol10-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dp13-10) In other instances, a flow of probability distributions with an increasing level of sampling complexity arise (path spaces models with an increasing time horizon, Boltzmann–Gibbs measures associated with decreasing temperature parameters, and many others). These models can also be seen as the evolution of the law of the random states of a nonlinear Markov chain.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dp13-10)[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-11)
A natural way to simulate these sophisticated nonlinear Markov processes is to sample multiple copies of the process, replacing in the evolution equation the unknown distributions of the random states by the sampled empirical measures. In contrast with traditional Monte Carlo and MCMC methodologies, these [mean-field particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean-field_particle_methods "Mean-field particle methods") techniques rely on sequential interacting samples. The terminology _mean field_ reflects the fact that each of the _samples_ (a.k.a. particles, individuals, walkers, agents, creatures, or phenotypes) interacts with the empirical measures of the process. When the size of the system tends to infinity, these random empirical measures converge to the deterministic distribution of the random states of the nonlinear Markov chain, so that the statistical interaction between particles vanishes.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
## Simple Monte Carlo
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Simple Monte Carlo")]
Suppose one wants to know the expected value  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161) of a population (and knows that  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161) exists), but does not have a formula available to compute it. The simple Monte Carlo method gives an estimate for  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161) by running  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) simulations and averaging the simulations' results. It has no restrictions on the probability distribution of the inputs to the simulations, requiring only that the inputs are randomly generated and are independent of each other and that  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161) exists. A sufficiently large  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) will produce a value for  m {\displaystyle m} ![{\\displaystyle m}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0a07d98bb302f3856cbabc47b2b9016692e3f7bc) that is arbitrarily close to  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161); more formally, it will be the case that, for any  ϵ > 0 {\displaystyle \epsilon >0} ![{\\displaystyle \\epsilon >0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/568095ad3924314374a5ab68fae17343661f2a71),  | μ − m | ≤ ϵ {\displaystyle |\mu -m|\leq \epsilon } ![{\\displaystyle |\\mu -m|\\leq \\epsilon }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/32a52cf007e700fb1c72ab071cdb20a9d770f3c0). [[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:1-12) Typically, the algorithm to obtain  m {\displaystyle m} ![{\\displaystyle m}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0a07d98bb302f3856cbabc47b2b9016692e3f7bc) is 
```
_s_ = 0; 
**for** _i_ = 1 to _n_ **do**
   run the simulation for the _i_th time, giving result _ri_;
   _s_ = _s_ + _ri_;
**repeat**
_m_ = _s_ / _n_;

```

### Examples
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Examples")]
Suppose we want to know how many times we should expect to throw three eight-sided dice for the total of the dice throws to be at least  T {\displaystyle T} ![{\\displaystyle T}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ec7200acd984a1d3a3d7dc455e262fbe54f7f6e0). We know the expected value exists. The dice throws are randomly distributed and independent of each other. So simple Monte Carlo is applicable: 
```
_s_ = 0; 
**for** _i_ = 1 to _n_ **do**
   throw the three dice until _T_ is met or first exceeded; _ri_ = the number of throws;
   _s_ = _s_ + _ri_;
**repeat**
_m_ = _s_ / _n_;

```

If  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) is large enough,  m {\displaystyle m} ![{\\displaystyle m}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0a07d98bb302f3856cbabc47b2b9016692e3f7bc) will be within  ϵ {\displaystyle \epsilon } ![{\\displaystyle \\epsilon }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3837cad72483d97bcdde49c85d3b7b859fb3fd2) of  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161) for any  ϵ > 0 {\displaystyle \epsilon >0} ![{\\displaystyle \\epsilon >0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/568095ad3924314374a5ab68fae17343661f2a71). 
### Determining a sufficiently large _n_

#### General formula
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: General formula")]
Let  ϵ = | μ − m | > 0 {\displaystyle \epsilon =|\mu -m|>0} ![{\\displaystyle \\epsilon =|\\mu -m|>0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/afc33c179ff251a2caa5bdcbc5b93292023ecfe6). Choose the desired confidence level – the percent chance that, when the Monte Carlo algorithm completes,  m {\displaystyle m} ![{\\displaystyle m}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0a07d98bb302f3856cbabc47b2b9016692e3f7bc) is indeed within  ϵ {\displaystyle \epsilon } ![{\\displaystyle \\epsilon }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3837cad72483d97bcdde49c85d3b7b859fb3fd2) of  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161). Let  z {\displaystyle z} ![{\\displaystyle z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bf368e72c009decd9b6686ee84a375632e11de98) be the [ z {\displaystyle z} ![{\\displaystyle z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bf368e72c009decd9b6686ee84a375632e11de98)-score](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z-score "Z-score") corresponding to that confidence level. 
Let  s 2 {\displaystyle s^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle s^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/58d7841cee3671436949ee789b84a848fd150bd3) be the estimated variance, sometimes called the "sample" variance; it is the variance of the results obtained from a relatively small number  k {\displaystyle k} ![{\\displaystyle k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3c9a2c7b599b37105512c5d570edc034056dd40) of "sample" simulations. Choose a  k {\displaystyle k} ![{\\displaystyle k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3c9a2c7b599b37105512c5d570edc034056dd40); Driels and Shin observe that "_even for sample sizes an order of magnitude lower than the number required, the calculation of that number is quite stable._ "[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:2-13) The following algorithm computes  s 2 {\displaystyle s^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle s^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/58d7841cee3671436949ee789b84a848fd150bd3) in one pass while minimizing the possibility that accumulated numerical error produces erroneous results:[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:1-12)
```
_s1_ = 0; 
run the simulation for the first time, producing result _r_1;
_m_1 = _r_1; //_mi_ is the mean of the first _i_ simulations
**for** i = 2 to _k_ **do**
   run the simulation for the _i_th time, producing result _ri_;
   _δi_ = _ri_ - _mi_−1;
   _mi = mi-1_ + (1/_i_)_δi_;
   _si = si-1_ + ((_i_ - 1)/_i_)(_δi_)2;
**repeat**
_s2_ = _sk_/(_k_ - 1);

```

The value  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) is sufficiently large when       n ≥ s 2 z 2 / ϵ 2 . {\displaystyle n\geq s^{2}z^{2}/\epsilon ^{2}.} ![{\\displaystyle n\\geq s^{2}z^{2}/\\epsilon ^{2}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/8f4021665b0e7ba7b830f560647349ca48d00102)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:1-12)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:2-13)
If  n ≤ k {\displaystyle n\leq k} ![{\\displaystyle n\\leq k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9b0ac61b7d1fad06bf9d004c774bd7931b8b51bf), then  m k = m {\displaystyle m_{k}=m} ![{\\displaystyle m_{k}=m}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/44c1258c7f3dabee761d5e98b61c742abdb316ee); sufficient sample simulations were done to ensure that  m k {\displaystyle m_{k}} ![{\\displaystyle m_{k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0d5e3b1ba705c5adda8381cb22580d26d64968f8) is within  ϵ {\displaystyle \epsilon } ![{\\displaystyle \\epsilon }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3837cad72483d97bcdde49c85d3b7b859fb3fd2) of  μ {\displaystyle \mu } ![{\\displaystyle \\mu }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9fd47b2a39f7a7856952afec1f1db72c67af6161). If  n > k {\displaystyle n>k} ![{\\displaystyle n>k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a8afbc0693bee3f48a31d2c991ddc8b6b4a35322), then  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) simulations can be run "from scratch," or, since  k {\displaystyle k} ![{\\displaystyle k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3c9a2c7b599b37105512c5d570edc034056dd40) simulations have already been done, one can just run  n − k {\displaystyle n-k} ![{\\displaystyle n-k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b98e1d6a69bccd09a4b9b69bdf03a08c1706c8c1) more simulations and add their results into those from the sample simulations: 
```
_s_ = _mk_ * _k_; 
for i = _k_ + 1 to _n_ do
   run the simulation for the _i_th time, giving result _ri_;
   _s_ = _s_ + _ri_;
_m_ = _s_ / _n_;

```

## Computational costs
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Computational costs")]
Despite its conceptual and algorithmic simplicity, the computational cost associated with a Monte Carlo simulation can be staggeringly high. In general the method requires many samples to get a good approximation, which may incur an arbitrarily large total runtime if the processing time of a single sample is high.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-sw09-14) Although this is a severe limitation in very complex problems, the [embarrassingly parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embarrassingly_parallel "Embarrassingly parallel") nature of the algorithm allows this large cost to be reduced (perhaps to a feasible level) through [parallel computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_computing "Parallel computing") strategies in local processors, clusters, cloud computing, GPU, FPGA, etc.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-15)[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-16)[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-17)[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-18) In financial and safety-critical applications, floating-point non-determinism across hardware platforms can compound these costs, as results may vary between runs or across different processors (x86, ARM, GPU), sometimes requiring redundant simulations or consensus mechanisms to verify numerical correctness.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-19)
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: History")]
Before the Monte Carlo method was developed, simulations tested a previously understood deterministic problem, and statistical sampling was used to estimate uncertainties in the simulations. Monte Carlo simulations invert this approach, solving deterministic problems using [probabilistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability "Probability") [metaheuristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaheuristic "Metaheuristic") (see [simulated annealing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulated_annealing "Simulated annealing")).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
An early variant of the Monte Carlo method was devised to solve the [Buffon's needle problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffon%27s_needle_problem "Buffon's needle problem"), in which π can be estimated by dropping needles on a floor made of parallel equidistant strips. In the 1930s, [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi "Enrico Fermi") first experimented with the Monte Carlo method while studying neutron diffusion, but he did not publish this work.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMetropolis1987-20)
In the late 1940s, [Stanisław Ulam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Ulam "Stanisław Ulam") invented the modern version of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo method while he was working on nuclear weapons projects at the [Los Alamos National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory "Los Alamos National Laboratory"). In 1946, nuclear weapons physicists at Los Alamos were investigating neutron diffusion in the core of a nuclear weapon.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMetropolis1987-20)
Despite having most of the necessary data, such as the average distance a neutron would travel in a substance before it collided with an atomic nucleus and how much energy the neutron was likely to give off following a collision, the Los Alamos physicists were unable to solve the problem using conventional, deterministic mathematical methods. Ulam proposed using random experiments. He recounts his inspiration as follows: 
> The first thoughts and attempts I made to practice [the Monte Carlo Method] were suggested by a question which occurred to me in 1946 as I was convalescing from an illness and playing solitaires. The question was what are the chances that a [Canfield solitaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canfield_\(solitaire\) "Canfield \(solitaire\)") laid out with 52 cards will come out successfully? After spending a lot of time trying to estimate them by pure combinatorial calculations, I wondered whether a more practical method than "abstract thinking" might not be to lay it out say one hundred times and simply observe and count the number of successful plays. This was already possible to envisage with the beginning of the new era of fast computers, and I immediately thought of problems of neutron diffusion and other questions of mathematical physics, and more generally how to change processes described by certain differential equations into an equivalent form interpretable as a succession of random operations. Later [in 1946], I described the idea to [John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann "John von Neumann"), and we began to plan actual calculations.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEEckhardt1987-21)
Being secret, the work of von Neumann and Ulam required a code name.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMazhdrakovBenovValkanov2018250-22) A colleague of von Neumann and Ulam, [Nicholas Metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Metropolis "Nicholas Metropolis"), suggested using the name _Monte Carlo_ , which refers to the [Monte Carlo Casino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_Casino "Monte Carlo Casino") in [Monaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monaco "Monaco") where Ulam's uncle would borrow money from relatives to gamble.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMetropolis1987-20) Monte Carlo methods were central to the [simulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation "Simulation") required for further postwar development of nuclear weapons, including the design of the H-bomb, though severely limited by the computational tools at the time. Von Neumann, [Nicholas Metropolis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_Metropolis "Nicholas Metropolis") and others programmed the [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC "ENIAC") computer to perform the first fully automated Monte Carlo calculations, of a [fission weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon_design#Pure_fission_weapons "Nuclear weapon design") core, in the spring of 1948.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-ENIAC-23)
In the 1950s Monte Carlo methods were used at [Los Alamos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Los_Alamos_National_Laboratory "Los Alamos National Laboratory") for the development of the [hydrogen bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bomb "Hydrogen bomb"), and became popularized in the fields of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry"), and [operations research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operations_research "Operations research"). The [Rand Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rand_Corporation "Rand Corporation") and the [U.S. Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Air_Force "U.S. Air Force") were two of the major organizations responsible for funding and disseminating information on Monte Carlo methods during this time, and they began to find a wide application in many different fields.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
The theory of more sophisticated mean-field type particle Monte Carlo methods had certainly started by the mid-1960s, with the work of [Henry P. McKean Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_McKean "Henry McKean") on Markov interpretations of a class of nonlinear parabolic partial differential equations arising in fluid mechanics.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-mck67-24)[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-25) An earlier pioneering article by [Theodore E. Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Harris_\(mathematician\) "Ted Harris \(mathematician\)") and Herman Kahn, published in 1951, used mean-field [genetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_algorithm "Genetic algorithm")-type Monte Carlo methods for estimating particle transmission energies.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-26)
Mean-field genetic type Monte Carlo methodologies are also used as heuristic natural search algorithms (a.k.a. [metaheuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaheuristic "Metaheuristic")) in evolutionary computing. The origins of these mean-field computational techniques can be traced to 1950 and 1954 with the work of [Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing "Alan Turing") on genetic type mutation-selection learning machines[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-27) and the articles by [Nils Aall Barricelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nils_Aall_Barricelli "Nils Aall Barricelli") at the [Institute for Advanced Study](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study "Institute for Advanced Study") in [Princeton, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey "Princeton, New Jersey").[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-28)[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-29)
[Quantum Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_Monte_Carlo "Quantum Monte Carlo"), and more specifically [diffusion Monte Carlo methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_Monte_Carlo "Diffusion Monte Carlo") can also be interpreted as a mean-field particle Monte Carlo approximation of [Feynman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Feynman "Richard Feynman")–[Kac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Kac "Mark Kac") path integrals.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dp04-30)[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dmm002-31)[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dmm00m-32)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dm-esaim03-33)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-caffarel1-34)[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-caffarel2-35)[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-h84-36) The origins of Quantum Monte Carlo methods are often attributed to Enrico Fermi and [Robert Richtmyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_D._Richtmyer "Robert D. Richtmyer") who developed in 1948 a mean-field particle interpretation of neutron-chain reactions,[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-37) but the first heuristic-like and genetic type particle algorithm (a.k.a. Resampled or Reconfiguration Monte Carlo methods) for estimating ground state energies of quantum systems (in reduced matrix models) is due to Jack H. Hetherington in 1984.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-h84-36) In molecular chemistry, the use of genetic heuristic-like particle methodologies (a.k.a. pruning and enrichment strategies) can be traced back to 1955 with the seminal work of [Marshall N. Rosenbluth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Rosenbluth "Marshall Rosenbluth") and [Arianna W. Rosenbluth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianna_W._Rosenbluth "Arianna W. Rosenbluth").[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:0-38)
The use of [sequential Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_Monte_Carlo_method "Sequential Monte Carlo method") in advanced [signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_processing "Signal processing") and [Bayesian inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference "Bayesian inference") is more recent. It was in 1993, that Gordon et al., published in their seminal work[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-39) the first application of a Monte Carlo [resampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resampling_\(statistics\) "Resampling \(statistics\)") algorithm in Bayesian statistical inference. The authors named their algorithm 'the bootstrap filter', and demonstrated that compared to other filtering methods, their bootstrap algorithm does not require any assumption about that state-space or the noise of the system. Another pioneering article in this field was Genshiro Kitagawa's, on a related "Monte Carlo filter",[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-40) and the ones by Pierre Del Moral[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dm9622-41) and Himilcon Carvalho, Pierre Del Moral, André Monin and Gérard Salut[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-42) on particle filters published in the mid-1990s. Particle filters were also developed in signal processing in 1989–1992 by P. Del Moral, J. C. Noyer, G. Rigal, and G. Salut in the LAAS-CNRS in a series of restricted and classified research reports with STCAN (Service Technique des Constructions et Armes Navales), the IT company DIGILOG, and the [LAAS-CNRS](https://www.laas.fr/public/en) (the Laboratory for Analysis and Architecture of Systems) on radar/sonar and GPS signal processing problems.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-43)[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-44)[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-45)[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-46)[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-47)[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-48) These Sequential Monte Carlo methodologies can be interpreted as an acceptance-rejection sampler equipped with an interacting recycling mechanism. 
From 1950 to 1996, all the publications on sequential Monte Carlo methodologies, including the pruning and resample Monte Carlo methods introduced in computational physics and molecular chemistry, present natural and heuristic-like algorithms applied to different situations without a single proof of their consistency, nor a discussion on the bias of the estimates and on genealogical and ancestral tree based algorithms. The mathematical foundations and the first rigorous analysis of these particle algorithms were written by Pierre Del Moral in 1996.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dm9622-41)[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:22-49)
Branching type particle methodologies with varying population sizes were also developed in the end of the 1990s by Dan Crisan, Jessica Gaines and Terry Lyons,[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:42-50)[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-51)[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-52) and by Dan Crisan, Pierre Del Moral and Terry Lyons.[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-:52-53) Further developments in this field were described in 1999 to 2001 by P. Del Moral, A. Guionnet and L. Miclo.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dmm002-31)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dg99-54)[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-dg01-55)
## Definitions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Definitions")]
There is no consensus on how _Monte Carlo_ should be defined. For example, Ripley[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-Ripley-56) defines most probabilistic modeling as _[stochastic simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_simulation "Stochastic simulation")_ , with _Monte Carlo_ being reserved for [Monte Carlo integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_integration "Monte Carlo integration") and Monte Carlo statistical tests. [Sawilowsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shlomo_Sawilowsky "Shlomo Sawilowsky")[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-Sawilowsky-57) distinguishes between a [simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation "Simulation"), a Monte Carlo method, and a Monte Carlo simulation: a simulation is a fictitious representation of reality, a Monte Carlo method is a technique that can be used to solve a mathematical or statistical problem. A Monte Carlo simulation uses repeated sampling to obtain the statistical properties of some phenomena. Here are some examples: 
  * Simulation: Drawing _one_ pseudo-random uniform variable from the interval [0,1] can be used to simulate the tossing of a coin: If the value is less than or equal to 0.50 designate the outcome as heads, but if the value is greater than 0.50 designate the outcome as tails. This is a simulation, but not a Monte Carlo simulation.
  * Monte Carlo method: Pouring out a box of coins on a table, and then computing the ratio of coins that land heads versus tails is a Monte Carlo method of determining the behavior of repeated coin tosses, but it is not a simulation.
  * Monte Carlo simulation: Drawing _a large number_ of pseudo-random uniform variables from the interval [0,1] at one time, or once at many different times, and assigning values less than or equal to 0.50 as heads and greater than 0.50 as tails, is a _Monte Carlo simulation_ of the behavior of repeatedly tossing a coin.


Kalos and Whitlock[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-Kalos-58) point out that such distinctions are not always easy to maintain. For example, the emission of radiation from atoms is a natural stochastic process. It can be simulated directly, or its average behavior can be described by stochastic equations that can themselves be solved using Monte Carlo methods. "Indeed, the same computer code can be viewed simultaneously as a 'natural simulation' or as a solution of the equations by natural sampling." Convergence of the Monte Carlo simulation can be checked with the [Gelman-Rubin statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelman-Rubin_statistic "Gelman-Rubin statistic").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
### Monte Carlo and random numbers
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Monte Carlo and random numbers")]
The main idea behind this method is that the results are computed based on repeated random sampling and statistical analysis. The Monte Carlo simulation is, in fact, random experimentations, in the case that, the results of these experiments are not well known. Monte Carlo simulations are typically characterized by many unknown parameters, many of which are difficult to obtain experimentally.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-usaus-59) Monte Carlo simulation methods do not always require [truly random numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_number_generation#True_vs._pseudo-random_numbers "Random number generation") to be useful (although, for some applications such as [primality testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality_testing "Primality testing"), unpredictability is vital).[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-60) Many of the most useful techniques use deterministic, [pseudorandom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom_number_generator "Pseudorandom number generator") sequences, making it easy to test and re-run simulations. The only quality usually necessary to make good [simulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation "Simulation") is for the pseudo-random sequence to appear "random enough" in a certain sense.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
What this means depends on the application, but typically they should pass a series of statistical tests. Testing that the numbers are [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_\(continuous\) "Uniform distribution \(continuous\)") or follow another desired distribution when a large enough number of elements of the sequence are considered is one of the simplest and most common ones. Weak correlations between successive samples are also often desirable/necessary.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Sawilowsky lists the characteristics of a high-quality Monte Carlo simulation:[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-Sawilowsky-57)
  * the (pseudo-random) number generator has certain characteristics (e.g. a long "period" before the sequence repeats)
  * the (pseudo-random) number generator produces values that pass tests for randomness
  * there are enough samples to ensure accurate results
  * the proper sampling technique is used
  * the algorithm used is valid for what is being modeled
  * it simulates the phenomenon in question.


[Pseudo-random number sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-random_number_sampling "Pseudo-random number sampling") algorithms are used to transform uniformly distributed pseudo-random numbers into numbers that are distributed according to a given [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution"). [Low-discrepancy sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-discrepancy_sequences "Low-discrepancy sequences") are often used instead of random sampling from a space as they ensure even coverage and normally have a faster order of convergence than Monte Carlo simulations using random or pseudorandom sequences. Methods based on their use are called [quasi-Monte Carlo methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-Monte_Carlo_method "Quasi-Monte Carlo method").[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
In an effort to assess the impact of random number quality on Monte Carlo simulation outcomes, astrophysical researchers tested cryptographically secure pseudorandom numbers generated via Intel's [RDRAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RDRAND "RDRAND") instruction set, as compared to those derived from algorithms, like the [Mersenne Twister](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_Twister "Mersenne Twister"), in Monte Carlo simulations of radio flares from [brown dwarfs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brown_dwarfs "Brown dwarfs"). No statistically significant difference was found between models generated with typical pseudorandom number generators and RDRAND for trials consisting of the generation of 107 random numbers.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-61)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Monte Carlo simulation versus "what if" scenarios")]
There are ways of using probabilities that are definitely not Monte Carlo simulations – for example, deterministic modeling using single-point estimates. Each uncertain variable within a model is assigned a "best guess" estimate. Scenarios (such as best, worst, or most likely case) for each input variable are chosen and the results recorded.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVose200813-62)
By contrast, Monte Carlo simulations sample from a [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") for each variable to produce hundreds or thousands of possible outcomes. The results are analyzed to get probabilities of different outcomes occurring.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEVose200816-63) For example, a comparison of a spreadsheet cost construction model run using traditional "what if" scenarios, and then running the comparison again with Monte Carlo simulation and [triangular probability distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_distribution "Triangular distribution") shows that the Monte Carlo analysis has a narrower range than the "what if" analysis.[_[example needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:AUDIENCE "Wikipedia:AUDIENCE")_] This is because the "what if" analysis gives equal weight to all scenarios (see [quantifying uncertainty in corporate finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_finance#Quantifying_uncertainty "Corporate finance")), while the Monte Carlo method hardly samples in the very low probability regions. The samples in such regions are called "rare events".[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]

Monte Carlo methods are very important in [computational physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_physics "Computational physics"), [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry "Physical chemistry"), and related applied fields, and have diverse applications from complicated [quantum chromodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chromodynamics "Quantum chromodynamics") calculations to designing [heat shields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_shield "Heat shield") and [aerodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics "Aerodynamics") forms as well as in modeling radiation transport for radiation dosimetry calculations.[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-64)[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-65)[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-66)

### Engineering
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Engineering")]
Monte Carlo methods are widely used in engineering for [sensitivity analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensitivity_analysis "Sensitivity analysis") and quantitative [probabilistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic "Probabilistic") analysis in [process design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_design_\(chemical_engineering\) "Process design \(chemical engineering\)"). The need arises from the interactive, co-linear and non-linear behavior of typical process simulations. For example: 
  * In [microelectronics engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microelectronics "Microelectronics"), Monte Carlo methods are applied to analyze correlated and uncorrelated variations in [analog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_signal "Analog signal") and [digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_data "Digital data") [integrated circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuits "Integrated circuits").
  * In [geostatistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostatistics "Geostatistics") and [geometallurgy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometallurgy "Geometallurgy"), Monte Carlo methods underpin the design of [mineral processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral_processing "Mineral processing") [flowsheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_flow_diagram "Process flow diagram") and contribute to [quantitative risk analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_risk_analysis "Quantitative risk analysis").[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-FOOTNOTEMazhdrakovBenovValkanov2018250-22)
  * In [fluid dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_dynamics "Fluid dynamics"), in particular [rarefied gas dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_dynamics "Gas dynamics"), where the Boltzmann equation is solved for finite [Knudsen number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knudsen_number "Knudsen number") fluid flows using the [direct simulation Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_simulation_Monte_Carlo "Direct simulation Monte Carlo")[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-71) method in combination with highly efficient computational algorithms.[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-72)
  * In [autonomous robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_robotics "Autonomous robotics"), [Monte Carlo localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_localization "Monte Carlo localization") can determine the position of a robot. It is often applied to stochastic filters such as the [Kalman filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalman_filter "Kalman filter") or [particle filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_filter "Particle filter") that forms the heart of the [SLAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_localization_and_mapping "Simultaneous localization and mapping") (simultaneous localization and mapping) algorithm.
  * In [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications "Telecommunications"), when planning a wireless network, the design must be proven to work for a wide variety of scenarios that depend mainly on the number of users, their locations and the services they want to use. Monte Carlo methods are typically used to generate these users and their states. The network performance is then evaluated and, if results are not satisfactory, the network design goes through an optimization process.
  * In [reliability engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reliability_engineering "Reliability engineering"), Monte Carlo simulation is used to compute system-level response given the component-level response.
  * In [signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_processing "Signal processing") and [Bayesian inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference "Bayesian inference"), [particle filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_filter "Particle filter") and [sequential Monte Carlo techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_Monte_Carlo_method "Sequential Monte Carlo method") are a class of [mean-field particle methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean-field_particle_methods "Mean-field particle methods") for sampling and computing the posterior distribution of a signal process given some noisy and partial observations using interacting [empirical measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_measure "Empirical measure").[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-73)

### Computer graphics

[Path tracing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_tracing "Path tracing"), occasionally referred to as Monte Carlo ray tracing, renders a 3D scene by randomly tracing samples of possible light paths. ### Applied statistics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Applied statistics")]
The standards for Monte Carlo experiments in statistics were set by Sawilowsky.[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-77) In applied statistics, Monte Carlo methods may be used for at least four purposes: 
  1. To compare competing statistics for small samples under realistic data conditions. Although [type I error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_error "Type I error") and power properties of statistics can be calculated for data drawn from classical theoretical distributions (_e.g._ , [normal curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_curve "Normal curve"), [Cauchy distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_distribution "Cauchy distribution")) for [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic "Asymptotic") conditions (_i. e_ , infinite sample size and infinitesimally small treatment effect), real data often do not have such distributions.[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-78)
  2. To provide implementations of [hypothesis tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing "Statistical hypothesis testing") that are more efficient than exact tests such as [permutation tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_tests "Permutation tests") (which are often impossible to compute) while being more accurate than critical values for [asymptotic distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_distribution "Asymptotic distribution").
  3. To provide a random sample from the posterior distribution in [Bayesian inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference "Bayesian inference"). This sample then approximates and summarizes all the essential features of the posterior.
  4. To provide efficient random estimates of the Hessian matrix of the negative log-likelihood function that may be averaged to form an estimate of the [Fisher information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher_information "Fisher information") matrix.[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-79)[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-80)


Monte Carlo methods are also a compromise between approximate randomization and permutation tests. An approximate [randomization test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomization_test "Randomization test") is based on a specified subset of all permutations (which entails potentially enormous housekeeping of which permutations have been considered). The Monte Carlo approach is based on a specified number of randomly drawn permutations (exchanging a minor loss in precision if a permutation is drawn twice—or more frequently—for the efficiency of not having to track which permutations have already been selected). 
### Artificial intelligence for games
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Artificial intelligence for games")]
Main article: [Monte Carlo tree search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_tree_search "Monte Carlo tree search")
Monte Carlo methods have been developed into a technique called [Monte-Carlo tree search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte-Carlo_tree_search "Monte-Carlo tree search") that is useful for searching for the best move in a game. Possible moves are organized in a [search tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_tree "Search tree") and many random simulations are used to estimate the long-term potential of each move. A black box simulator represents the opponent's moves.[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-81) The Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) method has four steps:[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-82)
  1. Starting at root node of the tree, select optimal child nodes until a leaf node is reached.
  2. Expand the leaf node and choose one of its children.
  3. Play a simulated game starting with that node.
  4. Use the results of that simulated game to update the node and its ancestors.


The net effect, over the course of many simulated games, is that the value of a node representing a move will go up or down, hopefully corresponding to whether or not that node represents a good move. Monte Carlo Tree Search has been used successfully to play games such as [Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_\(game\) "Go \(game\)"),[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-83) [Tantrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tantrix "Tantrix"),[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-84) [Battleship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battleship_\(game\) "Battleship \(game\)"),[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-85) [Havannah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Havannah_\(board_game\) "Havannah \(board game\)"),[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-86) and [Arimaa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arimaa "Arimaa").[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-87)

### Search and rescue
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Search and rescue")]
The [US Coast Guard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Coast_Guard "US Coast Guard") utilizes Monte Carlo methods within its computer modeling software [SAROPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAROPS "SAROPS") in order to calculate the probable locations of vessels during [search and rescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Search_and_rescue "Search and rescue") operations. Each simulation can generate as many as ten thousand data points that are randomly distributed based upon provided variables.[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-89)
Search patterns are then generated based upon extrapolations of these data in order to optimize the probability of containment (POC) and the probability of detection (POD), which together will equal an overall probability of success (POS). Ultimately this serves as a practical application of [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") in order to provide the swiftest and most expedient method of rescue, saving both lives and resources.[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-90)
### Finance and business
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Finance and business")]
Further information: [Monte Carlo methods in finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_methods_in_finance "Monte Carlo methods in finance"), [Monte Carlo methods for option pricing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_methods_for_option_pricing "Monte Carlo methods for option pricing"), [Stochastic modelling (insurance)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_modelling_\(insurance\) "Stochastic modelling \(insurance\)"), [Stochastic asset model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_asset_model "Stochastic asset model"), and [Quasi-Monte Carlo methods in finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-Monte_Carlo_methods_in_finance "Quasi-Monte Carlo methods in finance")

## Mathematical applications
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=29 "Edit section: Mathematical applications")]
In general, the Monte Carlo methods are used in mathematics to solve various problems by generating suitable random numbers (see also [Random number generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_number_generation "Random number generation")) and observing that fraction of the numbers that obeys some property or properties. The method is useful for obtaining numerical solutions to problems too complicated to solve analytically. The most common application of the Monte Carlo method is Monte Carlo integration.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
### Integration
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=30 "Edit section: Integration")]
Main article: [Monte Carlo integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_integration "Monte Carlo integration")
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/46/Monte-carlo2.gif/250px-Monte-carlo2.gif)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Monte-carlo2.gif)Monte-Carlo integration works by comparing random points with the value of the function. [![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/Monte-Carlo_method_%28errors%29.png/250px-Monte-Carlo_method_%28errors%29.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Monte-Carlo_method_\(errors\).png)Errors reduce by a factor of  1 / N {\displaystyle \scriptstyle 1/{\sqrt {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\scriptstyle 1/{\\sqrt {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b0ed8ecb5f36fc5a2afacdde925d088e50ef38a3).
Deterministic [numerical integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_integration "Numerical integration") algorithms work well in a small number of dimensions, but encounter two problems when the functions have many variables. First, the number of function evaluations needed increases rapidly with the number of dimensions. For example, if 10 evaluations provide adequate accuracy in one dimension, then [10100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Googol "Googol") points are needed for 100 dimensions—far too many to be computed. This is called the [curse of dimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curse_of_dimensionality "Curse of dimensionality"). Second, the boundary of a multidimensional region may be very complicated, so it may not be feasible to reduce the problem to an [iterated integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterated_integral "Iterated integral").[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-Press-97) 100 [dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension "Dimension") is by no means unusual, since in many physical problems, a "dimension" is equivalent to a [degree of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_\(physics_and_chemistry\) "Degrees of freedom \(physics and chemistry\)"). 
Monte Carlo methods provide a way out of this exponential increase in computation time. As long as the function in question is reasonably [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved "Well-behaved"), it can be estimated by randomly selecting points in 100-dimensional space, and taking some kind of average of the function values at these points. By the [central limit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_limit_theorem "Central limit theorem"), this method displays  1 / N {\displaystyle \scriptstyle 1/{\sqrt {N}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\scriptstyle 1/{\\sqrt {N}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b0ed8ecb5f36fc5a2afacdde925d088e50ef38a3) convergence—i.e., quadrupling the number of sampled points halves the error, regardless of the number of dimensions.[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-Press-97)
A refinement of this method, known as [importance sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Importance_sampling "Importance sampling") in statistics, involves sampling the points randomly, but more frequently where the integrand is large. To do this precisely one would have to already know the integral, but one can approximate the integral by an integral of a similar function or use adaptive routines such as [stratified sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratified_sampling "Stratified sampling"), [recursive stratified sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_integration#Recursive_stratified_sampling "Monte Carlo integration"), adaptive umbrella sampling[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-98)[[99]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-99) or the [VEGAS algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VEGAS_algorithm "VEGAS algorithm"). 
A similar approach, the [quasi-Monte Carlo method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-Monte_Carlo_method "Quasi-Monte Carlo method"), uses [low-discrepancy sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-discrepancy_sequence "Low-discrepancy sequence"). These sequences "fill" the area better and sample the most important points more frequently, so quasi-Monte Carlo methods can often converge on the integral more quickly.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Another class of methods for sampling points in a volume is to simulate random walks over it ([Markov chain Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain_Monte_Carlo "Markov chain Monte Carlo")). Such methods include the [Metropolis–Hastings algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolis%E2%80%93Hastings_algorithm "Metropolis–Hastings algorithm"), [Gibbs sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gibbs_sampling "Gibbs sampling"), [Wang and Landau algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_and_Landau_algorithm "Wang and Landau algorithm"), and interacting type MCMC methodologies such as the [sequential Monte Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_filter "Particle filter") samplers.[[100]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-100)
### Simulation and optimization
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=31 "Edit section: Simulation and optimization")]
Main article: [Stochastic optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_optimization "Stochastic optimization")
Another powerful and very popular application for random numbers in numerical simulation is in [numerical optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_\(mathematics\) "Optimization \(mathematics\)"). The problem is to minimize (or maximize) functions of some vector that often has many dimensions. Many problems can be phrased in this way: for example, a [computer chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_chess "Computer chess") program could be seen as trying to find the set of, say, 10 moves that produces the best evaluation function at the end. In the [traveling salesman problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traveling_salesman_problem "Traveling salesman problem") the goal is to minimize distance traveled. There are also applications to engineering design, such as [multidisciplinary design optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multidisciplinary_design_optimization "Multidisciplinary design optimization"). It has been applied with quasi-one-dimensional models to solve particle dynamics problems by efficiently exploring large configuration space. Reference[[101]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-101) is a comprehensive review of many issues related to simulation and optimization. 
The [traveling salesman problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traveling_salesman_problem "Traveling salesman problem") is what is called a conventional optimization problem. That is, all the facts (distances between each destination point) needed to determine the optimal path to follow are known with certainty and the goal is to run through the possible travel choices to come up with the one with the lowest total distance. If instead of the goal being to minimize the total distance traveled to visit each desired destination but rather to minimize the total time needed to reach each destination, this goes beyond conventional optimization since travel time is inherently uncertain (traffic jams, time of day, etc.). As a result, to determine the optimal path a different simulation is required: optimization to first understand the range of potential times it could take to go from one point to another (represented by a probability distribution in this case rather than a specific distance) and then optimize the travel decisions to identify the best path to follow taking that uncertainty into account.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
### Inverse problems
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Monte_Carlo_method&action=edit&section=32 "Edit section: Inverse problems")]
Probabilistic formulation of [inverse problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_problem "Inverse problem") leads to the definition of a [probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution "Probability distribution") in the model space. This probability distribution combines [prior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_probability "Prior probability") information with new information obtained by measuring some observable parameters (data). As, in the general case, the theory linking data with model parameters is nonlinear, the posterior probability in the model space may not be easy to describe (it may be multimodal, some moments may not be defined, etc.).[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
When analyzing an inverse problem, obtaining a maximum likelihood model is usually not sufficient, as normally information on the resolution power of the data is desired. In the general case many parameters are modeled, and an inspection of the [marginal probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_probability "Marginal probability") densities of interest may be impractical, or even useless. But it is possible to pseudorandomly generate a large collection of models according to the [posterior probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_probability_distribution "Posterior probability distribution") and to analyze and display the models in such a way that information on the relative likelihoods of model properties is conveyed to the spectator. This can be accomplished by means of an efficient Monte Carlo method, even in cases where no explicit formula for the _a priori_ distribution is available.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
The best-known importance sampling method, the Metropolis algorithm, can be generalized, and this gives a method that allows analysis of (possibly highly nonlinear) inverse problems with complex _a priori_ information and data with an arbitrary noise distribution.[[102]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-102)[[103]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_method#cite_note-103)
