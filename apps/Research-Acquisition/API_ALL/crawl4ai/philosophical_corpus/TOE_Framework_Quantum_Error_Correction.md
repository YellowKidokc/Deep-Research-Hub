From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Process in quantum computing
**Quantum error correction** (**QEC**) comprises a set of techniques used in [quantum memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_memory "Quantum memory") and [quantum computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computing "Quantum computing") to protect [quantum information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_information "Quantum information") from errors arising from [decoherence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_decoherence "Quantum decoherence") and other sources of quantum noise. QEC schemes that employ codewords stabilized by a set of commuting operators are known as _[stabilizer codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stabilizer_code "Stabilizer code")_ , and the corresponding codewords are referred to as **quantum error-correcting codes** (QECCs). 
Conceptually, to use a quantum error-correcting code, one can append [ancilla qubits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancilla_\(quantum_computing\) "Ancilla \(quantum computing\)") to qubits that need protection, and apply a unitary encoding circuit to rotate the global state into a subspace of a larger [Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_space "Hilbert space"). This highly [entangled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_entanglement "Quantum entanglement"), encoded state corrects for local noisy errors. A quantum error-correcting code makes [quantum computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computation "Quantum computation") and [quantum communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_communication "Quantum communication") practical by providing a way for a sender and receiver to simulate a noiseless qubit channel given a [noisy qubit channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noisy_qubit_channel "Noisy qubit channel") whose noise conforms to a particular error model. 
## Overview
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Overview")]
Much of the terminology in QEC is derived from its classical counterpart, the _[classical error-correcting code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction_code "Error correction code")_. In classical coding theory, a code is commonly denoted by the notation  [ n , k , d ] {\displaystyle [n,k,d]} ![{\\displaystyle \[n,k,d\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ef7bd63229a2d74455e0037c5d70d39a13184a75), which represents the encoding of  k {\displaystyle k} ![{\\displaystyle k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3c9a2c7b599b37105512c5d570edc034056dd40) logical bits into  n {\displaystyle n} ![{\\displaystyle n}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a601995d55609f2d9f5e233e36fbe9ea26011b3b) physical bits with code distance  d {\displaystyle d} ![{\\displaystyle d}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e85ff03cbe0c7341af6b982e47e9f90d235c66ab); that is, any logical operation requires flipping at least d bits. Analogously, a quantum code that encodes k logical qubits into n physical qubits with code distance d is denoted by  [ [ n , k , d ] ] {\displaystyle [[n,k,d]]} ![{\\displaystyle \[\[n,k,d\]\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/da61d8ec97492d7a47a90f855e3d3bac99e6fd82). Although this qubit-to-qubit encoding is the most common setting, other variants exist—such as encodings between qubits and oscillators, or between oscillators themselves—since physical implementations of quantum information may involve systems with more than two energy levels. 
Based on the parameters  [ [ n , k , d ] ] {\displaystyle [[n,k,d]]} ![{\\displaystyle \[\[n,k,d\]\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/da61d8ec97492d7a47a90f855e3d3bac99e6fd82), one can define a key figure of merit for QECCs—the code rate, given by the ratio  k n {\displaystyle {\tfrac {k}{n}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\tfrac {k}{n}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/34a6eef68dca1a520343bc3cab055d6f348e6e2c). The code rate measures a code's efficiency: a higher value corresponds to lower resource overhead. It generally depends on the code distance d. An ideal QECC simultaneously achieves a large distance and a high code rate. Consequently, optimizing QECC designs to improve code rate while maintaining sufficient distance is a central objective in QEC, both theoretically and experimentally. Conversely, for cases where  k {\displaystyle k} ![{\\displaystyle k}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c3c9a2c7b599b37105512c5d570edc034056dd40) and  d {\displaystyle d} ![{\\displaystyle d}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e85ff03cbe0c7341af6b982e47e9f90d235c66ab) are fixed (often small), increasing the code rate reduces resource requirements, making such codes particularly suitable for small-scale or resource-limited experimental implementations. 
Before considering scenario-dependent objectives, a QEC scheme fundamentally consists of three stages: 
  1. Encoding the logical information into physical carriers,
  2. Transmitting or storing the encoded information through a spatial or temporal channel (corresponding to communication or memory, respectively), and
  3. Syndrome extraction and recovery (decoding) to identify and correct errors.


A QECC is constructed under specific assumptions about the types of errors that may occur and must be capable of correcting them. The stabilizers to be measured are carefully chosen so as not to reveal any logical information, but only information about the errors themselves—as otherwise the measurement would destroy any [quantum superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_superposition "Quantum superposition") of this logical qubit with other qubits in the quantum computer, which would prevent it from being used to convey quantum information. In most QECCs, the type of error is either a bit flip, or a phase flip, or both (corresponding to the [Pauli matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_matrices "Pauli matrices") X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab),  Y {\displaystyle Y} ![{\\displaystyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/961d67d6b454b4df2301ac571808a3538b3a6d3f), and  Z {\displaystyle Z} ![{\\displaystyle Z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1cc6b75e09a8aa3f04d8584b11db534f88fb56bd)). 
Various strategies exist for encoding and decoding, including classical algorithms that map measured error syndromes to their corresponding recovery operations. The sequence of applied quantum gates can also be optimized, as multi-qubit gates are generally more challenging to implement than single-qubit ones. Furthermore, the total number of possible syndromes is  2 n − k {\displaystyle 2^{n-k}} ![{\\displaystyle 2^{n-k}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ac056f26711f5e3b771cda221a0d99977f7bc4ef), which can be prohibitively large for a simple lookup-table approach. Consequently, efficient classical decoding algorithms are generally required, except in cases where the code structure is sufficiently simple.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-:0-1)
Compared with quantum memory, where channel-induced errors are the primary concern, the frequent application of quantum gates in quantum computation necessitates [fault-tolerant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_tolerance "Fault tolerance") design. For QECCs implemented on qubit-based platforms, fault tolerance additionally accounts for imperfect quantum gates, faulty state preparation, and measurement errors. In contrast, for QECCs that encode information into oscillators, the term _fault tolerance_ is sometimes used interchangeably with ordinary quantum error correction and does not carry additional meaning.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-2)
### Types of errors
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Types of errors")]
The types of errors that occur in a quantum system depend strongly on the underlying physical platform, rather than on device-independent assumptions. For instance, even when a qubit is under active control, it remains coupled to its environment through nonzero [Einstein coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_coefficients "Einstein coefficients"). When the environment is cooled to its vacuum state, this coupling gives rise to [amplitude-damping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude_damping_channel "Amplitude damping channel") errors (or excitation loss), which reflect the system's tendency to relax toward thermal equilibrium and are characterized by a [relaxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relaxation_\(physics\) "Relaxation \(physics\)") time. Moreover, even an isolated qubit possesses an intrinsic [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_\(quantum_mechanics\) "Hamiltonian \(quantum mechanics\)") corresponding to its internal dynamics, leading to coherent errors. Together, amplitude damping and coherent evolution contribute to [dephasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dephasing "Dephasing"), one of the dominant noise processes in most qubit implementations. 
As noted earlier, most QECCs assume that the dominant errors are bit flips, phase flips, or combinations of both—corresponding to the Pauli operators. An implicit assumption in this framework is that general physical errors can be approximated as elements of the [Pauli group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_group "Pauli group"). Under this model, each qubit's error can be represented by two classical bits (00: no error, 01:  Z {\displaystyle Z} ![{\\displaystyle Z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1cc6b75e09a8aa3f04d8584b11db534f88fb56bd), 10:  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab), 11:  Y {\displaystyle Y} ![{\\displaystyle Y}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/961d67d6b454b4df2301ac571808a3538b3a6d3f)). Consequently, errors on an n-qubit system can be described by a binary string of length 2n, allowing classical error-correction techniques to be applied under suitable constraints. Although this approximation does not capture all realistic noise processes, it remains widely used because it greatly simplifies both theoretical analysis and code design. 
### More general QEC schemes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: More general QEC schemes")]
The  [ [ n , k , d ] ] {\displaystyle [[n,k,d]]} ![{\\displaystyle \[\[n,k,d\]\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/da61d8ec97492d7a47a90f855e3d3bac99e6fd82) QECCs do not encompass all possible quantum codes. These belong to the class of additive codes, defined within the stabilizer formalism. A more general class, known as non-additive codes,[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-3) extends beyond this framework. For instance, the  ( ( 5 , 6 , 2 ) ) {\displaystyle ((5,6,2))} ![{\\displaystyle \(\(5,6,2\)\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ceeae82eba6664e416b92991e5dfd8530b569be9) code[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-4) encodes more than two qubits  ( log 2 ⁡ 6 ≈ 2.585 ) {\displaystyle (\log _{2}6\approx 2.585)} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\log _{2}6\\approx 2.585\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b6c039fc8ea01461fc93853c4775be555155750e) into five physical qubits with code distance two. Non-additive codes can, in principle, achieve higher code rates than additive ones, but their construction and analysis are considerably more challenging. As a result, they remain relatively unexplored, with only limited studies to date. 
Beyond encoding qubits into qubits, quantum information can also be stored in more general physical systems, such as  d {\displaystyle d} ![{\\displaystyle d}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e85ff03cbe0c7341af6b982e47e9f90d235c66ab)-level systems (qudits) or infinite-dimensional oscillators. Encoding a smaller logical system into a larger physical Hilbert space is an active area of research.

The first QECC, named after [Peter Shor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Shor "Peter Shor"), can be generalized as a  [ [ d 2 , 1 , d ] ] {\displaystyle [[d^{2},1,d]]} ![{\\displaystyle \[\[d^{2},1,d\]\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/96389582cdddff8bf8435c73a5fd9264893a64ca) code, which increases the code distance at the expense of a reduced code rate. Its design philosophy employs inner and outer  [ d , 1 , d ] {\displaystyle [d,1,d]} ![{\\displaystyle \[d,1,d\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/512552f6a04068f7aa93ac9073b30eda21e299fb) [repetition codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetition_code "Repetition code") to independently correct bit-flip and phase-flip errors. In contrast, [Andrew Steane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Steane "Andrew Steane") improved the code rate by replacing repetition codes with the classical  [ 7 , 4 ] {\displaystyle [7,4]} ![{\\displaystyle \[7,4\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d9376b8ac7c0edcf16a80b4602b3ec1cb73ec78f) [Hamming code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_code "Hamming code") and treating bit-flip and phase-flip errors symmetrically, without distinguishing inner and outer layers. The approach of Steane can be generalized as  [ [ 2 r − 1 , 2 r − 1 − 2 r , 3 ] ] {\displaystyle [[2^{r}-1,2^{r}-1-2r,3]]} ![{\\displaystyle \[\[2^{r}-1,2^{r}-1-2r,3\]\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fe00403028ec1dd96e3b8463f0adc09bfefb8b16) quantum Hamming codes.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-10) A generalization of these approaches led to the development of the [CSS codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS_code "CSS code")—named after [Robert Calderbank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Calderbank "Robert Calderbank"), [Peter Shor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Shor "Peter Shor"), and Andrew Steane. The structure of CSS codes is particularly well-suited for [fault-tolerant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault_tolerance "Fault tolerance") syndrome measurement, as the  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) and  Z {\displaystyle Z} ![{\\displaystyle Z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1cc6b75e09a8aa3f04d8584b11db534f88fb56bd) stabilizers are cleanly separated. 
While the Shor code emphasizes code distance and the Steane code emphasizes code rate, other CSS codes can be constructed to balance these parameters. For example, using overlapped-repetition codes[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-11)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-:3-12)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-:0-1) enables CSS codes with improved performance and the  [ [ 7 , 1 , 3 ] ] {\displaystyle [[7,1,3]]} ![{\\displaystyle \[\[7,1,3\]\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/77ca97a28919d5c04a4ee9b5e77cbbb8190718f8) Shor-type code is shown. Furthermore, this Shor-type code can be modified as subsystem codes such as the [Bacon–Shor code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacon%E2%80%93Shor_code "Bacon–Shor code")[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-13) which might optimize the syndrome measurement. 
The [quantum threshold theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threshold_theorem "Threshold theorem"), shows that quantum computations of arbitrary length are possible. It states that errors can be corrected by recursively concatenating quantum codes—such as CSS codes—across logarithmically many levels, provided the error rate of individual [quantum gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_logic_gate "Quantum logic gate") remains below a certain threshold. Above this threshold, attempts to measure syndromes and correct errors would introduce more errors than they eliminate.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-DiVincenzo_3260%E2%80%933263-14) As of 2004, estimates suggest this threshold could be as high as 1–3%,[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-15) assuming a sufficiently large number of qubits is available. To achieve a higher code rate for encoding a single logical qubit with single-error correction, [Raymond Laflamme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_Laflamme "Raymond Laflamme") et al. discovered a five-qubit code using four stabilizers that mix  X {\displaystyle X} ![{\\displaystyle X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/68baa052181f707c662844a465bfeeb135e82bab) and  Z {\displaystyle Z} ![{\\displaystyle Z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1cc6b75e09a8aa3f04d8584b11db534f88fb56bd) operators. A well-known variant employs four cyclic  X Z Z X I {\displaystyle XZZXI} ![{\\displaystyle XZZXI}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/662ec08b71a2828e1aa45dcba93cbe7df0e6274e) stabilizers. Although this code is clearly not a CSS code, DiVincenzo and Shor demonstrated that it can still be made fault-tolerant.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-DiVincenzo_3260%E2%80%933263-14) The five-qubit code is the smallest possible code capable of protecting one logical qubit against arbitrary single-qubit errors. According to the **quantum Hamming bound** , encoding a single logical qubit with the ability to correct any single-qubit error requires at least five physical qubits. 
Beyond coding-theoretic designs, topological QECCs are particularly intuitive to visualize and can provide a clear layout of local stabilizer measurements, which is experimentally friendly. [Alexei Kitaev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Kitaev "Alexei Kitaev") introduced the toric code without boundaries, which was later adapted into the surface code with boundaries, yielding a 2D planar layout that avoids non-local measurements.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-16) Surface codes are pivotal for scalable quantum error correction in 2025, enabling below-threshold logical qubits with improved fidelity in superconducting systems.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-17)

While the cat code and GKP codes are purely bosonic with no direct qubit correspondence, (extended) binomial codes[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-:6-23) are closely related to (high-rate) Shor codes.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-:3-12) The underlying idea is to treat the grouped qubits in each inner repetition code as identical particles, mapping them to a single bosonic mode in the Fock basis, thereby linking qubit codes to bosonic codes.

## Classical codes as bias quantum code
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Classical codes as bias quantum code")]
Classical [error-correcting codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_correction_code "Error correction code") that employ redundancy can be mapped to **biased quantum codes** that correct either Pauli X (bit-flip) or Pauli Z (phase-flip) errors. The simplest, though inefficient, example is the [repetition code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetition_code "Repetition code"). In a repetition code, the logical information is stored as multiple copies of a bit. If these copies are later found to disagree due to errors, the most likely original value is inferred via majority vote. 
For example, consider a logical bit in the "1" state copied three times. If noise corrupts one of the three bits, leaving the other two unchanged, the most likely scenario is that a single-bit error occurred, and the original logical value was "1." While it is possible that two bits flip, producing three zeros, this outcome is less probable. In this example, the logical information is the single bit, and the three copies are the physical representation. 
Repetition codes work in classical channels because classical bits can be freely measured and duplicated. In quantum channels, however, the [no-cloning theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-cloning_theorem "No-cloning theorem") prevents copying an unknown qubit, seemingly posing an obstacle to quantum error correction. This challenge is overcome by encoding the logical information of a single qubit into a highly entangled state of multiple physical qubits. For example, the **three-qubit bit-flip code** , first proposed by [Asher Peres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asher_Peres "Asher Peres") in 1985,[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-53) uses entanglement and syndrome measurements to correct errors in a manner analogous to the classical repetition code. A **phase-flip code** is similarly constructed and is equivalent to the bit-flip code up to transversal [Hadamard gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard_transform "Hadamard transform").

Let us assume for simplicity that  p {\displaystyle p} ![{\\displaystyle p}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/81eac1e205430d1f40810df36a0edffdc367af36) is small enough that the probability of more than a single qubit being flipped is negligible. One can then detect whether a qubit was flipped, without also querying for the values being transmitted, by asking whether one of the qubits differs from the others. This amounts to performing a measurement with four different outcomes, corresponding to the following four projective measurements: P 0 = | 000 ⟩ ⟨ 000 | + | 111 ⟩ ⟨ 111 | , P 1 = | 100 ⟩ ⟨ 100 | + | 011 ⟩ ⟨ 011 | , P 2 = | 010 ⟩ ⟨ 010 | + | 101 ⟩ ⟨ 101 | , P 3 = | 001 ⟩ ⟨ 001 | + | 110 ⟩ ⟨ 110 | . {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}P_{0}&=|000\rangle \langle 000|+|111\rangle \langle 111|,\\\P_{1}&=|100\rangle \langle 100|+|011\rangle \langle 011|,\\\P_{2}&=|010\rangle \langle 010|+|101\rangle \langle 101|,\\\P_{3}&=|001\rangle \langle 001|+|110\rangle \langle 110|.\end{aligned}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\begin{aligned}P_{0}&=|000\\rangle \\langle 000|+|111\\rangle \\langle 111|,\\\\P_{1}&=|100\\rangle \\langle 100|+|011\\rangle \\langle 011|,\\\\P_{2}&=|010\\rangle \\langle 010|+|101\\rangle \\langle 101|,\\\\P_{3}&=|001\\rangle \\langle 001|+|110\\rangle \\langle 110|.\\end{aligned}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cdf7061a4a47e89cb79acfaae6ad1d8615e02c45)This reveals which qubits are different from the others, without at the same time giving information about the state of the qubits themselves. If the outcome corresponding to  P 0 {\displaystyle P_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle P_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/671bd891701e0d6cfa6da0114a5dd64233b58709) is obtained, no correction is applied, while if the outcome corresponding to  P i {\displaystyle P_{i}} ![{\\displaystyle P_{i}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3ba1396129f7be3c7f828a571b6649e6807d10d3) is observed, then the Pauli _X_ gate is applied to the  i {\displaystyle i} ![{\\displaystyle i}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/add78d8608ad86e54951b8c8bd6c8d8416533d20)-th qubit. For example, if the first and second qubits are flipped, then the syndrome measurement gives the outcome  P 3 {\displaystyle P_{3}} ![{\\displaystyle P_{3}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f92db8e65bb75b79799f0f3a29e975b37e227069), and the third qubit is flipped, instead of the first two. It follows that  F ( ψ ′ ) = ⟨ ψ ′ | ρ out | ψ ′ ⟩ ≥ ( 1 − p ) 3 + 3 p ( 1 − p ) 2 = 1 − 3 p 2 + 2 p 3 . {\displaystyle F(\psi ')=\langle \psi '\vert \rho _{\operatorname {out} }\vert \psi '\rangle \geq (1-p)^{3}+3p(1-p)^{2}=1-3p^{2}+2p^{3}.} ![{\\displaystyle F\(\\psi '\)=\\langle \\psi '\\vert \\rho _{\\operatorname {out} }\\vert \\psi '\\rangle \\geq \(1-p\)^{3}+3p\(1-p\)^{2}=1-3p^{2}+2p^{3}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1ef74d8d045f63b77231b887b6cb79b748aca68d)This [fidelity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidelity_of_quantum_states "Fidelity of quantum states") is to be compared with the corresponding fidelity obtained when no error-correcting protocol is used, which was shown before to equal  1 − p {\displaystyle {1-p}} ![{\\displaystyle {1-p}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b56658784bca9b33b54b8a7a916ed4343052f6fa). ### Sign-flip code
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Sign-flip code")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/Quantum_error_correction_of_phase_flip_using_three_qubits.svg/500px-Quantum_error_correction_of_phase_flip_using_three_qubits.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Quantum_error_correction_of_phase_flip_using_three_qubits.svg)[Quantum circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_circuit "Quantum circuit") of the phase-flip code
The bit flip is the only kind of error in classical computers. In quantum computers, however, another kind of error is possible: the sign flip. Through transmission in a channel, the relative sign between  | 0 ⟩ {\displaystyle |0\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |0\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ed066a3ad158da0ad6d6a421a606b1c8a35eb95b) and  | 1 ⟩ {\displaystyle |1\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |1\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2f53021ca18e77477ee5bd3c1523e5830189ec5c) can become inverted. For instance, a qubit in the state  | − ⟩ = ( | 0 ⟩ − | 1 ⟩ ) / 2 {\displaystyle |-\rangle =(|0\rangle -|1\rangle )/{\sqrt {2}}} ![{\\displaystyle |-\\rangle =\(|0\\rangle -|1\\rangle \)/{\\sqrt {2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/95999a875fa2a9e995b84eae3687b4dcde7f5aa7) may have its sign flip to  | + ⟩ = ( | 0 ⟩ + | 1 ⟩ ) / 2 . {\displaystyle |+\rangle =(|0\rangle +|1\rangle )/{\sqrt {2}}.} ![{\\displaystyle |+\\rangle =\(|0\\rangle +|1\\rangle \)/{\\sqrt {2}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/511cac2b59a9acabb57f70f1a9b9ad75d09de917)
The original state of the qubit  | ψ ⟩ = α 0 | 0 ⟩ + α 1 | 1 ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle =\alpha _{0}|0\rangle +\alpha _{1}|1\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle =\\alpha _{0}|0\\rangle +\\alpha _{1}|1\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1fab168ae201055c5c8caeeb1b3b690278c6d53f) will be changed into the state  | ψ ′ ⟩ = α 0 | + + + ⟩ + α 1 | − − − ⟩ . {\displaystyle |\psi '\rangle =\alpha _{0}|{+}{+}{+}\rangle +\alpha _{1}|{-}{-}{-}\rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi '\\rangle =\\alpha _{0}|{+}{+}{+}\\rangle +\\alpha _{1}|{-}{-}{-}\\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fe251368030af639217ab273a77aeedd82259b14)
In the Hadamard basis, bit flips become sign flips and sign flips become bit flips. Let  E phase {\displaystyle E_{\text{phase}}} ![{\\displaystyle E_{\\text{phase}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0e93113f08ff9467b6eb6ad2fe7f373980fc1791) be a quantum channel that can cause at most one phase flip. Then the bit-flip code from above can recover  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) by transforming into the Hadamard basis before and after transmission through  E phase {\displaystyle E_{\text{phase}}} ![{\\displaystyle E_{\\text{phase}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0e93113f08ff9467b6eb6ad2fe7f373980fc1791). 
## Encoding logical qubits into physical qubits
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Encoding logical qubits into physical qubits")]
### Shor code
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Shor code")]
The error channel may induce either a bit flip, a sign flip (i.e., a phase flip), or both. It is possible to correct for both types of errors on a logical qubit using a well-designed QEC code. One example of a code that does this is the Shor code, published in 1995.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-Shor1995-55)[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-56): 10  Since these two types of errors are the only types of errors that can result after a projective measurement, a Shor code corrects arbitrary single-qubit errors. 
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a9/Shore_code.svg/500px-Shore_code.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Shore_code.svg)Quantum circuit to encode a single logical qubit with the Shor code and then perform bit flip error correction on each of the three blocks.
Let  E {\displaystyle E} ![{\\displaystyle E}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4232c9de2ee3eec0a9c0a19b15ab92daa6223f9b) be a [quantum channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_channel "Quantum channel") that can arbitrarily corrupt a single qubit. The 1st, 4th and 7th qubits are for the sign flip code, while the three groups of qubits (1,2,3), (4,5,6), and (7,8,9) are designed for the bit flip code. With the Shor code, a qubit state  | ψ ⟩ = α 0 | 0 ⟩ + α 1 | 1 ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle =\alpha _{0}|0\rangle +\alpha _{1}|1\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle =\\alpha _{0}|0\\rangle +\\alpha _{1}|1\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1fab168ae201055c5c8caeeb1b3b690278c6d53f) will be transformed into the product of 9 qubits  | ψ ′ ⟩ = α 0 | 0 S ⟩ + α 1 | 1 S ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi '\rangle =\alpha _{0}|0_{S}\rangle +\alpha _{1}|1_{S}\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi '\\rangle =\\alpha _{0}|0_{S}\\rangle +\\alpha _{1}|1_{S}\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2d9824cc5e6930c0e3fe22d9f930b2353bf8b3f8), where  | 0 S ⟩ = 1 2 2 ( | 000 ⟩ + | 111 ⟩ ) ⊗ ( | 000 ⟩ + | 111 ⟩ ) ⊗ ( | 000 ⟩ + | 111 ⟩ ) {\displaystyle |0_{\rm {S}}\rangle ={\frac {1}{2{\sqrt {2}}}}(|000\rangle +|111\rangle )\otimes (|000\rangle +|111\rangle )\otimes (|000\rangle +|111\rangle )} ![{\\displaystyle |0_{\\rm {S}}\\rangle ={\\frac {1}{2{\\sqrt {2}}}}\(|000\\rangle +|111\\rangle \)\\otimes \(|000\\rangle +|111\\rangle \)\\otimes \(|000\\rangle +|111\\rangle \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/07c4bf5fd01454464b14363bd90f6b5d183f21df) | 1 S ⟩ = 1 2 2 ( | 000 ⟩ − | 111 ⟩ ) ⊗ ( | 000 ⟩ − | 111 ⟩ ) ⊗ ( | 000 ⟩ − | 111 ⟩ ) {\displaystyle |1_{\rm {S}}\rangle ={\frac {1}{2{\sqrt {2}}}}(|000\rangle -|111\rangle )\otimes (|000\rangle -|111\rangle )\otimes (|000\rangle -|111\rangle )} ![{\\displaystyle |1_{\\rm {S}}\\rangle ={\\frac {1}{2{\\sqrt {2}}}}\(|000\\rangle -|111\\rangle \)\\otimes \(|000\\rangle -|111\\rangle \)\\otimes \(|000\\rangle -|111\\rangle \)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7fac056d301709682ed7516fcd1d9e2b8f4cf3ba)
If a bit flip error happens to a qubit, the syndrome analysis will be performed on each block of qubits (1,2,3), (4,5,6), and (7,8,9) to detect and correct at most one bit flip error in each block. 
If the three bit flip group (1,2,3), (4,5,6), and (7,8,9) are considered as three inputs, then the Shor code circuit can be reduced as a sign flip code. This means that the Shor code can also repair a sign flip error for a single qubit. 
The Shor code also can correct for any arbitrary errors (both bit flip and sign flip) to a single qubit. If an error is modeled by a unitary transform U, which will act on a qubit  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e), then  U {\displaystyle U} ![{\\displaystyle U}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/458a728f53b9a0274f059cd695e067c430956025) can be described in the form  U = c 0 I + c 1 X + c 2 Y + c 3 Z {\displaystyle U=c_{0}I+c_{1}X+c_{2}Y+c_{3}Z} ![{\\displaystyle U=c_{0}I+c_{1}X+c_{2}Y+c_{3}Z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9c4d28905789816799a8d5bb08ceb519be475ff7) where  c 0 {\displaystyle c_{0}} ![{\\displaystyle c_{0}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1882ba8f1dc60f0c68a642abb5af093c73910921), c 1 {\displaystyle c_{1}} ![{\\displaystyle c_{1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/77b7dc6d279091d354e0b90889b463bfa7eb7247), c 2 {\displaystyle c_{2}} ![{\\displaystyle c_{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0b30ba1b247fb8d334580cec68561e749d24aff2), and  c 3 {\displaystyle c_{3}} ![{\\displaystyle c_{3}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b1dc52bfbaf6e577fbed72a716068f4533700bd3) are complex constants, I is the identity, and the [Pauli matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_matrices "Pauli matrices") are given by  X = ( 0 1 1 0 ) ; Y = ( 0 − i i 0 ) ; Z = ( 1 0 0 − 1 ) . {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}X&={\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\\1&0\end{pmatrix}};\\\Y&={\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\\i&0\end{pmatrix}};\\\Z&={\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}}.\end{aligned}}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\begin{aligned}X&={\\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\\\1&0\\end{pmatrix}};\\\\Y&={\\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\\\i&0\\end{pmatrix}};\\\\Z&={\\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\\0&-1\\end{pmatrix}}.\\end{aligned}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/60e30cd6369c89f0bcd09331f0557f83db9dadcd)
If _U_ is equal to _I_ , then no error occurs. If  U = X {\displaystyle U=X} ![{\\displaystyle U=X}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cca72818979772cdb03198880a7ec32fffa57816), a bit flip error occurs. If  U = Z {\displaystyle U=Z} ![{\\displaystyle U=Z}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a9f2efd723a863e23f9e596901d4064e11e0c066), a sign flip error occurs. If  U = i Y {\displaystyle U=iY} ![{\\displaystyle U=iY}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6dd11139c6395323936605e9cfe3e91ae6716d5f) then both a bit flip error and a sign flip error occur. In other words, the Shor code can correct any combination of bit or phase errors on a single qubit. 
More generally, the error operator _U_ does not need to be unitary, but can be a Kraus operator from a [quantum operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_operation "Quantum operation") representing a system interacting with its environment. 
## Application

### In quantum metrology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_error_correction&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: In quantum metrology")]
Quantum error correction can be applied to [quantum metrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_metrology "Quantum metrology"). Thus, a logical qubit is stored in several physical qubits. In the case of a linear interferometer, there is not interaction between the logical qubits. However, the dynamics is given with operators that contain multiqubit correlation operators of the physical qubits corresponding to a logical qubits. In this scheme, the errors can be detected, and corrected following the general rules of quantum error correction.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-57)[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-58)
In another approach, the goal is not correcting the quantum state, but to keep a state that makes quantum metrology with high precision possible even if noise is present. It has been observed that some quantum states that cannot outperform separable states in quantum metrology, can be better than separable states in the multi-copy case, hence their metrological abilities can be activated.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-59) Thus, instead of stroring each logical qubit in several physical qubits, we store several copies of the entire quantum state. increases exponentially with the number of copies,  M {\displaystyle M} ![{\\displaystyle M}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f82cade9898ced02fdd08712e5f0c0151758a0dd), and approaches the metrological usefulness of the GHZ state,  4 N 2 . {\displaystyle 4N^{2}.} ![{\\displaystyle 4N^{2}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/87ed4cdbfa4124c1590e90e37d85767a64173d69) Separable states reach  4 N . {\displaystyle 4N.} ![{\\displaystyle 4N.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/df5f289d87b88bd13a922a9c29317df32f49ca32)[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_error_correction#cite_note-NJP2024-60)
If the state is outside of the subspace described above, then it can be brought back to the subspace with the usual steps of error correction with the bitflip code. 
In another example, one can see that in this scheme a phase error is suppressed even without error correction. Let us call the three copies of the  N {\displaystyle N} ![{\\displaystyle N}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f5e3890c981ae85503089652feb48b191b57aae3)-qubit GHZ state then a single phase 
| Ψ ⟩ = | G H Z N ⟩ ⊗ | G H Z N ⟩ ⊗ | G H Z N ⟩ , {\displaystyle |\Psi \rangle =|{\rm {GHZ}}_{N}\rangle \otimes |{\rm {GHZ}}_{N}\rangle \otimes |{\rm {GHZ}}_{N}\rangle ,} ![{\\displaystyle |\\Psi \\rangle =|{\\rm {GHZ}}_{N}\\rangle \\otimes |{\\rm {GHZ}}_{N}\\rangle \\otimes |{\\rm {GHZ}}_{N}\\rangle ,}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/3e845c479e7836b89521d75cad063f69f0e58579)
and consider the Hamiltonian above. Then, the metrological usefulness of the state is characterized by the quantum Fisher information  F Q [ | Ψ ⟩ ⟨ Ψ | , H ] {\displaystyle F_{Q}[|\Psi \rangle \langle \Psi |,H]} ![{\\displaystyle F_{Q}\[|\\Psi \\rangle \\langle \\Psi |,H\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/b95a267755e6d370e57a02f0824784efff1e8ce0). 
Let us denote the state after one of the qubits passes through a phase flip channel by  ϱ p h a s e f l i p . {\displaystyle \varrho _{\rm {phaseflip}}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\varrho _{\\rm {phaseflip}}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9374d42e1bc2a16b52f1fc8e9f4b2c23307a981c) It can be shown that the metrological usefulness of the state does not change 
F Q [ ϱ p h a s e f l i p , H ] = F Q [ | Ψ ⟩ ⟨ Ψ | , H ] {\displaystyle F_{Q}[\varrho _{\rm {phaseflip}},H]=F_{Q}[|\Psi \rangle \langle \Psi |,H]} ![{\\displaystyle F_{Q}\[\\varrho _{\\rm {phaseflip}},H\]=F_{Q}\[|\\Psi \\rangle \\langle \\Psi |,H\]}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/fda4a7236f7454d655939e596cba73ed536f35c6)
and it remains maximal. Thus, even without an error correction step, the metrological properties remain the same.
