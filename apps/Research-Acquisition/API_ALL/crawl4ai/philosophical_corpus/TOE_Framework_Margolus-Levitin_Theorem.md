From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
(Redirected from [Margolus–Levitin theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Margolus%E2%80%93Levitin_theorem&redirect=no "Margolus–Levitin theorem"))
Limitation on the minimum time for a quantum system to evolve between two states
In [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics"), a **quantum speed limit** (**QSL**) is a limitation on the minimum time for a quantum system to evolve between two distinguishable (orthogonal) states.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-DEF-1) QSL theorems are closely related to time-energy uncertainty relations. In 1945, [Leonid Mandelstam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Mandelstam "Leonid Mandelstam") and [Igor Tamm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igor_Tamm "Igor Tamm") derived a time-energy uncertainty relation that bounds the speed of evolution in terms of the energy dispersion.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-MTT-2) Over half a century later, [Norman Margolus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Margolus "Norman Margolus") and [Lev Levitin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lev_Levitin "Lev Levitin") showed that the speed of evolution cannot exceed the mean energy,[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-Margolus1998-3) a result known as the Margolus–Levitin theorem. Realistic physical systems in contact with an environment are known as [open quantum systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_quantum_system "Open quantum system") and their evolution is also subject to QSL.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-5) Quite remarkably it was shown that environmental effects, such as non-Markovian dynamics can speed up quantum processes,[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-6) which was verified in a cavity QED experiment.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-7)
QSL have been used to explore the [limits of computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limits_of_computation "Limits of computation")[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-8)[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-9) and complexity. In 2017, QSLs were studied in a quantum oscillator at high temperature.[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-10) In 2018, it was shown that QSL are not restricted to the quantum domain and that similar bounds hold in classical systems.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-11)[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-12) In 2021, both the Mandelstam-Tamm and the Margolus–Levitin QSL bounds were concurrently tested in a single experiment[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-13) which indicated there are "two different regimes: one where the Mandelstam-Tamm limit constrains the evolution at all times, and a second where a crossover to the Margolus-Levitin limit occurs at longer times." 
In [quantum sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_sensor "Quantum sensor"), QSLs impose fundamental constraints on the maximum achievable time resolution of quantum sensors. These limits stem from the requirement that quantum states must evolve to orthogonal states to extract precise information. For example, in applications like [Ramsey interferometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey_interferometry "Ramsey interferometry"), the QSL determines the minimum time required for phase accumulation during control sequences, directly impacting the sensor's temporal resolution and sensitivity.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-14)
## Preliminary definitions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_speed_limit&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Preliminary definitions")]
The speed limit theorems can be stated for [pure states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state "Pure state"), and for [mixed states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_quantum_state "Mixed quantum state"); they take a simpler form for pure states. An arbitrary pure state can be written as a [linear combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_superposition "Quantum superposition") of energy eigenstates:       | ψ ⟩ = ∑ n c n | E n ⟩ . {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle =\sum _{n}c_{n}|E_{n}\rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle =\\sum _{n}c_{n}|E_{n}\\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9876aee6166c6e18c54117a39b5945d612033abb)
The task is to provide a lower bound for the time interval  t ⊥ {\displaystyle t_{\perp }} ![{\\displaystyle t_{\\perp }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ce6787143fe271aecedc8715b1e52c3fcfd71974) required for the initial state  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e) to evolve into a state orthogonal to  | ψ ⟩ {\displaystyle |\psi \rangle } ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi \\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/cc27f1893b769a08cd6b296e115a29e61cab675e). The time evolution of a pure state is given by the [Schrödinger equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schr%C3%B6dinger_equation "Schrödinger equation"):       | ψ t ⟩ = ∑ n c n e i t E n / ℏ | E n ⟩ . {\displaystyle |\psi _{t}\rangle =\sum _{n}c_{n}e^{itE_{n}/\hbar }|E_{n}\rangle .} ![{\\displaystyle |\\psi _{t}\\rangle =\\sum _{n}c_{n}e^{itE_{n}/\\hbar }|E_{n}\\rangle .}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5bb456999f4cf216875d2349f5d093d5c29c5445)
Orthogonality is obtained when       ⟨ ψ 0 | ψ t ⟩ = 0 {\displaystyle \langle \psi _{0}|\psi _{t}\rangle =0} ![{\\displaystyle \\langle \\psi _{0}|\\psi _{t}\\rangle =0}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7d0eb94f9942ddc611ac5a2f216a51dd3e63a0e7)
and the minimum time interval  t = t ⊥ {\displaystyle t=t_{\perp }} ![{\\displaystyle t=t_{\\perp }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/9c0e31bfb770d239c0006320351bedba158fca3a) required to achieve this condition is called the orthogonalization interval[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-MTT-2) or orthogonalization time.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-LTT-15)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_speed_limit&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Mandelstam–Tamm limit")]
For pure states, the Mandelstam–Tamm theorem states that the minimum time  t ⊥ {\displaystyle t_{\perp }} ![{\\displaystyle t_{\\perp }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/ce6787143fe271aecedc8715b1e52c3fcfd71974) required for a state to evolve into an orthogonal state is bounded below:       t ⊥ ≥ π ℏ 2 δ E = h 4 δ E {\displaystyle t_{\perp }\geq {\frac {\pi \hbar }{2\,\delta E}}={\frac {h}{4\,\delta E}}} ![{\\displaystyle t_{\\perp }\\geq {\\frac {\\pi \\hbar }{2\\,\\delta E}}={\\frac {h}{4\\,\\delta E}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5802a643633e9cfe00f0544eee443b499317bf28),
where       ( δ E ) 2 = ⟨ ψ | H 2 | ψ ⟩ − ( ⟨ ψ | H | ψ ⟩ ) 2 = 1 2 ∑ n , m | c n | 2 | c m | 2 ( E n − E m ) 2 {\displaystyle (\delta E)^{2}=\left\langle \psi |H^{2}|\psi \right\rangle -(\left\langle \psi |H|\psi \right\rangle )^{2}={\frac {1}{2}}\sum _{n,m}|c_{n}|^{2}|c_{m}|^{2}(E_{n}-E_{m})^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle \(\\delta E\)^{2}=\\left\\langle \\psi |H^{2}|\\psi \\right\\rangle -\(\\left\\langle \\psi |H|\\psi \\right\\rangle \)^{2}={\\frac {1}{2}}\\sum _{n,m}|c_{n}|^{2}|c_{m}|^{2}\(E_{n}-E_{m}\)^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/a899a99ccdfde228f19dbf7ef68a3bee51cccc4b),
is the [variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variance "Variance") of the system's energy and  H {\displaystyle H} ![{\\displaystyle H}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/75a9edddcca2f782014371f75dca39d7e13a9c1b) is the [Hamiltonian operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_operator "Hamiltonian operator"). The quantum evolution is independent of the particular Hamiltonian used to transport the quantum system along a given curve in the [projective Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_Hilbert_space "Projective Hilbert space"); the distance along this curve is measured by the [Fubini–Study metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fubini%E2%80%93Study_metric "Fubini–Study metric").[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-GQE-16) This is sometimes called the [quantum angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_angle "Quantum angle"), as it can be understood as the arccos of the inner product of the initial and final states. 
### For mixed states
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_speed_limit&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: For mixed states")]
The Mandelstam–Tamm limit can also be stated for mixed states and for time-varying Hamiltonians. In this case, the [Bures metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bures_metric "Bures metric") must be employed in place of the Fubini–Study metric. A mixed state can be understood as a sum over pure states, weighted by [classical probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_measure "Probability measure"); likewise, the Bures metric is a weighted sum of the Fubini–Study metric. For a time-varying Hamiltonian  H t {\displaystyle H_{t}} ![{\\displaystyle H_{t}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5e33c7739bb90406c5b8dd53d3f9cb82b071ac2b) and time-varying [density matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_matrix "Density matrix") ρ t , {\displaystyle \rho _{t},} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho _{t},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e846e9072496c77e678abe163c8c10d2215a684b) the variance of the energy is given by       σ H 2 ( t ) = | tr ( ρ t H t 2 ) | − | tr ( ρ t H t ) | 2 {\displaystyle \sigma _{H}^{2}(t)=|{\text{tr}}(\rho _{t}H_{t}^{2})|-|{\text{tr}}(\rho _{t}H_{t})|^{2}} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{H}^{2}\(t\)=|{\\text{tr}}\(\\rho _{t}H_{t}^{2}\)|-|{\\text{tr}}\(\\rho _{t}H_{t}\)|^{2}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5e061545cde2614bbd90f451ff49cb7c412f63aa)
The Mandelstam–Tamm limit then takes the form       ∫ 0 τ σ H ( t ) d t ≥ ℏ D B ( ρ 0 , ρ τ ) {\displaystyle \int _{0}^{\tau }\sigma _{H}(t)dt\geq \hbar D_{B}(\rho _{0},\rho _{\tau })} ![{\\displaystyle \\int _{0}^{\\tau }\\sigma _{H}\(t\)dt\\geq \\hbar D_{B}\(\\rho _{0},\\rho _{\\tau }\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2e0dacd99b5707e1fee9251698ab351ad31b492b),
where  D B {\displaystyle D_{B}} ![{\\displaystyle D_{B}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/f4c98399752f15ad50d12f7f05417da4869f6d36) is the [Bures distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bures_distance "Bures distance") between the starting and ending states. The Bures distance is [geodesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic "Geodesic"), giving the shortest possible distance of any continuous curve connecting two points, with  σ H ( t ) {\displaystyle \sigma _{H}(t)} ![{\\displaystyle \\sigma _{H}\(t\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/6b33ecde42aba0377c58d8af19bc96e67b2e2ae7) understood as an infinitessimal path length along a curve parametrized by  t . {\displaystyle t.} ![{\\displaystyle t.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/d3e6cc375ac6123d2342be53eba87b92fbbacf07) Equivalently, the time  τ {\displaystyle \tau } ![{\\displaystyle \\tau }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/38a7dcde9730ef0853809fefc18d88771f95206c) taken to evolve from  ρ {\displaystyle \rho } ![{\\displaystyle \\rho }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f7d439671d1289b6a816e6af7a304be40608d64) to  ρ ′ {\displaystyle \rho '} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho '}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e2400eb83af71f78fcb442e55bf294cebdfb9803) is bounded as       τ ≥ ℏ σ ¯ H D B ( ρ , ρ ′ ) {\displaystyle \tau \geq {\frac {\hbar }{{\overline {\sigma }}_{H}}}D_{B}(\rho ,\rho ')} ![{\\displaystyle \\tau \\geq {\\frac {\\hbar }{{\\overline {\\sigma }}_{H}}}D_{B}\(\\rho ,\\rho '\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/7df15f274181d25d9a894a3553f4197cfe5f6002)
where       σ ¯ H = 1 τ ∫ 0 τ σ H ( t ) d t {\displaystyle {\overline {\sigma }}_{H}={\frac {1}{\tau }}\int _{0}^{\tau }\sigma _{H}(t)dt} ![{\\displaystyle {\\overline {\\sigma }}_{H}={\\frac {1}{\\tau }}\\int _{0}^{\\tau }\\sigma _{H}\(t\)dt}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/e802b8dc7db9395b3780e965a745b4ed2488ca16)
is the time-averaged uncertainty in energy. For a pure state evolving under a time-varying Hamiltonian, the time  τ {\displaystyle \tau } ![{\\displaystyle \\tau }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/38a7dcde9730ef0853809fefc18d88771f95206c) taken to evolve from one pure state to another pure state orthogonal to it is bounded as[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-Deffner2013-17)      τ ≥ ℏ σ ¯ H π 2 {\displaystyle \tau \geq {\frac {\hbar }{{\overline {\sigma }}_{H}}}{\frac {\pi }{2}}} ![{\\displaystyle \\tau \\geq {\\frac {\\hbar }{{\\overline {\\sigma }}_{H}}}{\\frac {\\pi }{2}}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/216d06250a5a57d279192461fa373bb4ffa3a2e0)
This follows, as for a pure state, one has the density matrix  ρ t = | ψ t ⟩ ⟨ ψ t | . {\displaystyle \rho _{t}=|\psi _{t}\rangle \langle \psi _{t}|.} ![{\\displaystyle \\rho _{t}=|\\psi _{t}\\rangle \\langle \\psi _{t}|.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/0e9bf703298d4da27edc5192d1923bc796686897) The quantum angle (Fubini–Study distance) is then  D B ( ρ 0 , ρ t ) = arccos ⁡ | ⟨ ψ 0 | ψ t ⟩ | {\displaystyle D_{B}(\rho _{0},\rho _{t})=\arccos |\langle \psi _{0}|\psi _{t}\rangle |} ![{\\displaystyle D_{B}\(\\rho _{0},\\rho _{t}\)=\\arccos |\\langle \\psi _{0}|\\psi _{t}\\rangle |}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/644f0003767802735cb063c9b17cfa5c343bdf6c) and so one concludes  D B = arccos ⁡ 0 = π / 2 {\displaystyle D_{B}=\arccos 0=\pi /2} ![{\\displaystyle D_{B}=\\arccos 0=\\pi /2}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/66d710da4e1a4f05ca0e6275f02d83de1cf7891e) when the initial and final states are orthogonal. 
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_speed_limit&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Margolus–Levitin limit")]
For the case of a pure state, Margolus and Levitin[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-Margolus1998-3) obtain a different limit, that       τ ⊥ ≥ h 4 ⟨ E ⟩ , {\displaystyle \tau _{\perp }\geq {\frac {h}{4\langle E\rangle }},} ![{\\displaystyle \\tau _{\\perp }\\geq {\\frac {h}{4\\langle E\\rangle }},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/4f30e2746f1e53497b6603f4c564729048ca398e)
where  ⟨ E ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle E\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle E\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/70555e89feeab8115d807a1fb93b9636475e3566) is the average energy,  ⟨ E ⟩ = E avg = ⟨ ψ | H | ψ ⟩ = ∑ n | c n | 2 E n . {\displaystyle \langle E\rangle =E_{\text{avg}}=\langle \psi |H|\psi \rangle =\sum _{n}|c_{n}|^{2}E_{n}.} ![{\\displaystyle \\langle E\\rangle =E_{\\text{avg}}=\\langle \\psi |H|\\psi \\rangle =\\sum _{n}|c_{n}|^{2}E_{n}.}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/2b5c5b46aa9abf151ef3acf8330741bf1080f1b6) This form applies when the Hamiltonian is not time-dependent, and the ground-state energy is defined to be zero. 
### For time-varying states
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_speed_limit&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: For time-varying states")]
The Margolus–Levitin theorem can also be generalized to the case where the Hamiltonian varies with time, and the system is described by a [mixed state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_quantum_state "Mixed quantum state").[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-Deffner2013-17) In this form, it is given by       ∫ 0 τ | tr ( ρ t H t ) | d t ≥ ℏ D B ( ρ 0 , ρ τ ) {\displaystyle \int _{0}^{\tau }|{\text{tr}}(\rho _{t}H_{t})|dt\geq \hbar D_{B}(\rho _{0},\rho _{\tau })} ![{\\displaystyle \\int _{0}^{\\tau }|{\\text{tr}}\(\\rho _{t}H_{t}\)|dt\\geq \\hbar D_{B}\(\\rho _{0},\\rho _{\\tau }\)}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/635968831e9560bc604e93f44ddee37849933826)
with the ground-state defined so that it has energy zero at all times. 
This provides a result for time varying states. Although it also provides a bound for mixed states, the bound (for mixed states) can be so loose as to be uninformative.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-18) The Margolus–Levitin theorem has not yet been experimentally established in time-dependent quantum systems, whose Hamiltonians  H t {\displaystyle H_{t}} ![{\\displaystyle H_{t}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/5e33c7739bb90406c5b8dd53d3f9cb82b071ac2b) are driven by arbitrary time-dependent parameters, except for the adiabatic case.[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-CETURDQS-19)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_speed_limit&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Dual Margolus–Levitin limit")]
In addition to the original Margolus–Levitin limit, a dual bound exists for quantum systems with a bounded energy spectrum. This dual bound, also known as the Ness–Alberti–Sagi limit or the Ness limit, depends on the difference between the state's mean energy and the energy of the highest occupied eigenstate. In bounded systems, the minimum time  τ ⊥ {\displaystyle \tau _{\perp }} ![{\\displaystyle \\tau _{\\perp }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/bfd5e546dc009355de18edb556f1ce9fe74e7f6a) required for a state to evolve to an orthogonal state is bounded by 
τ ⊥ ≥ h 4 ( E max − ⟨ E ⟩ ) , {\displaystyle \tau _{\perp }\geq {\frac {h}{4(E_{\max }-\langle E\rangle )}},} ![{\\displaystyle \\tau _{\\perp }\\geq {\\frac {h}{4\(E_{\\max }-\\langle E\\rangle \)}},}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/dcabd117e3b059bc7b58d4dfea2fa9ee03771975)
where  E max {\displaystyle E_{\max }} ![{\\displaystyle E_{\\max }}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/c216e15937a36c6d77e18204a4ade1162daa93bf) is the energy of the highest occupied eigenstate and  ⟨ E ⟩ {\displaystyle \langle E\rangle } ![{\\displaystyle \\langle E\\rangle }](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/70555e89feeab8115d807a1fb93b9636475e3566) is the mean energy of the state. This bound complements the original Margolus–Levitin limit and the Mandelstam–Tamm limit, forming a trio of constraints on quantum evolution speed.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-20)

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Quantum_speed_limit&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Bremermann's limit")]
Main article: [Bremermann's limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bremermann%27s_limit "Bremermann's limit")
The quantum speed limit bounds establish an upper bound at which [computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computation "Computation") can be performed. Computational machinery is constructed out of physical matter that follows quantum mechanics, and each operation, if it is to be unambiguous, must be a transition of the system from one state to an orthogonal state. Suppose the computing machinery is a physical system evolving under Hamiltonian that does not change with time. Then, according to the Margolus–Levitin theorem, the number of operations per unit time per unit energy is bounded above by       2 ℏ π = 6 × 10 33 s − 1 ⋅ J − 1 {\displaystyle {\frac {2}{\hbar \pi }}=6\times 10^{33}\mathrm {s} ^{-1}\cdot \mathrm {J} ^{-1}} ![{\\displaystyle {\\frac {2}{\\hbar \\pi }}=6\\times 10^{33}\\mathrm {s} ^{-1}\\cdot \\mathrm {J} ^{-1}}](https://wikimedia.org/api/rest_v1/media/math/render/svg/1f2fbbf6dd3c9dc2e907963dc68449cc2341623b)
This establishes a strict upper limit on the number of calculations that can be performed by physical matter. The processing rate of _all_ forms of computation cannot be higher than about 6 × 1033 operations per second per [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule "Joule") of energy. This is including "classical" computers, since even classical computers are still made of matter that follows quantum mechanics.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-22)
This bound is not merely a fanciful limit: it has practical ramifications for [quantum-resistant cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum-resistant_cryptography "Quantum-resistant cryptography"). Imagining a computer operating at this limit, a [brute-force search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brute-force_search "Brute-force search") to break a 128-bit [encryption key](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption_key "Encryption key") requires only modest resources. Brute-forcing a 256-bit key requires planetary-scale computers, while a brute-force search of 512-bit keys is effectively unattainable within the lifetime of the universe, even if galactic-sized computers were applied to the problem. 
The [Bekenstein bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bekenstein_bound "Bekenstein bound") limits the amount of information that can be stored within a volume of space. The maximal rate of change of information within that volume of space is given by the quantum speed limit. This product of limits is sometimes called the **Bremermann–Bekenstein limit** ; it is saturated by [Hawking radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawking_radiation "Hawking radiation").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_speed_limit#cite_note-DEF-1) That is, Hawking radiation is emitted at the maximal allowed rate set by these bounds.
