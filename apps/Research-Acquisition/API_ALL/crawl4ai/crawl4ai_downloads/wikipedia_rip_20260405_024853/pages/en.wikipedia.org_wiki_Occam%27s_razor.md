Philosophical problem-solving principle
For other uses, see [Occam's Razor (disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_Razor_\(disambiguation\) "Occam's Razor \(disambiguation\)").
In [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy "Philosophy"), **Occam's razor** (also spelled **Ockham's razor** or **Ocham's razor** ; [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language"): _novacula Occami_) is the [problem-solving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problem-solving "Problem-solving") principle that recommends searching for explanations constructed with the smallest possible set of elements. It is also known as the **principle of parsimony** or the **law of parsimony** ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language "Latin language"): _lex parsimoniae_). Attributed to [William of Ockham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham "William of Ockham"), a 14th-century English [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher "Philosopher") and [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theologian "Theologian"), it is frequently cited as _Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem_ , which translates as "Entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity",[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-2) although Occam never used those exact words. Popularly, the principle is sometimes paraphrased as "of two competing theories, the simpler explanation of an entity is to be preferred".[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Britannica2021-3)
This [philosophical razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_razor "Philosophical razor") advocates that when presented with competing [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis "Hypothesis") about the same prediction and both hypotheses have equal explanatory power, one should prefer the hypothesis that requires the fewest assumptions,[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-:0-4) and that this is not meant to be a way of choosing between hypotheses that make different predictions. Similarly, in science, Occam's razor is used as an [abductive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abductive_reasoning "Abductive reasoning") [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic "Heuristic") in the development of theoretical models rather than as a rigorous arbiter between candidate models.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(100\)-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(101\)-6)
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")]
The phrase _Occam's razor_ did not appear until a few centuries after William of Ockham's death in 1347. [Libert Froidmont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libert_Froidmont "Libert Froidmont"), in his 1649 _Philosophia Christiana de Anima_ (_On Christian Philosophy of the Soul_), gives him credit for the phrase, speaking of "_novacula occami_ ".[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Sober_2015_4-7) Ockham did not invent this principle, but its fame – and its association with him – may be due to the frequency and effectiveness with which he used it.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-8) Ockham stated the principle in various ways, but the most popular version – "Entities are not to be multiplied without necessity" (_Non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate_) – was formulated by the Irish [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscan "Franciscan") philosopher [John Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Punch_\(theologian\) "John Punch \(theologian\)") in his 1639 commentary on the works of [Duns Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duns_Scotus "Duns Scotus").[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-commentary-9)
### Formulations before William of Ockham
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Formulations before William of Ockham")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/Pluralitas.jpg/250px-Pluralitas.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Pluralitas.jpg)Part of a page from [John Duns Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Duns_Scotus "John Duns Scotus")'s book _Commentaria oxoniensia ad IV libros magistri Sententiarus_ , showing the words: "_Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate_ ", i.e., "Plurality is not to be posited without necessity"
The origins of what has come to be known as Occam's razor are traceable to the works of earlier philosophers such as [John Duns Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Duns_Scotus "John Duns Scotus") (1265–1308), [Robert Grosseteste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Grosseteste "Robert Grosseteste") (1175–1253), [Maimonides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maimonides "Maimonides") (Moses ben-Maimon, 1138–1204), and even [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle "Aristotle") (384–322 BC).[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-10)[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-11) Aristotle writes in his _[Posterior Analytics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_Analytics "Posterior Analytics")_ , "We may assume the superiority _ceteris paribus_ [all other things being equal] of the demonstration which derives from fewer postulates or hypotheses." [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy "Ptolemy") (c. AD 90 – c. 168) stated, "We consider it a good principle to explain the phenomena by the simplest hypothesis possible."[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Franklin-12)
Phrases such as "It is vain to do with more what can be done with fewer" and "A plurality is not to be posited without necessity" were commonplace in 13th-century [scholastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism "Scholasticism") writing.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Franklin-12) Robert Grosseteste, in _Commentary on_ [Aristotle's] _the Posterior Analytics Books_ (_Commentarius in Posteriorum Analyticorum Libros_) (c. 1217–1220), declares: 
> "That is better and more valuable which requires fewer, other circumstances being equal ... For if one thing were demonstrated from many and another thing from fewer equally known premises, clearly that is better which is from fewer because it makes us know quickly, just as a universal demonstration is better than particular because it produces knowledge from fewer premises. Similarly in natural science, in moral science, and in metaphysics the best is that which needs no premises and the better that which needs the fewer, other circumstances being equal."[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-13)
The _[Summa Theologica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica "Summa Theologica")_ of [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas") (1225–1274) states that "it is superfluous to suppose that what can be accounted for by a few principles has been produced by many". Aquinas uses this principle to construct an objection to [God's existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God%27s_existence "God's existence"), an objection that he in turn answers and refutes generally (cf. _[quinque viae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinque_viae "Quinque viae")_), and specifically, through an argument based on [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causality "Causality").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-14) Hence, Aquinas acknowledges the principle that today is known as Occam's razor, but prefers causal explanations to other simple explanations (cf. also [Correlation does not imply causation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_does_not_imply_causation "Correlation does not imply causation")). 
### William of Ockham
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: William of Ockham")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ab/William_of_Ockham_-_Logica_1341.jpg/250px-William_of_Ockham_-_Logica_1341.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:William_of_Ockham_-_Logica_1341.jpg)[Manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript "Manuscript") illustration of William of Ockham
[William of Ockham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham "William of Ockham") (c. 1287–1347) was an English Franciscan friar and [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theologian "Theologian"), an influential medieval philosopher and a [nominalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominalist "Nominalist"). His popular fame as a great logician rests chiefly on the maxim attributed to him and known as Occam's razor. The term _razor_ refers to distinguishing between two hypotheses either by "shaving away" unnecessary assumptions or cutting apart two similar conclusions. 
While it has been claimed that Occam's razor is not found in any of William's writings,[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-15) one can cite statements such as _Numquam ponenda est pluralitas sine necessitate_ ("Plurality must never be posited without necessity"), which occurs in his theological work on the [_Sentences of Peter Lombard_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Four_Books_of_Sentences "The Four Books of Sentences") (_Quaestiones et decisiones in quattuor libros Sententiarum Petri Lombardi_ ; ed. Lugd., 1495, i, dist. 27, qu. 2, K). 
Nevertheless, the precise words sometimes attributed to William of Ockham, _Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem_ (Entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity),[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-16) are absent in his extant works;[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-17) this particular phrasing comes from [John Punch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Punch_\(theologian\) "John Punch \(theologian\)"),[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-18) who described the principle as a "common axiom" (_axioma vulgare_) of the Scholastics.[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-commentary-9) William of Ockham himself seems to restrict the operation of this principle in matters pertaining to miracles and God's power, considering a plurality of miracles possible in the [Eucharist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucharist "Eucharist")[_[further explanation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] simply because it pleases God.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Franklin-12)
This principle is sometimes phrased as _Pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate_ ("Plurality should not be posited without necessity").[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Britannica-19) In his _Summa Totius Logicae_ , i. 12, William of Ockham cites the principle of economy, _Frustra fit per plura quod potest fieri per pauciora_ ("It is futile to do with more things that which can be done with fewer"; Thorburn, 1918, pp. 352–53; [Kneale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Kneale_\(logician\) "William Kneale \(logician\)") and Kneale, 1962, p. 243.) 
### Later formulations
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Later formulations")]
To quote [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton"), "We are to admit no more causes of natural things than such as are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances. Therefore, to the same natural effects we must, as far as possible, assign the same causes."[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Hawking-20)[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-21) In the sentence [hypotheses non fingo ("I contrive no hypotheses")](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypotheses_non_fingo "Hypotheses non fingo"), Newton affirms the success of this approach. 
[Bertrand Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertrand_Russell "Bertrand Russell") offers a particular version of Occam's razor: "Whenever possible, substitute constructions out of known entities for inferences to unknown entities."[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-22)
Around 1960, [Ray Solomonoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Solomonoff "Ray Solomonoff") founded the [theory of universal inductive inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonoff%27s_theory_of_inductive_inference "Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference"), the theory of prediction based on observations – for example, predicting the next symbol based upon a given series of symbols. The only assumption is that the environment follows some unknown but computable probability distribution. This theory is a mathematical formalization of Occam's razor.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-ReferenceA-23)[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-ReferenceB-24)[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-25)
Another technical approach to Occam's razor is [ontological parsimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological_commitment#Ontological_parsimony "Ontological commitment").[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Baker-26) Parsimony means spareness and is also referred to as the Rule of Simplicity. This is considered a strong version of Occam's razor.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-math.ucr.edu-27)[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-28) A variation used in medicine is called the "[Zebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebra_\(medicine\) "Zebra \(medicine\)")": a physician should reject an exotic medical diagnosis when a more commonplace explanation is more likely, derived from [Theodore Woodward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Woodward "Theodore Woodward")'s dictum "When you hear hoofbeats, think of horses not zebras".[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-29)
[Ernst Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Mach "Ernst Mach") formulated the stronger version of Occam's razor into [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), which he called the Principle of Economy stating: "Scientists must use the simplest means of arriving at their results and exclude everything not perceived by the senses."[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-30)
This principle goes back at least as far as Aristotle, who wrote "Nature operates in the shortest way possible."[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-math.ucr.edu-27) The idea of parsimony or simplicity in deciding between theories, though not the intent of the original expression of Occam's razor, has been assimilated into common culture as the widespread layman's formulation that "the simplest explanation is usually the correct one".[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-math.ucr.edu-27)
## Justifications
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Justifications")]
### Aesthetic
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Aesthetic")]
Prior to the 20th century, it was a commonly held belief that nature itself was simple and that simpler hypotheses about nature were thus more likely to be true. This notion was deeply rooted in the aesthetic value that simplicity holds for human thought and the justifications presented for it often drew from [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology").[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas") made this argument in the 13th century, writing, "If a thing can be done adequately by means of one, it is superfluous to do it by means of several; for we observe that nature does not employ two instruments [if] one suffices."[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-31)
Beginning in the 20th century, [epistemological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology") justifications based on [induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_reasoning "Inductive reasoning"), [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic"), [pragmatism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatism "Pragmatism"), and especially [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory") have become more popular among philosophers.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Sober_2015_4-7)
### Empirical
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Empirical")]
Occam's razor has gained strong empirical support in helping to converge on better theories (see [Uses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#Uses) section below for some examples). 
In the related concept of [overfitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfitting "Overfitting"), excessively complex models are affected by [statistical noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_noise "Statistical noise") (a problem also known as the [bias–variance tradeoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias%E2%80%93variance_tradeoff "Bias–variance tradeoff")), whereas simpler models may capture the underlying structure better and may thus have better [predictive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predictive_inference "Predictive inference") performance. It is, however, often difficult to deduce which part of the data is noise (cf. [model selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_selection "Model selection"), [test set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_set "Test set"), [minimum description length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_description_length "Minimum description length"), [Bayesian inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference "Bayesian inference"), etc.). 
#### Testing the razor
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Testing the razor")]

The razor's statement that "other things being equal, simpler explanations are generally better than more complex ones" is amenable to empirical testing. Another interpretation of the razor's statement would be that "simpler hypotheses are generally better than the complex ones". The procedure to test the former interpretation would compare the track records of simple and comparatively complex explanations. If one accepts the first interpretation, the validity of Occam's razor as a tool would then have to be rejected if the more complex explanations were more often correct than the less complex ones (while the converse would lend support to its use). If the latter interpretation is accepted, the validity of Occam's razor as a tool could possibly be accepted if the simpler hypotheses led to correct conclusions more often than not. 
Even if some increases in complexity are sometimes necessary, there still remains a justified general bias toward the simpler of two competing explanations. To understand why, consider that for each accepted explanation of a phenomenon, there is always an infinite number of possible, more complex, and ultimately incorrect, alternatives. This is so because one can always burden a failing explanation with an [ad hoc hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_hoc_hypothesis "Ad hoc hypothesis"). Ad hoc hypotheses are justifications that prevent theories from being falsified. 
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/43/Celtic_Fairy_Tales-1892-048-1.jpg/250px-Celtic_Fairy_Tales-1892-048-1.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Celtic_Fairy_Tales-1892-048-1.jpg)Possible explanations can become needlessly complex. It might be coherent, for instance, to add the involvement of [leprechauns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprechaun "Leprechaun") to any explanation, but Occam's razor would prevent such additions unless they were necessary.
For example, if a man, accused of breaking a vase, makes [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural "Supernatural") claims that [leprechauns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprechauns "Leprechauns") were responsible for the breakage, a simple explanation might be that the man did it, but ongoing ad hoc justifications (e.g., "... and that's not me breaking it on the film; they tampered with that, too") could successfully prevent complete disproof. This endless supply of elaborate competing explanations, called saving hypotheses, cannot be technically ruled out – except by using Occam's razor.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Stanovich2007-32)[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-33)[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-34)
Any more complex theory might still possibly be true. A study of the predictive validity of Occam's razor found 32 published papers that included 97 comparisons of economic forecasts from simple and complex forecasting methods. None of the papers provided a balance of evidence that complexity of method improved forecast accuracy. In the 25 papers with quantitative comparisons, complexity increased forecast errors by an average of 27 percent.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-35)

### Mathematical
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Mathematical")]
Main article: [Akaike information criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akaike_information_criterion "Akaike information criterion")
One justification of Occam's razor is a direct result of basic [probability theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory "Probability theory"). By definition, all assumptions introduce possibilities for error; if an assumption does not improve the accuracy of a theory, its only effect is to increase the probability that the overall theory is wrong. 
There have also been other attempts to derive Occam's razor from probability theory, including notable attempts made by [Harold Jeffreys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Jeffreys "Harold Jeffreys") and [E. T. Jaynes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Thompson_Jaynes "Edwin Thompson Jaynes"). The probabilistic (Bayesian) basis for Occam's razor is elaborated by [David J. C. MacKay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_J._C._MacKay "David J. C. MacKay") in chapter 28 of his book _Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms_ ,[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-36) where he emphasizes that a prior bias in favor of simpler models is not required. 
[William H. Jefferys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Jefferys "William H. Jefferys") and [James O. Berger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Berger_\(statistician\) "James Berger \(statistician\)") (1991) generalize and quantify the original formulation's "assumptions" concept as the degree to which a proposition is unnecessarily accommodating to possible observable data.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Jefferys-37) They state, "A hypothesis with fewer adjustable parameters will automatically have an enhanced posterior probability, due to the fact that the predictions it makes are sharp."[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Jefferys-37) The use of "sharp" here is not only a tongue-in-cheek reference to the idea of a razor, but also indicates that such predictions are more [accurate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accuracy_and_precision "Accuracy and precision") than competing predictions. The model they propose balances the precision of a theory's predictions against their sharpness, preferring theories that sharply make correct predictions over theories that accommodate a wide range of other possible results. This, again, reflects the mathematical relationship between key concepts in [Bayesian inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference "Bayesian inference") (namely [marginal probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_probability "Marginal probability"), [conditional probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_probability "Conditional probability"), and [posterior probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_probability "Posterior probability")). 
The [bias–variance tradeoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias%E2%80%93variance_tradeoff "Bias–variance tradeoff") is a framework that incorporates the Occam's razor principle in its balance between overfitting (associated with lower bias but higher variance) and underfitting (associated with lower variance but higher bias).[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-38)
### Other philosophers
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Other philosophers")]
#### Karl Popper
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Karl Popper")]
[Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper "Karl Popper") argues that a preference for simple theories need not appeal to practical or aesthetic considerations. Our preference for simplicity may be justified by the [falsifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiability "Falsifiability") criterion: we prefer simpler theories to more complex ones "because their empirical content is greater; and because they are better testable".[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-39) The idea here is that a simple theory applies to more cases than a more complex one, and is thus more easily falsifiable. This is again comparing a simple theory to a more complex theory where both explain the data equally well. 
#### Elliott Sober
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Elliott Sober")]
The philosopher of science [Elliott Sober](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliott_Sober "Elliott Sober") once argued along the same lines as Popper, tying simplicity with "informativeness": The simplest theory is the more informative, in the sense that it requires less information to a question.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Sober975-40) He has since rejected this account of simplicity, purportedly because it fails to provide an [epistemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology "Epistemology") justification for simplicity. He now believes that simplicity considerations (and considerations of parsimony in particular) do not count unless they reflect something more fundamental. Philosophers, he suggests, may have made the error of hypostatizing simplicity (i.e., endowed it with a _[sui generis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sui_generis "Sui generis")_ existence), when it has meaning only when embedded in a specific context (Sober 1992). If we fail to justify simplicity considerations on the basis of the context in which we use them, we may have no non-circular justification: "Just as the question 'why be rational?' may have no non-circular answer, the same may be true of the question 'why should simplicity be considered in evaluating the plausibility of hypotheses?'"[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Sober2002-41)
#### Richard Swinburne
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Richard Swinburne")]
[Richard Swinburne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Swinburne "Richard Swinburne") argues for simplicity on logical grounds: 
> ... the simplest hypothesis proposed as an explanation of phenomena is more likely to be the true one than is any other available hypothesis, that its predictions are more likely to be true than those of any other available hypothesis, and that it is an ultimate _a priori_ epistemic principle that simplicity is evidence for truth.
— Swinburne 1997
According to Swinburne, since our choice of theory cannot be determined by data (see [Underdetermination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underdetermination "Underdetermination") and [Duhem–Quine thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duhem%E2%80%93Quine_thesis "Duhem–Quine thesis")), we must rely on some criterion to determine which theory to use. Since it is absurd to have no logical method for settling on one hypothesis amongst an infinite number of equally data-compliant hypotheses, we should choose the simplest theory: "Either science is irrational [in the way it judges theories and predictions probable] or the principle of simplicity is a fundamental synthetic a priori truth."[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-42)
#### Ludwig Wittgenstein

### Science and the scientific method
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Science and the scientific method")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/57/Heliocentric.jpg/250px-Heliocentric.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Heliocentric.jpg)[Andreas Cellarius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andreas_Cellarius "Andreas Cellarius")'s illustration of the Copernican system, from the _[Harmonia Macrocosmica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonia_Macrocosmica "Harmonia Macrocosmica")_ (1660). Future positions of the sun, moon and other solar system bodies can be calculated using a [geocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentrism "Geocentrism") model (the earth is at the centre) or using a [heliocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentrism#Modern_science "Heliocentrism") (the sun is at the centre). Both work, but the geocentric model requires a much more complex system of calculations than the heliocentric model. This was pointed out in a preface to [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus "Copernicus")'s first edition of _[De revolutionibus orbium coelestium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_revolutionibus_orbium_coelestium "De revolutionibus orbium coelestium")_.
In [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science "Science"), Occam's razor is used as a [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic "Heuristic") to guide scientists in developing theoretical models rather than as an arbiter between published models.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(100\)-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(101\)-6) In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics "Physics"), parsimony was an important heuristic in the development and application of the [principle of least action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_least_action "Principle of least action") by [Pierre Louis Maupertuis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Louis_Maupertuis "Pierre Louis Maupertuis") and [Leonhard Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler "Leonhard Euler"),[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(104\)-43) in [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein")'s formulation of [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity "Special relativity"),[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(102\)-44)[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(103\)-45) and in the development of [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics "Quantum mechanics") by [Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck "Max Planck"), [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg") and [Louis de Broglie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_de_Broglie "Louis de Broglie").[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(101\)-6)[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(105\)-46)
In [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry "Chemistry"), Occam's razor is often an important heuristic when developing a model of a [reaction mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_mechanism "Reaction mechanism").[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(107\)-47)[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(108\)-48) Although it is useful as a heuristic in developing models of reaction mechanisms, it has been shown to fail as a criterion for selecting among some selected published models.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(101\)-6) In this context, Einstein himself expressed caution when he formulated Einstein's [Constraint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constraint_counting "Constraint counting"): "It can scarcely be denied that the supreme goal of all theory is to make the irreducible basic elements as simple and as few as possible without having to surrender the adequate representation of a single datum of experience."[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-49)[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-50)[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-51) An often-quoted version of this constraint (which cannot be verified as posited by Einstein himself)[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-52) reduces this to "Everything should be kept as simple as possible, but not simpler." 
In the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method "Scientific method"), Occam's razor is not considered an irrefutable principle of [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic "Logic") or a scientific result; the preference for simplicity in the scientific method is based on the [falsifiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falsifiability "Falsifiability") criterion. For each accepted explanation of a phenomenon, there may be an extremely large, perhaps even incomprehensible, number of possible and more complex alternatives. Since failing explanations can always be burdened with [_ad hoc_ hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_hoc_hypothesis "Ad hoc hypothesis") to prevent them from being falsified, simpler theories are preferable to more complex ones because they tend to be more [testable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_method "Test method").[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(109\)-53)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(110\)-54)[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(114\)-55) As a logical principle, Occam's razor would demand that scientists accept the simplest possible theoretical explanation for existing data. However, science has shown repeatedly that future data often support more complex theories than do existing data. Science prefers the simplest explanation that is consistent with the data available at a given time, but the simplest explanation may be ruled out as new data become available.[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(100\)-5)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(110\)-54) That is, science is open to the possibility that future experiments might support more complex theories than demanded by current data and is more interested in designing experiments to discriminate between competing theories than favoring one theory over another based merely on philosophical principles.[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(109\)-53)[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(110\)-54)[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(114\)-55)
When scientists use the idea of parsimony, it has meaning only in a very specific context of inquiry. Several background assumptions are required for parsimony to connect with plausibility in a particular research problem.[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] The reasonableness of parsimony in one research context may have nothing to do with its reasonableness in another. It is a mistake to think that there is a single global principle that spans diverse subject matter.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(114\)-55)
It has been suggested that Occam's razor is a widely accepted example of extraevidential consideration, even though it is entirely a metaphysical assumption. Most of the time, however, Occam's razor is a conservative tool, cutting out "crazy, complicated constructions" and assuring "that hypotheses are grounded in the science of the day", thus yielding "normal" science: models of explanation and prediction.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(101\)-6) There are, however, notable exceptions where Occam's razor turns a conservative scientist into a reluctant revolutionary. For example, [Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck "Max Planck") interpolated between the [Wien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien_approximation "Wien approximation") and [Jeans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh%E2%80%93Jeans_law "Rayleigh–Jeans law") radiation laws and used Occam's razor logic to formulate the quantum hypothesis, even resisting that hypothesis as it became more obvious that it was correct.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(101\)-6)
Appeals to simplicity were used to argue against the phenomena of meteorites, [ball lightning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_lightning "Ball lightning"), [continental drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continental_drift "Continental drift"), and [reverse transcriptase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reverse_transcriptase "Reverse transcriptase").[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-56) One can argue for atomic building blocks for matter, because it provides a simpler explanation for the observed reversibility of both mixing[_[clarification needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Please_clarify "Wikipedia:Please clarify")_] and chemical reactions as simple separation and rearrangements of atomic building blocks. At the time, however, the [atomic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory "Atomic theory") was considered more complex because it implied the existence of invisible particles that had not been directly detected. [Ernst Mach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Mach "Ernst Mach") and the logical positivists rejected [John Dalton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dalton "John Dalton")'s [atomic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_theory "Atomic theory") until the reality of atoms was more evident in [Brownian motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownian_motion "Brownian motion"), as shown by [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein").[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Pojman2009-57)
In the same way, postulating the [aether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminiferous_aether "Luminiferous aether") is more complex than transmission of light through a [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum "Vacuum"). At the time, however, all known waves propagated through a physical medium, and it seemed simpler to postulate the existence of a medium than to theorize about wave propagation without a medium. Likewise, [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton "Isaac Newton")'s idea of light particles seemed simpler than [Christiaan Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens "Christiaan Huygens")'s idea of waves, so many favored it. In this case, as it turned out, neither the wave – nor the particle – explanation alone suffices, as [light behaves like waves and like particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave%E2%80%93particle_duality "Wave–particle duality"). 
Three axioms presupposed by the scientific method are realism (the existence of objective reality), the existence of natural laws, and the constancy of natural law. Rather than depend on provability of these axioms, science depends on the fact that they have not been objectively falsified. Occam's razor and parsimony support, but do not prove, these axioms of science. The general principle of science is that theories (or models) of natural law must be consistent with repeatable experimental observations. This ultimate arbiter (selection criterion) rests upon the axioms mentioned above.[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(110\)-54)
If multiple models of natural law make exactly the same testable predictions, they are equivalent and there is no need for parsimony to choose a preferred one. For example, [Newtonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_mechanics "Newtonian mechanics"), [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics "Hamiltonian mechanics") and [Lagrangian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_mechanics "Lagrangian mechanics") classical mechanics are equivalent. Physicists have no interest in using Occam's razor to say the other two are wrong. Likewise, there is no demand for simplicity principles to arbitrate between wave and matrix formulations of quantum mechanics. Science often does not demand arbitration or selection criteria between models that make the same testable predictions.[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(110\)-54)
### Biology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Biology")]
![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/f/f2/Edit-clear.svg/40px-Edit-clear.svg.png) | This section **has an unclear[citation style](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources#Citation_style "Wikipedia:Citing sources")**. The references used may be made clearer with a different or consistent style of [citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources") and [footnoting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Footnotes "Help:Footnotes"). _( January 2023)__([Learn how and when to remove this message](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:Maintenance_template_removal "Help:Maintenance template removal"))_

Biologists or philosophers of biology use Occam's razor in either of two contexts both in [evolutionary biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution "Evolution"): the units of selection controversy and [systematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematics "Systematics"). [George C. Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_C._Williams_\(biologist\) "George C. Williams \(biologist\)") in his book _[Adaptation and Natural Selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation_and_Natural_Selection "Adaptation and Natural Selection")_ (1966) argues that the best way to explain [altruism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altruism "Altruism") among animals is based on low-level (i.e., individual) selection as opposed to high-level group selection. Altruism is defined by some evolutionary biologists (e.g., R. Alexander, 1987; W. D. Hamilton, 1964) as behavior that is beneficial to others (or to the group) at a cost to the individual, and many posit individual selection as the mechanism that explains altruism solely in terms of the behaviors of individual organisms acting in their own self-interest (or in the interest of their genes, via kin selection). Williams was arguing against the perspective of others who propose selection at the level of the group as an evolutionary mechanism that selects for altruistic traits (e.g., D. S. Wilson & E. O. Wilson, 2007). The basis for Williams's contention is that of the two, individual selection is the more parsimonious theory. In doing so he is invoking a variant of Occam's razor known as [Morgan's Canon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgan%27s_Canon "Morgan's Canon"): "In no case is an animal activity to be interpreted in terms of higher psychological processes, if it can be fairly interpreted in terms of processes which stand lower in the scale of psychological evolution and development." (Morgan 1903). 
However, more recent biological analyses, such as [Richard Dawkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Dawkins "Richard Dawkins")'s _[The Selfish Gene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Selfish_Gene "The Selfish Gene")_ , have contended that Morgan's Canon is not the simplest and most basic explanation. Dawkins argues the way evolution works is that the genes propagated in most copies end up determining the development of that particular species, i.e., natural selection turns out to select specific genes, and this is really the fundamental underlying principle that automatically gives individual and group selection as [emergent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergent_evolution "Emergent evolution") features of evolution. 
[Zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology "Zoology") provides an example. [Muskoxen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muskox "Muskox"), when threatened by [wolves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gray_wolf "Gray wolf"), form a circle with the males on the outside and the females and young on the inside. This is an example of a behavior by the males that seems to be altruistic. The behavior is disadvantageous to them individually but beneficial to the group as a whole; thus, it was seen by some to support the group selection theory. Another interpretation is kin selection: if the males are protecting their offspring, they are protecting copies of their own alleles. Engaging in this behavior would be favored by individual selection if the cost to the male musk ox is less than half of the benefit received by his calf – which could easily be the case if wolves have an easier time killing calves than adult males. It could also be the case that male musk oxen would be individually less likely to be killed by wolves if they stood in a circle with their horns pointing out, regardless of whether they were protecting the females and offspring. That would be an example of regular natural selection – a phenomenon called "[the selfish herd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfish_herd_theory "Selfish herd theory")". 
[Systematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematics "Systematics") is the branch of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology "Biology") that attempts to establish patterns of relationship among biological taxa, today generally thought to reflect evolutionary history. It is also concerned with their classification. There are three primary camps in systematics: cladists, pheneticists, and evolutionary taxonomists. Cladists hold that classification should be based on [synapomorphies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapomorphies "Synapomorphies") (shared, derived character states), pheneticists contend that overall similarity (synapomorphies and complementary [symplesiomorphies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplesiomorphies "Symplesiomorphies")) is the determining criterion, while evolutionary taxonomists say that both genealogy and similarity count in classification (in a manner determined by the evolutionary taxonomist).[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-58)[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-59)
It is among the cladists that Occam's razor is applied, through the method of _cladistic parsimony_. Cladistic parsimony (or [maximum parsimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_parsimony "Maximum parsimony")) is a method of phylogenetic inference that yields [phylogenetic trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phylogenetic_tree "Phylogenetic tree") (more specifically, cladograms). [Cladograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cladistics "Cladistics") are branching, diagrams used to represent hypotheses of relative degree of relationship, based on [synapomorphies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synapomorphies "Synapomorphies"). Cladistic parsimony is used to select as the preferred hypothesis of relationships the cladogram that requires the fewest implied character state transformations (or smallest weight, if characters are differentially weighted). Critics of the cladistic approach often observe that for some types of data, parsimony could produce the wrong results, regardless of how much data is collected (this is called statistical inconsistency, or [long branch attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_branch_attraction "Long branch attraction")). However, this criticism is also potentially true for any type of phylogenetic inference, unless the model used to estimate the tree reflects the way that evolution actually happened. Because this information is not empirically accessible, the criticism of statistical inconsistency against parsimony holds no force.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-60) For a book-length treatment of cladistic parsimony, see [Elliott Sober](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliott_Sober "Elliott Sober")'s _Reconstructing the Past: Parsimony, Evolution, and Inference_ (1988). For a discussion of both uses of Occam's razor in biology, see Sober's article "Let's Razor Ockham's Razor" (1990). 
Other methods for inferring evolutionary relationships use parsimony in a more general way. [Likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood_function "Likelihood function") methods for phylogeny use parsimony as they do for all likelihood tests, with hypotheses requiring fewer differing parameters (i.e., numbers or different rates of character change or different frequencies of character state transitions) being treated as null hypotheses relative to hypotheses requiring more differing parameters. Thus, complex hypotheses must predict data much better than do simple hypotheses before researchers reject the simple hypotheses. Recent advances employ [information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory "Information theory"), a close cousin of likelihood, which uses Occam's razor in the same way. The choice of the "shortest tree" relative to a not-so-short tree under any optimality criterion (smallest distance, fewest steps, or maximum likelihood) is always based on parsimony.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-61)
[Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick") has commented on potential limitations of Occam's razor in biology. He advances the argument that because biological systems are the products of (an ongoing) natural selection, the mechanisms are not necessarily optimal in an obvious sense. He cautions: "While Ockham's razor is a useful tool in the physical sciences, it can be a very dangerous implement in biology. It is thus very rash to use simplicity and elegance as a guide in biological research."[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-62) This is an ontological critique of parsimony. 
In [biogeography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biogeography "Biogeography"), parsimony is used to infer ancient vicariant events or [migrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historical_migration "Historical migration") of [species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Species "Species") or [populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_\(biology\) "Population \(biology\)") by observing the geographic distribution and relationships of existing [organisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism "Organism"). Given the phylogenetic tree, ancestral population subdivisions are inferred to be those that require the minimum amount of change.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
### Religion
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Religion")]
Main article: [Existence of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence_of_God "Existence of God")
In the [philosophy of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_religion "Philosophy of religion"), Occam's razor is sometimes applied to the existence of God. William of Ockham himself was a [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"). He believed in God, and in the [authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblical_authority "Biblical authority") of [Christian scripture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_scripture "Christian scripture"); he writes that "nothing ought to be posited without a reason given, unless it is self-evident (literally, known through itself) or known by experience or proved by the authority of Sacred Scripture."[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-63) Ockham believed that an explanation has no sufficient basis in reality when it does not harmonize with reason, experience, or the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Bible "Christian Bible"). Unlike many theologians of his time, though, Ockham did not believe God could be logically proven with arguments. To Ockham, science was a matter of discovery; [theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology") was a matter of [revelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation "Revelation") and [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith "Faith"). He states: "Only faith gives us access to theological truths. The ways of God are not open to reason, for God has freely chosen to create a world and establish a way of salvation within it apart from any necessary laws that human logic or rationality can uncover."[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-64)
[Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas"), in the _[Summa Theologica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Theologica "Summa Theologica")_ , uses a formulation of Occam's razor to construct an objection to the idea that God exists, which he refutes directly with a counterargument:[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-65)
> Further, it is superfluous to suppose that what can be accounted for by a few principles has been produced by many. But it seems that everything we see in the world can be accounted for by other principles, supposing God did not exist. For all natural things can be reduced to one principle which is nature; and all voluntary things can be reduced to one principle which is human reason, or will. Therefore there is no need to suppose God's existence.
In turn, Aquinas answers this with the _[quinque viae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quinque_viae "Quinque viae")_ , and addresses the particular objection above with the following answer: 
> Since nature works for a determinate end under the direction of a higher agent, whatever is done by nature must needs be traced back to God, as to its first cause. So also whatever is done voluntarily must also be traced back to some higher cause other than human reason or will, since these can change or fail; for all things that are changeable and capable of defect must be traced back to an immovable and self-necessary first principle, as was shown in the body of the Article.
Rather than argue for the necessity of a god, some [theists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theism "Theism") base their belief upon grounds independent of, or prior to, reason, making Occam's razor irrelevant. This was the stance of [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8ren_Kierkegaard "Søren Kierkegaard"), who viewed belief in God as a [leap of faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_of_faith "Leap of faith") that sometimes directly opposed reason.[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-66) This is also the doctrine of [Gordon Clark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Clark "Gordon Clark")'s [presuppositional apologetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presuppositional_apologetics "Presuppositional apologetics"), with the exception that Clark never thought the leap of faith was contrary to reason (see also [Fideism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fideism "Fideism")). 
Various [arguments in favor of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arguments_for_the_existence_of_God "Arguments for the existence of God") establish God as a useful or even necessary assumption. Contrastingly some anti-theists hold firmly to the belief that assuming the existence of God introduces unnecessary complexity (e.g., the [Ultimate Boeing 747 gambit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_Boeing_747_gambit "Ultimate Boeing 747 gambit") from Dawkins's _[The God Delusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_God_Delusion "The God Delusion")_[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-67)).[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-68)
Another application of the principle is to be found in the work of [George Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berkeley "George Berkeley") (1685–1753). Berkeley was an idealist who believed that all of reality could be explained in terms of the mind alone. He invoked Occam's razor against [materialism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism "Materialism"), stating that matter was not required by his metaphysics and was thus eliminable. One potential problem with this belief[_[for whom?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:WHATPLACE "Wikipedia:WHATPLACE")_] is that it's possible, given Berkeley's position, to find [solipsism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solipsism "Solipsism") itself more in line with the razor than a God-mediated world beyond a single thinker. 
Occam's razor may also be recognized in the apocryphal story about an exchange between [Pierre-Simon Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Simon_Laplace "Pierre-Simon Laplace") and [Napoleon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napoleon "Napoleon"). It is said that in praising Laplace for one of his recent publications, the emperor asked how it was that the name of God, which featured so frequently in the writings of [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange "Lagrange"), appeared nowhere in Laplace's. At that, he is said to have replied, "It's because I had no need of that hypothesis."[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-69) Though some points of this story illustrate Laplace's [atheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism "Atheism"), more careful consideration suggests that he may instead have intended merely to illustrate the power of [methodological naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodological_naturalism "Methodological naturalism"), or even simply that the fewer [logical premises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premise "Premise") one assumes, the [stronger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_mathematical_jargon#strong "List of mathematical jargon") is one's conclusion. 
### Philosophy of mind
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Philosophy of mind")]
In his article "Sensations and Brain Processes" (1959), [J. J. C. Smart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._C._Smart "J. J. C. Smart") invoked Occam's razor with the aim to justify his preference of the [mind–brain identity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_physicalism "Type physicalism") over [spirit–body dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind%E2%80%93body_dualism "Mind–body dualism"). Dualists state that there are two kinds of substances in the universe: physical (including the body) and spiritual, which is non-physical. In contrast, identity theorists state that everything is physical, including consciousness, and that there is nothing nonphysical. Though it is impossible to appreciate the spiritual when limiting oneself to the physical,[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Smart maintained that identity theory explains all phenomena by assuming only a physical reality. Subsequently, Smart has been severely criticized for his use (or misuse) of Occam's razor and ultimately retracted his advocacy of it in this context. [Paul Churchland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Churchland "Paul Churchland") (1984) states that by itself Occam's razor is inconclusive regarding duality. In a similar way, Dale Jacquette (1994) stated that Occam's razor has been used in attempts to justify eliminativism and reductionism in the philosophy of mind. Eliminativism is the thesis that the ontology of [folk psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_psychology "Folk psychology") including such entities as "pain", "joy", "desire", "fear", etc., are eliminable in favor of an ontology of a completed neuroscience. 
### Penal ethics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Penal ethics")]
In penal theory and the philosophy of punishment, parsimony refers specifically to taking care in the distribution of [punishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punishment "Punishment") in order to avoid excessive punishment. In the [utilitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism "Utilitarianism") approach to the philosophy of punishment, [Jeremy Bentham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Bentham "Jeremy Bentham")'s "parsimony principle" states that any punishment greater than is required to achieve its end is unjust. The concept is related but not identical to the legal concept of [proportionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportionality_\(law\) "Proportionality \(law\)"). Parsimony is a key consideration of the modern [restorative justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restorative_justice "Restorative justice"), and is a component of utilitarian approaches to punishment, as well as the [prison abolition movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prison_abolition_movement "Prison abolition movement"). Bentham believed that true parsimony would require punishment to be individualised to take account of the [sensibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensibility "Sensibility") of the individual – an individual more sensitive to punishment should be given a proportionately lesser one, since otherwise needless pain would be inflicted. Later utilitarian writers have tended to abandon this idea, in large part due to the impracticality of determining each alleged criminal's relative sensitivity to specific punishments.[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-70)
### Probability theory and statistics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Probability theory and statistics")]
Marcus Hutter's universal artificial intelligence builds upon [Solomonoff's mathematical formalization of the razor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonoff%27s_theory_of_inductive_inference "Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference") to calculate the expected value of an action. 
There are various papers in scholarly journals deriving formal versions of Occam's razor from probability theory, applying it in [statistical inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_inference "Statistical inference"), and using it to come up with criteria for penalizing complexity in statistical inference. Papers[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-ReferenceC-71)[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-auto-72) have suggested a connection between Occam's razor and [Kolmogorov complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_complexity "Kolmogorov complexity").[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Volker-73)
One of the problems with the original formulation of the razor is that it only applies to models with the same explanatory power (i.e., it only tells us to prefer the simplest of equally good models). A more general form of the razor can be derived from Bayesian model comparison, which is based on [Bayes factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayes_factor "Bayes factor") and can be used to compare models that do not fit the observations equally well. These methods can sometimes optimally balance the complexity and power of a model. Generally, the exact Occam factor is intractable, but approximations such as [Akaike information criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akaike_information_criterion "Akaike information criterion"), [Bayesian information criterion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_information_criterion "Bayesian information criterion"), [Variational Bayesian methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_Bayesian_methods "Variational Bayesian methods"), [false discovery rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_discovery_rate "False discovery rate"), and [Laplace's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace%27s_method "Laplace's method") are used. Many [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") researchers are now employing such techniques, for instance through work on [Occam Learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam_Learning "Occam Learning") or more generally on the [Free energy principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_energy_principle "Free energy principle"). 
Statistical versions of Occam's razor have a more rigorous formulation than what philosophical discussions produce. In particular, they must have a specific definition of the term _simplicity_ , and that definition can vary. For example, in the [Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Kolmogorov "Andrey Kolmogorov")–[Chaitin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_Chaitin "Gregory Chaitin") [minimum description length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_description_length "Minimum description length") approach, the subject must pick a [Turing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_machine "Turing machine") whose operations describe the basic operations _believed_ to represent "simplicity" by the subject. However, one could always choose a Turing machine with a simple operation that happened to construct one's entire theory and would hence score highly under the razor. This has led to two opposing camps: one that believes Occam's razor is objective, and one that believes it is subjective. 
#### Objective razor
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Objective razor")]
The minimum instruction set of a [universal Turing machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Turing_machine "Universal Turing machine") requires approximately the same length description across different formulations, and is small compared to the [Kolmogorov complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_complexity "Kolmogorov complexity") of most practical theories. [Marcus Hutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Hutter "Marcus Hutter") has used this consistency to define a "natural" Turing machine of small size as the proper basis for excluding arbitrarily complex instruction sets in the formulation of razors.[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-74) Describing the program for the universal program as the "hypothesis", and the representation of the evidence as program data, it has been formally proven under [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo%E2%80%93Fraenkel_set_theory "Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory") that "the sum of the log universal probability of the model plus the log of the probability of the data given the model should be minimized."[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-75) Interpreting this as minimising the total length of a two-part message encoding model followed by data given model gives us the [minimum message length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_message_length "Minimum message length") (MML) principle.[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-ReferenceC-71)[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-auto-72)
One possible conclusion from mixing the concepts of Kolmogorov complexity and Occam's razor is that an ideal data compressor would also be a scientific explanation/formulation generator. Some attempts have been made to re-derive known laws from considerations of simplicity or compressibility.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-ReferenceB-24)[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-76)
According to [Jürgen Schmidhuber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Schmidhuber "Jürgen Schmidhuber"), the appropriate mathematical theory of Occam's razor already exists, namely, [Solomonoff's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Solomonoff "Ray Solomonoff") [theory of optimal inductive inference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomonoff%27s_theory_of_inductive_inference "Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference")[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-77) and its extensions.[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-78) See discussions in David L. Dowe's "Foreword re C. S. Wallace"[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-79) for the subtle distinctions between the [algorithmic probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_probability "Algorithmic probability") work of Solomonoff and the MML work of [Chris Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chris_Wallace_\(computer_scientist\) "Chris Wallace \(computer scientist\)"), and see Dowe's "MML, hybrid Bayesian network graphical models, statistical consistency, invariance and uniqueness"[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-80) both for such discussions and for (in section 4) discussions of MML and Occam's razor. For a specific example of MML as Occam's razor in the problem of decision tree induction, see Dowe and Needham's "Message Length as an Effective Ockham's Razor in Decision Tree Induction".[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-81)

The [no free lunch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_free_lunch_theorem "No free lunch theorem") (NFL) theorems for inductive inference prove that Occam's razor must rely on ultimately arbitrary assumptions concerning the prior probability distribution found in our world.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Adam2019-82) Specifically, suppose one is given two inductive inference algorithms, A and B, where A is a [Bayesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_inference "Bayesian inference") procedure based on the choice of some prior distribution motivated by Occam's razor (e.g., the prior might favor hypotheses with smaller [Kolmogorov complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolmogorov_complexity "Kolmogorov complexity")). Suppose that B is the anti-Bayes procedure, which calculates what the Bayesian algorithm A based on Occam's razor will predict – and then predicts the exact opposite. Then there are just as many actual priors (including those different from the Occam's razor prior assumed by A) in which algorithm B outperforms A as priors in which the procedure A based on Occam's razor comes out on top. In particular, the NFL theorems show that the "Occam factors" Bayesian argument for Occam's razor must make ultimately arbitrary modeling assumptions.[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-WOLP95-83)
### Software development
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Software development")]
In software development, the [rule of least power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_least_power "Rule of least power") argues the correct [programming language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_language "Programming language") to use is the one that is simplest while also solving the targeted software problem. In that form the rule is often credited to [Tim Berners-Lee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Berners-Lee "Tim Berners-Lee") since it appeared in his design guidelines for the original [Hypertext Transfer Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypertext_Transfer_Protocol "Hypertext Transfer Protocol").[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-84) Complexity in this context is measured either by placing a language into the [Chomsky hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chomsky_hierarchy "Chomsky hierarchy") or by listing idiomatic features of the language and comparing according to some agreed to scale of difficulties between idioms. Many languages once thought to be of lower complexity have evolved or later been discovered to be more complex than originally intended; so, in practice this rule is applied to the relative ease of a programmer to obtain the power of the language, rather than the precise theoretical limits of the language. 
### Artificial intelligence
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Artificial intelligence")]
Scientists have discovered that [deep neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_neural_network "Deep neural network") (DNN) prefer simpler mathematical functions while learning. This simplicity bias enables DNNs to overcome [overfitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfitting "Overfitting") – a scenario where the model gets overwhelmed with noise due to the presence of too many parameters.[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-85)
## Controversial aspects
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Controversial aspects")]
Occam's razor is not an embargo against the positing of any kind of entity, or a recommendation of the simplest theory come what may.[[a]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-87) Occam's razor is used to adjudicate between theories that have already passed "theoretical scrutiny" tests and are equally well-supported by evidence.[[b]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-88) Furthermore, it may be used to prioritize empirical testing between two equally plausible but unequally testable hypotheses; thereby minimizing costs and wastes while increasing chances of falsification of the simpler-to-test hypothesis.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_]
Another contentious aspect of the razor is that a theory can become more complex in terms of its structure (or [syntax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax "Syntax")), while its [ontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology "Ontology") (or [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics "Semantics")) becomes simpler, or vice versa.[[c]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-89) Quine, in a discussion on definition, referred to these two perspectives as "economy of practical expression" and "economy in grammar and vocabulary", respectively.[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-90)
[Galileo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Galilei "Galileo Galilei") lampooned the _misuse_ of Occam's razor in his _[Dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue_Concerning_the_Two_Chief_World_Systems "Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems")_. The principle is represented in the dialogue by Simplicio. The telling point that Galileo presented ironically was that if one really wanted to start from a small number of entities, one could always consider the letters of the alphabet as the fundamental entities, since one could construct the whole of human knowledge out of them. 
Instances of using Occam's razor to justify belief in less complex and more simple theories have been criticized as using the razor inappropriately. For instance [Francis Crick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Crick "Francis Crick") stated that "While Occam's razor is a useful tool in the physical sciences, it can be a very dangerous implement in biology. It is thus very rash to use simplicity and elegance as a guide in biological research."[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-91)
## Anti-razors
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=28 "Edit section: Anti-razors")]
Occam's razor has met some opposition from people who consider it too extreme or rash. [Walter Chatton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Chatton "Walter Chatton") (c. 1290–1343) was a contemporary of William of Ockham who took exception to Occam's razor and Ockham's use of it. In response he devised his own _anti-razor_ : "If three things are not enough to verify an affirmative proposition about things, a fourth must be added and so on." Although there have been several philosophers who have formulated similar anti-razors since Chatton's time, no one anti-razor has perpetuated as notably as Chatton's anti-razor, although this could be the case of the Late Renaissance Italian motto of unknown attribution _Se non è vero, è ben trovato_ ("Even if it is not true, it is well conceived") when referred to a particularly artful explanation. 
Anti-razors have also been created by [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz "Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz") (1646–1716), [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant "Immanuel Kant") (1724–1804), and [Karl Menger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Menger "Karl Menger") (1902–1985). Leibniz's version took the form of a [principle of plenitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_plenitude "Principle of plenitude"), as [Arthur Lovejoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Lovejoy "Arthur Lovejoy") has called it: the idea being that God created the most varied and populous of possible worlds. Kant felt a need to moderate the effects of Occam's razor and thus created his own counter-razor: "The variety of beings should not rashly be diminished."[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-92)
Karl Menger found mathematicians to be too parsimonious with regard to variables so he formulated his Law Against Miserliness, which took one of two forms: "Entities must not be reduced to the point of inadequacy" and "It is vain to do with fewer what requires more." A less serious but even more extremist anti-razor is ['Pataphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%27Pataphysics "'Pataphysics"), the "science of imaginary solutions" developed by [Alfred Jarry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Jarry "Alfred Jarry") (1873–1907). Perhaps the ultimate in anti-reductionism, "'Pataphysics seeks no less than to view each event in the universe as completely unique, subject to no laws but its own." Variations on this theme were subsequently explored by the Argentine writer [Jorge Luis Borges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Luis_Borges "Jorge Luis Borges") in his story/mock-essay "[Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tl%C3%B6n,_Uqbar,_Orbis_Tertius "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius")". Physicist [R. V. Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reginald_Victor_Jones "Reginald Victor Jones") contrived Crabtree's Bludgeon, which states that "[n]o set of mutually inconsistent observations can exist for which some human intellect cannot conceive a coherent explanation, however complicated."[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-Woo2011-93)
Recently, American physicist Igor Mazin argued that because high-profile physics journals prefer publications offering exotic and unusual interpretations, the Occam's razor principle is being replaced by an "Inverse Occam's razor", implying that the simplest possible explanation is usually rejected.[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-94)

## Notes
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Occam%27s_razor&action=edit&section=31 "Edit section: Notes")]
  1. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_ref-87 "Jump up")** "Ockham's razor does not say that the more simple a hypothesis, the better."[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-SkepticDict2012-86)
  2. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_ref-88 "Jump up")** "Today, we think of the principle of parsimony as a heuristic device. We don't assume that the simpler theory is correct and the more complex one false. We know from experience that more often than not the theory that requires more complicated machinations is wrong. Until proved otherwise, the more complex theory competing with a simpler explanation should be put on the back burner, but not thrown onto the trash heap of history until proven false."[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-SkepticDict2012-86)
  3. **[^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_ref-89 "Jump up")** "While these two facets of simplicity are frequently conflated, it is important to treat them as distinct. One reason for doing so is that considerations of parsimony and of elegance typically pull in different directions. Postulating extra entities may allow a theory to be formulated more simply, while reducing the ontology of a theory may only be possible at the price of making it syntactically more complex."[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occam%27s_razor#cite_note-fn_\(109\)-53)
