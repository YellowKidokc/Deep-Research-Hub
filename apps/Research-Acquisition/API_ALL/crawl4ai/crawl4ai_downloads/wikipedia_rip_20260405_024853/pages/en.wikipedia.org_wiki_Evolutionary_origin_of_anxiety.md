**Anxiety** is an [emotion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotion "Emotion") characterised by an unpleasant state of inner [turmoil](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/turmoil "wikt:turmoil") and includes feelings of dread over [anticipated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticipation "Anticipation") events.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-2)[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Chand_2022-3) Anxiety is different from [fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear "Fear") in that fear is defined as the [emotional response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_response "Emotional response") to a present [threat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threat "Threat"), whereas anxiety is the anticipation of a future one.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-4) It is often accompanied by nervous behaviour such as pacing back and forth, [somatic complaints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatic_anxiety "Somatic anxiety"), and [rumination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumination_\(psychology\) "Rumination \(psychology\)").[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Seligman-5)
Anxiety is a feeling of uneasiness and [worry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worry "Worry"), usually generalised and unfocused as an [overreaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overreaction "Overreaction") to a situation that is only subjectively seen as menacing.[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-6) It is often accompanied by muscular tension,[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7) restlessness, [fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_\(medical\) "Fatigue \(medical\)"), inability to catch one's breath, tightness in the [abdominal region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdominal_region "Abdominal region"), nausea, and problems in concentration. Anxiety is closely related to fear,[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Chand_2022-3) which is a response to a real or perceived immediate threat ([fight-or-flight response](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fight-or-flight_response "Fight-or-flight response")); anxiety involves the expectation of a future threat, including dread.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7) People facing anxiety may withdraw from situations which have provoked anxiety in the past.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Barker,_P._2003-8)
The emotion appropriate time periods in response to specific events, and thus turn into one of the multiple [anxiety disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety_disorder "Anxiety disorder") (e.g., [generalised anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_anxiety_disorder "Generalized anxiety disorder"), [panic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_disorder "Panic disorder")).[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Robinson_Pike_Cornwell_Grillon_2019_pp._jnnp%E2%80%932019%E2%80%93321400-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Treating_and_Preventing_Adolescent_Mental_Health_Disorders:_What_We_Know_and_What_We_Don't_Know._A_Research_Agenda_for_Improving_the_Mental_Health_of_Our_Youth-10) The difference between _anxiety disorder_ and _anxiety_ (as a normal emotion) is that people with an anxiety disorder experience anxiety excessively or persistently for approximately 6 months, or even during shorter time periods in children.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7) Anxiety disorders are among the most persistent [mental problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_problem "Mental problem") and often last decades.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Lancet2021-11) Anxiety can also be experienced within other [mental disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder "Mental disorder") (e.g., [obsessive–compulsive disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsessive%E2%80%93compulsive_disorder "Obsessive–compulsive disorder"), [post-traumatic stress disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-traumatic_stress_disorder "Post-traumatic stress disorder")).[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Marras_et_al._\(2016\)-12)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-ICD11-13)
## Anxiety vs. fear
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Anxiety vs. fear")]
Anxiety is distinguished from [fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear "Fear"), which is an appropriate cognitive and emotional response to a perceived [threat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threat "Threat").[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-14) Fear is related to the specific behaviours of [fight-or-flight responses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fight-or-flight_response "Fight-or-flight response"), defensive behaviour or escape.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-15) There is a false presumption that often circulates that anxiety only occurs in situations perceived as uncontrollable or unavoidable, but this is not always so.[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-16) [David Barlow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_H._Barlow "David H. Barlow") defines anxiety as "a future-oriented mood state in which one is not ready or prepared to attempt to [cope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coping "Coping") with upcoming negative events,"[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-17) and that it is a distinction between future and present dangers which divides anxiety and fear. Another description of anxiety is agony, dread, terror, or even apprehension.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-18) In [positive psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_psychology "Positive psychology"), anxiety is described as the mental state that results from a difficult challenge for which the subject has insufficient [coping skills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coping_skills "Coping skills").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Chand_2022-3)[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-19)
Fear and anxiety can be differentiated into four domains: (1) duration of emotional experience, (2) [temporal focus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_fossa "Temporal fossa"), (3) specificity of the threat, and (4) motivated direction. Fear is short-lived, present-focused, geared towards a specific threat, and facilitates escape from threat. On the other hand, anxiety is long-acting, future-focused, broadly focused towards a diffuse threat, and promotes excessive caution while approaching a potential threat and interferes with constructive coping.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-20)
[Joseph E. LeDoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_E._LeDoux "Joseph E. LeDoux") and [Lisa Feldman Barrett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Feldman_Barrett "Lisa Feldman Barrett") have both sought to separate automatic threat responses from additional associated cognitive activity within anxiety.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-21)[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-22)
## Evolutionary perspectives
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Evolutionary perspectives")]
[Evolutionary psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_psychiatry "Evolutionary psychiatry") and [evolutionary psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_psychology "Evolutionary psychology") interpret anxiety as an evolved defences that helps organisms avoid potential threats; by design, such defenses can produce "false alarms" when the cost of a missed danger would be high (sometimes described as a "smoke‑detector" principle). Contemporary reviews stress that this framing does not treat anxiety disorders as adaptive, but rather as dysregulations or context‑insensitive activation of otherwise useful systems; the perspective is used for explanation and [psychoeducation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoeducation "Psychoeducation"), not as a specific therapy.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-23)
## Symptoms
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Symptoms")]
Anxiety can be experienced with long, drawn-out daily symptoms that reduce quality of life, known as chronic (or generalised) anxiety, or it can be experienced in short spurts with sporadic, stressful [panic attacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_attack "Panic attack"), known as acute anxiety.[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-24) Symptoms of anxiety can range in number, intensity, and frequency, depending on the person. However, most people do not suffer from chronic anxiety.[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-25)
Anxiety can induce several psychological pains (e.g., [depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depression_\(mood\) "Depression \(mood\)")) or [mental disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder "Mental disorder"), and may lead to [self-harm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-harm "Self-harm") or [suicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide "Suicide").[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-WHO2009-26)[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-TestaGiannuzzi2013partI-27)
The behavioural effects of anxiety may include withdrawal from situations which have provoked anxiety or negative feelings in the past.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Barker,_P._2003-8) Other effects may include changes in sleeping patterns, changes in habits, an increase or decrease in food intake, and increased motor tension (such as foot tapping).[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Barker,_P._2003-8)
The emotional effects of anxiety may include feelings of apprehension or dread, trouble concentrating, feeling tense or jumpy, anticipating the worst, irritability, restlessness, watching for signs of danger, and a feeling of emptiness.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-28) It may also include a feeling of helplessness.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-29)
The cognitive effects of anxiety may include thoughts about suspected dangers, such as an [irrational fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_fear "Irrational fear") of dying or having a [heart attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_attack "Heart attack"), when in reality, all one is experiencing is mild chest pain, for example.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-30)[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-31)
The physiological symptoms of anxiety may include:[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-WHO2009-26)[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-TestaGiannuzzi2013partI-27)
  * Neurological, as [headache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Headache "Headache"), [paresthesias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paresthesia "Paresthesia"), [fasciculations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasciculation "Fasciculation"), [vertigo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertigo "Vertigo"), or [presyncope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presyncope "Presyncope").
  * Digestive, as abdominal pain, [nausea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nausea "Nausea"), [diarrhea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diarrhea "Diarrhea"), [indigestion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indigestion "Indigestion"), [dry mouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xerostomia "Xerostomia"), or [globus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globus_sensation "Globus sensation"). Stress hormones released in an anxious state have an impact on bowel function and can manifest physical symptoms that may contribute to or exacerbate [IBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irritable_bowel_syndrome "Irritable bowel syndrome").
  * Respiratory, as [shortness of breath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortness_of_breath "Shortness of breath") or sighing breathing.
  * Cardiac, as [palpitations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palpitations "Palpitations"), [tachycardia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tachycardia "Tachycardia"), or [chest pain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_pain "Chest pain").
  * Muscular, as [fatigue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_\(medical\) "Fatigue \(medical\)"), [tremors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tremor "Tremor"), or [tetany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetany "Tetany").
  * Cutaneous, as [perspiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspiration "Perspiration"), or [itchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itch "Itch") skin.
  * Uro-genital, as [frequent urination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequent_urination "Frequent urination"), [urinary urgency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urinary_urgency "Urinary urgency"), [dyspareunia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyspareunia "Dyspareunia"), or [impotence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erectile_dysfunction "Erectile dysfunction"), [chronic pelvic pain syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronic_pelvic_pain_syndrome "Chronic pelvic pain syndrome").


## Types
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Types")]
There are various types of anxiety. [Existential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential "Existential") anxiety can occur when a person faces [angst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angst "Angst"), an [existential crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_crisis "Existential crisis"), or [nihilistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihilism "Nihilism") feelings. People can also face [mathematical anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_anxiety "Mathematical anxiety"), [somatic anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatic_anxiety "Somatic anxiety"), [stage fright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_fright "Stage fright"), or [test anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_anxiety "Test anxiety"). [Social anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_anxiety "Social anxiety") refers to a fear of rejection and negative evaluation (being judged) by other people.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7)
### Existential
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Existential")]
Further information: [Angst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angst "Angst"), [Existential crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_crisis "Existential crisis"), and [Nihilism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihilism "Nihilism")
The philosopher [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8ren_Kierkegaard "Søren Kierkegaard"), in _[The Concept of Anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Concept_of_Anxiety "The Concept of Anxiety")_ (1844), described anxiety or dread associated with the "dizziness of freedom" and suggested the possibility for positive resolution of anxiety through the self-conscious exercise of responsibility and choosing. In _Art and Artist_ (1932), the psychologist [Otto Rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Rank "Otto Rank") wrote that the [psychological trauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_trauma "Psychological trauma") of birth was the pre-eminent human symbol of existential anxiety and encompasses the creative person's simultaneous fear of – and desire for – separation, individuation, and differentiation.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:1-32)
The [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology "Theology") [Paul Tillich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tillich "Paul Tillich") characterised existential anxiety as "the state in which a [being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being "Being") is aware of its possible nonbeing", and he listed three categories for the nonbeing and resulting anxiety: [ontic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontic "Ontic") (fate and death), [moral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality "Morality") ([guilt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guilt_\(emotion\) "Guilt \(emotion\)") and condemnation), and [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") (emptiness and [meaninglessness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_\(existential\) "Meaning \(existential\)")). According to Tillich, the last of these three types of existential anxiety is predominant in modern times, while the others were predominant earlier. Tillich argues that spiritual anxiety can either be [accepted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceptance#Self-acceptance "Acceptance") as part of the [human condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_condition "Human condition") or resisted with negative consequences. In its pathological form, it may "drive the person toward the creation of certitude in systems of meaning which are supported by [tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tradition "Tradition") and [authority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authority "Authority")" even though such "undoubted certitude is not built on the rock of [reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality "Reality")".[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Tillich-33)
According to [Viktor Frankl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viktor_Frankl "Viktor Frankl"), the author of _[Man's Search for Meaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man%27s_Search_for_Meaning "Man's Search for Meaning")_ , when a person is faced with extreme mortal dangers, the most basic of all human wishes is to find a [meaning of life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_of_life "Meaning of life") to combat the "trauma of nonbeing" as death is near.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-34)
Depending on the source of the threat, [psychoanalytic theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoanalytic_theory "Psychoanalytic theory") distinguishes three types of anxiety: realistic, [neurotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurosis "Neurosis") and moral.[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-35)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=6 "Edit section: Test, performance, and competitive")]
#### Test
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: Test")]
Main articles: [Test anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_anxiety "Test anxiety") and [Mathematical anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_anxiety "Mathematical anxiety")
According to the [Yerkes-Dodson law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yerkes-Dodson_law "Yerkes-Dodson law"), an optimal level of arousal is necessary to best complete a task such as an exam, performance, or competitive event. However, when the anxiety or level of arousal exceeds that optimum, the result is a decline in performance.[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Teigen-36)
Test anxiety is the uneasiness, apprehension, or nervousness felt by students who have a fear of failing an [exam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_\(student_assessment\) "Test \(student assessment\)"). Students who have test anxiety may experience any of the following: the association of [grades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grading_in_education "Grading in education") with [personal worth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-esteem "Self-esteem"); fear of embarrassment by a teacher; fear of [alienation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_alienation "Social alienation") from parents or friends; time pressures; or feeling a loss of control. Sweating, dizziness, headaches, racing heartbeats, nausea, fidgeting, uncontrollable crying or laughing and drumming on a desk are all common.[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-37) Because test anxiety hinges on [fear of negative evaluation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear_of_negative_evaluation "Fear of negative evaluation"),[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Liebert-38) debate exists as to whether test anxiety is itself a unique anxiety disorder or whether it is a specific type of social [phobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobia "Phobia").[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Beidel-39) The DSM-IV classifies test anxiety as a type of social phobia.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Rapee-40)
Research indicates that test anxiety among U.S. high-school and college students has been rising since the late 1950s. Test anxiety remains a challenge for students, regardless of age, and has considerable physiological and psychological impacts.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-41) Management of test anxiety focuses on achieving relaxation and developing mechanisms to manage anxiety.[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Mathur-42) The routine practice of slow, Device-Guided Breathing (DGB) is a major component of behavioural treatments for anxiety conditions.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-43)
#### Performance and competitive
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=8 "Edit section: Performance and competitive")]
Main articles: [Stage fright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_fright "Stage fright"), [Somatic anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatic_anxiety "Somatic anxiety"), and [Sport psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_psychology "Sport psychology")
[Performance anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stage_fright "Stage fright") and competitive anxiety ([competitive trait anxiety, competitive state anxiety](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-803377-7.00018-1 "doi:10.1016/B978-0-12-803377-7.00018-1")) happen when an individual's performance is measured against others. An important distinction between competitive and non-competitive anxiety is that competitive anxiety makes people view their performance as a threat.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:0-44) As a result, they experience a drop in their ordinary ability, whether physical or mental, due to that perceived stress.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:12-45)
Competitive anxiety is caused by a range of internal factors, including high expectations, outside pressure,[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:12-45) lack of experience, and external factors like the location of a competition.[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-46) It commonly occurs in those participating in high-pressure activities like sports and debates. Some common symptoms of competitive anxiety include muscle tension, fatigue, weakness, a sense of panic, apprehensiveness, and panic attacks.[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-47)
There are [4 major theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_psychology#Arousal_anxiety_and_stress "Sport psychology") of how anxiety affects performance: [Drive theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drive_theory "Drive theory"), Inverted U theory, Reversal theory, and The Zone of Optimal Functioning theory. 
_Drive theory_ believes that anxiety is positive and performance improves proportionally to the level of anxiety. This theory is not well accepted.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Jarvis_2006-48)
The _Inverted U theory_ is based on the idea that performance peaks at a moderate stress level. It is called Inverted U theory because the graph that plots performance against anxiety looks like an inverted "U".[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Jarvis_2006-48)
_Reversal theory_ suggests that performance increases in relation to the individual's interpretation of their arousal levels. If they believed their physical arousal level would help them, their performance would increase, if they didn't, their performance would decrease.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:12-45) For example: Athletes were shown to worry more when focusing on results and perfection rather than the effort and growth involved.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:0-44)
The _Zone of Optimal Functioning theory_ proposes that there is a zone where positive and [negative emotions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_emotion "Negative emotion") are in a balance which lead to feelings of dissociation and intense concentration, optimizing the individual's performance levels.[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:22-49)
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=9 "Edit section: Stranger, social, and intergroup anxiety")]
Main articles: [Stranger anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stranger_anxiety "Stranger anxiety") and [Social anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_anxiety "Social anxiety")
Humans generally require social acceptance and thus sometimes dread the disapproval of others. Apprehension of being judged by others may cause anxiety in social environments.[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-50)
Anxiety during social interactions, particularly between strangers, is common among young people. It may persist into adulthood and become social anxiety or social phobia. "[Stranger anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stranger_anxiety "Stranger anxiety")" in small children is not considered a phobia. In adults, an excessive fear of other people is not a developmentally common stage; it is called [social anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_anxiety "Social anxiety"). According to Cutting, social phobics do not fear the crowd but the fact that they may be judged negatively.[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-51)
Social anxiety varies in degree and severity. For some people, it is characterized by experiencing discomfort or awkwardness during physical social contact (e.g. embracing, shaking hands, etc.), while in other cases it can lead to a fear of interacting with unfamiliar people altogether. Those with this condition may restrict their lifestyles to accommodate the anxiety, minimizing social interaction whenever possible. Social anxiety also forms a core aspect of certain personality disorders, including [avoidant personality disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avoidant_personality_disorder "Avoidant personality disorder").[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-52)
To the extent that a person is fearful of social encounters with unfamiliar others, some people may experience anxiety particularly during interactions with outgroup members, or people who share different group memberships (i.e., by race, ethnicity, class, gender, etc.). Depending on the nature of the antecedent relations, cognitions, and situational factors, intergroup contact may be stressful and lead to feelings of anxiety. This apprehension or fear of contact with outgroup members is often called interracial or intergroup anxiety.[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-53)
As is the case with the more generalized forms of [social anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_anxiety "Social anxiety"), intergroup anxiety has behavioral, cognitive, and affective effects. For instance, increases in schematic processing and simplified information processing can occur when anxiety is high. Indeed, such is consistent with related work on attentional bias in [implicit memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_memory "Implicit memory").[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-54)[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-55)[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-56) Additionally recent research has found that [implicit racial evaluations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_racism "Implicit racism") (i.e. automatic prejudiced attitudes) can be amplified during intergroup interaction.[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-57) Negative experiences have been illustrated in producing not only negative expectations, but also avoidant, or antagonistic, behavior such as hostility.[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-58) Furthermore, when compared to anxiety levels and cognitive effort (e.g., impression management and self-presentation) in intragroup contexts, levels and depletion of resources may be exacerbated in the intergroup situation. 
### Trait
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=10 "Edit section: Trait")]
Anxiety can be either a short-term "state" or a long-term "[personality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality "Personality") trait". Trait anxiety reflects a stable tendency across the lifespan of responding with acute, state anxiety in the anticipation of threatening situations (whether they are actually deemed threatening or not).[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-59) A meta-analysis showed that a high level of [neuroticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroticism "Neuroticism") is a risk factor for development of anxiety symptoms and disorders.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-NeuroticismMA-60) Such anxiety may be conscious or unconscious.[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-61)
Personality can also be a trait leading to anxiety and depression and their persistence.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Lancet2021-11) Through experience, many find it difficult to collect themselves due to their own personal nature.[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-62)
### Choice or decision
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=11 "Edit section: Choice or decision")]
Anxiety induced by the need to choose between similar options is recognized as a problem for some individuals and for organizations.[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-63) In 2004, [Capgemini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capgemini "Capgemini") wrote: "Today we're all faced with greater choice, more competition and less time to consider our options or seek out the right advice."[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-64) Overthinking a choice is called [analysis paralysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_paralysis "Analysis paralysis"). 
In a decision context, unpredictability or uncertainty may trigger emotional responses in anxious individuals that systematically alter decision-making.[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Hartley-65) There are primarily two forms of this anxiety type. The first form refers to a choice in which there are multiple potential outcomes with known or calculable probabilities. The second form refers to the uncertainty and ambiguity related to a decision context in which there are multiple possible outcomes with unknown probabilities.[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Hartley-65)
### Panic disorder
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=12 "Edit section: Panic disorder")]
Main article: [Panic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_disorder "Panic disorder")
Panic disorder may share symptoms of stress and anxiety, but it is actually very different. Panic disorder is an anxiety disorder that occurs without any triggers. According to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, this disorder can be distinguished by unexpected and repeated episodes of intense fear.[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-66) Someone with panic disorder will eventually develop constant fear of another attack and as this progresses it will begin to affect daily functioning and an individual's general quality of life. It is reported by the Cleveland Clinic that panic disorder affects 2 to 3 percent of adult Americans and can begin around the time of the teenage and early adult years. Some symptoms include: difficulty breathing, chest pain, dizziness, trembling or shaking, feeling faint, nausea, fear that you are losing control or are about to die. Even though they have these symptoms during an attack, the main symptom is the persistent fear of having future panic attacks.[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-67)
## Anxiety disorders
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=13 "Edit section: Anxiety disorders")]
Main article: [Anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety_disorder "Anxiety disorder")
Anxiety disorders are a group of [mental disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder "Mental disorder") characterized by exaggerated feelings of [anxiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety_\(mood\) "Anxiety \(mood\)") and [fear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear "Fear") responses.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7) Anxiety is a worry about future events and fear is a reaction to current events. These feelings may cause physical symptoms, such as a [fast heart rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tachycardia "Tachycardia") and shakiness. There are a number of anxiety disorders: including [generalized anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_anxiety_disorder "Generalized anxiety disorder"), [specific phobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobia "Phobia"), [social anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_anxiety_disorder "Social anxiety disorder"), [separation anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_anxiety_disorder "Separation anxiety disorder"), [agoraphobia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agoraphobia "Agoraphobia"), [panic disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_disorder "Panic disorder"), and [selective mutism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_mutism "Selective mutism"). The disorder differs by what results in the symptoms. People often have more than one anxiety disorder.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7)
Anxiety disorders are caused by a complex combination of [genetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heredity "Heredity") and environmental factors.[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-68)[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Genetics_of_generalized_anxiety_dis-69) To be diagnosed, symptoms typically need to be present for at least six months, be more than would be expected for the situation, and decrease a person's ability to function in their daily lives.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7)[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-70) Other problems that may result in similar symptoms include [hyperthyroidism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperthyroidism "Hyperthyroidism"), [heart disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_disease "Heart disease"), [caffeine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caffeine "Caffeine"), [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\) "Alcohol \(drug\)"), or [cannabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_\(drug\) "Cannabis \(drug\)") use, and withdrawal from certain drugs, among others.[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-71)
Without treatment, anxiety disorders tend to remain.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7)[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-NIH2016-72) Treatment may include lifestyle changes, [counselling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychotherapy "Psychotherapy"), and medications. Counselling is typically with a type of [cognitive behavioral therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_behavioral_therapy "Cognitive behavioral therapy").[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Lancet2016-73) Medications, such as [antidepressants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antidepressants "Antidepressants") or [beta blockers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_blockers "Beta blockers"), may improve symptoms.[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-NIH2016-72) A 2023 review found that regular physical activity is effective for reducing anxiety.[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-74)
About 12% of people are affected by an anxiety disorder in a given year and between 12% and 30% are affected at some point in their life.[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Lancet2016-73)[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Kess2007-75) They occur about twice as often in women than they do in men, and generally begin before the age of 25.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7)[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Lancet2016-73) The most common anxiety disorders are specific phobias, which affect nearly 12% of people, and social anxiety disorder, which affects 10% of people at some point in their life. They affect those between the ages of 15 and 35 the most and become less common after the age of 55. Rates appear to be higher in the United States and Europe.[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Lancet2016-73)
### Short- and long-term anxiety
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=14 "Edit section: Short- and long-term anxiety")]
Anxiety can be either a short-term "state" or a long-term "[trait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trait_theory "Trait theory")". Whereas trait anxiety represents worrying about future events, anxiety disorders are a group of [mental disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_disorder "Mental disorder") characterized by feelings of anxiety and fears.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-DSM-5_189-7)
### Four ways to be anxious
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=15 "Edit section: Four ways to be anxious")]
In his book _Anxious: The Modern Mind in the Age of Anxiety_[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-76) [Joseph LeDoux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_E._LeDoux "Joseph E. LeDoux") examines four experiences of anxiety through a brain-based lens: 
  1. In the presence of an existing or imminent external threat, you worry about the event and its implications for your physical and/or psychological well-being. When a threat signal occurs, it signifies either that danger is present or near in space and time or that it might be coming in the future. Nonconscious threats processing by the brain activates defensive survival circuits, resulting in changes in information processing in the brain, controlled in part by increases in arousal and behavioral and physiological responses in the body that then produce signals that feed back to the brain and complement the physiological changes there, intensifying them and extending their duration.
  2. When you notice body sensations, you worry about what they might mean for your physical and/or psychological well-being. The trigger stimulus does not have to be an external stimulus but can be an internal one, as some people are particularly sensitive to body signals.
  3. Thoughts and memories may lead to you to worry about your physical and/or [psychological well-being](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six-factor_model_of_psychological_well-being "Six-factor model of psychological well-being"). We do not need to be in the presence of an external or internal stimulus to be anxious. An episodic memory of a past trauma or of a panic attack in the past is sufficient to activate the defence circuits.
  4. Thoughts and memories may result in existential dread, such as worry about leading a meaningful life or the eventuality of death. Examples are contemplations of whether one's life has been meaningful, the inevitability of death, or the difficulty of making decisions that have a [moral value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_theory "Value theory"). These do not necessarily activate defensive systems; they are more or less pure forms of cognitive anxiety.


## Co-morbidity
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=16 "Edit section: Co-morbidity")]
Anxiety disorders often occur with other mental health disorders, particularly [major depressive disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_depressive_disorder "Major depressive disorder"), [bipolar disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipolar_disorder "Bipolar disorder"), [eating disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eating_disorder "Eating disorder"), or certain [personality disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personality_disorder "Personality disorder"). It also commonly occurs with personality traits such as neuroticism. This observed co-occurrence is partly due to genetic and environmental influences shared between these traits and anxiety.[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-77)[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-78)
It is common for those with [obsessive–compulsive disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsessive%E2%80%93compulsive_disorder "Obsessive–compulsive disorder") to experience anxiety. Anxiety is also commonly found in those who experience [panic disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panic_disorders "Panic disorders"), [phobic anxiety disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobic_anxiety_disorders "Phobic anxiety disorders"), severe [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_stress "Psychological stress"), [dissociative disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociative_disorders "Dissociative disorders"), [somatoform disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatoform_disorders "Somatoform disorders"), and some [neurotic disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotic_disorders "Neurotic disorders").[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-79) Anxiety has also been linked to the experience of [intrusive thoughts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intrusive_thought "Intrusive thought"). Studies have revealed that individuals who experience high levels of anxiety (also known as clinical anxiety) are highly vulnerable to the experience of intense intrusive thoughts or psychological disorders that are characterized by intrusive thoughts.[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-80)
## Risk factors
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=17 "Edit section: Risk factors")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Emperor_Traianus_Decius_%28Mary_Harrsch%29.jpg/250px-Emperor_Traianus_Decius_%28Mary_Harrsch%29.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Emperor_Traianus_Decius_\(Mary_Harrsch\).jpg)A marble bust of the Roman Emperor [Decius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decius "Decius") from the [Capitoline Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitoline_Museum "Capitoline Museum"), conveying "an impression of anxiety and weariness, as of a man shouldering heavy [state] responsibilities"[[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-81)
Anxiety disorders are partly genetic, with twin studies suggesting 30–40% genetic influence on individual differences in anxiety.[[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-82) Environmental factors are also important. Twin studies show that individual-specific environments have a large influence on anxiety, whereas shared environmental influences (environments that affect twins in the same way) operate during childhood but decline through adolescence.[[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-83) Specific measured 'environments' that have been associated with anxiety include [child abuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_abuse "Child abuse"), family history of mental health disorders, and [poverty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty "Poverty").[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-84) Anxiety is also associated with [drug use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mood_disorder#Substance-induced "Mood disorder"), including [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\) "Alcohol \(drug\)") and [caffeine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caffeine "Caffeine"), as well as [benzodiazepines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzodiazepine "Benzodiazepine"), which are often prescribed to treat anxiety. 
### Genetics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=18 "Edit section: Genetics")]
Genetics and family history (e.g. parental anxiety) may put an individual at increased risk of an anxiety disorder, but generally external stimuli will trigger its onset or exacerbation.[[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-85) Estimates of genetic influence on anxiety, based on studies of twins, range from 25 to 40% depending on the specific type and age-group under study. For example, genetic differences account for about 43% of variance in panic disorder and 28% in generalized anxiety disorder.[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Genetics_of_generalized_anxiety_dis-69) Longitudinal twin studies have shown the moderate stability of anxiety from childhood through to adulthood is mainly influenced by stability in genetic influence.[[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-86)[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-87) When investigating how anxiety is passed on from parents to children, it is important to account for sharing of genes as well as environments, for example using the intergenerational children-of-twins design.[[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-88)
Many studies in the past used a candidate gene approach to test whether single genes were associated with anxiety. These investigations were based on hypotheses about how certain known genes influence neurotransmitters (such as serotonin and norepinephrine) and hormones (such as cortisol) that are implicated in anxiety. None of these findings are well replicated,[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:2-89) with the possible exception of TMEM132D, COMT and MAO-A.[[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-:2-89) The epigenetic signature of _[BDNF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BDNF "BDNF")_ , a gene that codes for a protein called _brain derived neurotrophic factor_ that is found in the brain, has also been associated with anxiety and specific patterns of neural activity.[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Purves-90) and a receptor gene for _BDNF_ called _NTRK2_ was associated with anxiety in a large genome-wide investigation.[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Purves-90) The reason that most candidate gene findings have not replicated is that anxiety is a complex trait that is influenced by many genomic variants, each of which has a small effect on its own. Increasingly, studies of anxiety are using a hypothesis-free approach to look for parts of the genome that are implicated in anxiety using big enough samples to find associations with variants that have small effects. The largest explorations of the common genetic architecture of anxiety have been facilitated by the UK Biobank, the ANGST consortium and the CRC Fear, Anxiety and Anxiety Disorders.[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Purves-90)[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-91)[[92]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-92)
#### Epigenetics
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=19 "Edit section: Epigenetics")]
This section is an excerpt from [Epigenetics of anxiety and stress–related disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigenetics_of_anxiety_and_stress%E2%80%93related_disorders "Epigenetics of anxiety and stress–related disorders").[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Epigenetics_of_anxiety_and_stress%E2%80%93related_disorders&action=edit)]
[Epigenetics of anxiety and stress–related disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigenetics_of_anxiety_and_stress%E2%80%93related_disorders "Epigenetics of anxiety and stress–related disorders") is the field studying the relationship between [epigenetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigenetic "Epigenetic") modifications of genes and anxiety and [stress-related disorders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress-related_disorders "Stress-related disorders"), including mental health disorders such as [generalized anxiety disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_anxiety_disorder "Generalized anxiety disorder") (GAD), [post-traumatic stress disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posttraumatic_stress_disorder "Posttraumatic stress disorder"), [obsessive-compulsive disorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsessive%E2%80%93compulsive_disorder "Obsessive–compulsive disorder") (OCD), and more. These changes can lead to transgenerational stress inheritance.[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Epigenetics_of_anxiety_and_stress%E2%80%93related_disorders_MatthewsPhillipsEN12-93)
Epigenetic modifications play a role in the development and heritability of these disorders and related symptoms. For example, regulation of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis by [glucocorticoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glucocorticoid "Glucocorticoid") plays a major role in stress response and is known to be epigenetically regulated. 
As of 2015 most work has been done in [animal models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_models "Animal models") in laboratories, and little work has been done in humans; the work is not yet applicable to clinical [psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatry "Psychiatry").[[94]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Epigenetics_of_anxiety_and_stress%E2%80%93related_disorders_Klengel_2015-94) Stress-induced epigenetic changes, particularly to genes that effect the [hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothalamic%E2%80%93pituitary%E2%80%93adrenal_axis "Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis") (HPA) axis, persist into future generations, negatively impacting the capacity of offspring to adapt to stress. Early life experiences, even when generations removed, can cause permanent epigenetic modifications of DNA resulting in changes in [gene expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_expression "Gene expression"), [endocrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endocrine "Endocrine") function and [metabolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism "Metabolism").[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Epigenetics_of_anxiety_and_stress%E2%80%93related_disorders_MatthewsPhillipsEN12-93) These [heritable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heritable "Heritable") epigenetic modifications include [DNA methylation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_methylation "DNA methylation") of the [promoter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promoter_\(genetics\) "Promoter \(genetics\)") regions of genes that affect sensitivity to stress. 
### Medical conditions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=20 "Edit section: Medical conditions")]
Many medical conditions can cause anxiety. This includes conditions that affect the ability to breathe, like [COPD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COPD "COPD") and [asthma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asthma "Asthma"), and the difficulty in breathing that often occurs near death.[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-95)[[96]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-96)[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-TselebisPachi2016-97) Conditions that cause abdominal pain or chest pain can cause anxiety and may in some cases be a [somatization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Somatization "Somatization") of anxiety;[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-98)[[99]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-99) the same is true for some sexual dysfunctions.[[100]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-100)[[101]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-101) Conditions that affect the face or the skin can cause social anxiety especially among adolescents,[[102]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-102) and developmental disabilities often lead to social anxiety for children as well.[[103]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-103) Life-threatening conditions like cancer also cause anxiety.[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-104)

### Substance-induced
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=21 "Edit section: Substance-induced")]
Several drugs can cause or worsen anxiety, whether in intoxication, withdrawal or as side effect. These include [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcohol_\(drug\) "Alcohol \(drug\)"), [tobacco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tobacco "Tobacco"), [sedatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedative "Sedative") (including prescription [benzodiazepines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzodiazepine "Benzodiazepine")), [opioids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opioid "Opioid") (including prescription pain killers and illicit drugs like heroin), [stimulants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimulant "Stimulant") (such as [caffeine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caffeine "Caffeine"), [cocaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocaine "Cocaine") and amphetamines), [hallucinogens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hallucinogen "Hallucinogen"), and [inhalants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inhalants "Inhalants").[[115]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-115)
While many often report self-medicating anxiety with these substances, improvements in anxiety from drugs are usually short-lived (with worsening of anxiety in the long term, sometimes with acute anxiety as soon as the drug effects wear off) and tend to be exaggerated.[[116]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-116)[[117]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-117) Acute exposure to toxic levels of [benzene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzene "Benzene") may cause euphoria, anxiety, and irritability lasting up to 2 weeks after the exposure.[[118]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-118)
### Psychological
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=22 "Edit section: Psychological")]
![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/Challenge_vs_skill.svg/330px-Challenge_vs_skill.svg.png)[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Challenge_vs_skill.svg)Mental state in terms of challenge level and skill level, according to [Csikszentmihalyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihaly_Csikszentmihalyi "Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi")'s [flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_\(psychology\) "Flow \(psychology\)") model.[[119]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Finding_Flow-119) (Click on a fragment of the image to go to the appropriate article)
Poor [coping skills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coping_\(psychology\) "Coping \(psychology\)") (e.g., rigidity/inflexible problem solving, denial, avoidance, impulsivity, extreme self-expectation, negative thoughts, affective instability, and inability to focus on problems) are associated with anxiety. Anxiety is also linked and perpetuated by the person's own pessimistic outcome expectancy and how they cope with feedback negativity.[[120]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-120)[[121]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-121) Temperament (e.g., [neuroticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuroticism "Neuroticism"))[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-NeuroticismMA-60) and attitudes (e.g. pessimism) have been found to be risk factors for anxiety.[[122]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Bienvenu_Ginsburg_2007-122)
[Cognitive distortions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_distortion "Cognitive distortion") such as overgeneralizing, catastrophizing, mind reading, [emotional reasoning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_reasoning "Emotional reasoning"), binocular trick, and mental filter can result in anxiety. For example, an overgeneralized belief that something bad "always" happens may lead someone to have excessive fears of even minimally risky situations and to avoid benign social situations due to anticipatory anxiety of [embarrassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embarrassment "Embarrassment"). In addition, those who have high anxiety can also create future stressful life events.[[123]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-123) Together, these findings suggest that anxious thoughts can lead to anticipatory anxiety as well as stressful events, which in turn cause more anxiety. Such unhealthy thoughts can be targets for successful treatment with [cognitive therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_therapy "Cognitive therapy"). 
[Psychodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychodynamics "Psychodynamics") theory posits that anxiety is often the result of opposing [unconscious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconscious_mind "Unconscious mind") wishes or fears that manifest via maladaptive [defense mechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defence_mechanisms "Defence mechanisms") (such as suppression, repression, anticipation, regression, somatization, passive aggression, dissociation) that develop to [adapt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ego_psychology "Ego psychology") to problems with early [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_relations_theory "Object relations theory") (e.g., caregivers) and [empathic failures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self_psychology "Self psychology") in childhood. For example, persistent parental discouragement of anger may result in repression/suppression of angry feelings which manifests as gastrointestinal distress (somatization) when provoked by another while the anger remains unconscious and outside the individual's awareness. Such conflicts can be targets for successful treatment with [psychodynamic therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychodynamic_psychotherapy "Psychodynamic psychotherapy"). While psychodynamic therapy tends to explore the underlying roots of anxiety, cognitive behavioral therapy has also been shown to be a successful treatment for anxiety by altering irrational thoughts and unwanted behaviors. 
#### Evolutionary psychology
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=23 "Edit section: Evolutionary psychology")]
An [evolutionary psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_psychology "Evolutionary psychology") explanation is that increased anxiety serves the purpose of increased [vigilance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vigilance_\(psychology\) "Vigilance \(psychology\)") regarding potential threats in the environment as well as increased tendency to take proactive actions regarding such possible threats. This may cause [false positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_positive "False positive") reactions but an individual with anxiety may also avoid real threats. This may explain why anxious people are less likely to die due to accidents.[[124]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-124) There is ample empirical evidence that anxiety can have adaptive value. Within a school, timid fish are more likely than bold fish to survive a predator.[[125]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-125)
When people are confronted with unpleasant and potentially harmful stimuli such as foul odors or tastes, [PET-scans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron_emission_tomography "Positron emission tomography") show increased blood flow in the [amygdala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amygdala "Amygdala").[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-126)[[127]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-127) In these studies, the participants also reported moderate anxiety. This might indicate that anxiety is a protective mechanism designed to prevent the organism from engaging in potentially harmful behaviors. 
### Social
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=24 "Edit section: Social")]
Social risk factors for anxiety include a history of trauma (e.g., physical, sexual or emotional abuse or assault), bullying, early life experiences and parenting factors (e.g., rejection, lack of warmth, high hostility, harsh discipline, high parental [negative affect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_affectivity "Negative affectivity"), anxious childrearing, modelling of dysfunctional and drug-abusing behaviour, discouragement of emotions, poor socialization, poor attachment, and child abuse and neglect), cultural factors (e.g., higher rates of anxiety in collectivistic cultures[[128]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-128)[[129]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-129) and persecuted minorities including those with disabilities), and [socioeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socioeconomic_status_and_mental_health "Socioeconomic status and mental health") (e.g., uneducated, unemployed, impoverished although developed countries have higher rates of anxiety disorders than developing countries).[[130]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-130) A 2019 comprehensive systematic review of over 50 studies showed that food insecurity in the United States is strongly associated with depression, anxiety, and sleep disorders.[[131]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-131) Food-insecure individuals had an almost 3 fold risk increase of testing positive for anxiety when compared to food-secure individuals. 
#### Gender socialization
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=25 "Edit section: Gender socialization")]
Contextual factors that are thought to contribute to anxiety include [gender socialization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialization#Gender_socialization "Socialization") and learning experiences. In particular, learning mastery (the degree to which people perceive their lives to be under their own control) and instrumentality, which includes such traits as self-confidence, [self-efficacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-efficacy "Self-efficacy"), independence, and competitiveness fully mediate the relation between gender and anxiety. That is, though gender differences in anxiety exist, with higher levels of anxiety in women compared to men, gender socialization and learning mastery explain these gender differences.[[132]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-132)
## Treatment
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=26 "Edit section: Treatment")]
See also: [Fear § Management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fear#Management "Fear")
The first step in the management of a person with anxiety symptoms involves evaluating the possible presence of an underlying medical cause, the recognition of which is essential in order to decide the correct treatment.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-WHO2009-26)[[105]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-TestaGiannuzzi2013partIII-105) Anxiety symptoms may mask an [organic disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disease "Disease"), or appear associated with or as a result of a medical disorder.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-WHO2009-26)[[105]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-TestaGiannuzzi2013partIII-105)[[133]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-TestaGiannuzzi2013partII-133)[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-TestaGiannuzzi2013partI-27)
[Cognitive behavioral therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_behavioral_therapy "Cognitive behavioral therapy") (CBT) is effective for anxiety disorders and is a first line treatment.[[134]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-NEJM2015-134)[[135]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-135)[[136]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-136)[[137]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-137)[[138]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Ol2016-138) CBT appears to be equally effective when carried out via the internet.[[138]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Ol2016-138) While evidence for mental health apps is promising, it is preliminary.[[139]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-139)[[140]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-140)
Anxiety often affects relationships, and [interpersonal psychotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpersonal_psychotherapy "Interpersonal psychotherapy") addresses these issues by improving communication and relationship skills.[[141]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-141)
Psychopharmacological treatment can be used in parallel to CBT or can be used alone. As a general rule, most anxiety disorders respond well to first-line agents. Such drugs, also used as [antidepressants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antidepressant "Antidepressant"), are the [selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_serotonin_reuptake_inhibitor "Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor") (SSRIs) and [serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotonin%E2%80%93norepinephrine_reuptake_inhibitor "Serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor") (SNRIs), that work by [blocking the reuptake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuptake_inhibitor "Reuptake inhibitor") of specific [neurotransmitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurotransmitter "Neurotransmitter") and resulting in the increase in availability of these neurotransmitters. Additionally, [benzodiazepines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benzodiazepine "Benzodiazepine") are often prescribed to individuals with anxiety disorder. Benzodiazepines produce an [anxiolytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiolytic "Anxiolytic") response by modulating GABA and increasing its receptor binding. A third common treatment involves a category of drug known as [serotonin agonists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serotonin_receptor_agonist "Serotonin receptor agonist"). This category of drug works by initiating a physiological response at [5-HT1A receptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5-HT1A_receptor "5-HT1A receptor") by increasing the action of serotonin at this receptor.[_[citation needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed "Wikipedia:Citation needed")_] Other treatment options include [pregabalin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pregabalin "Pregabalin"), [tricyclic antidepressants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricyclic_antidepressant "Tricyclic antidepressant"), and [moclobemide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moclobemide "Moclobemide"), among others.[[142]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Bandelow_2017-142)
Anxiety is considered to be a serious psychiatric illness that has an unknown true pervasiveness due to affected individuals not asking for proper treatment or aid, and due to professionals missing the diagnosis.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-Chand_2022-3)
## Prevention
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Anxiety&action=edit&section=27 "Edit section: Prevention")]
The above risk factors give natural avenues for prevention. Psychological or educational interventions have a small yet statistically significant benefit for the prevention of anxiety in varied population types.[[143]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-143)[[144]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-144) Improvement in dietary intake and habits may also help lower the risk of anxiety.[[145]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-145)[[146]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-146)[[147]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anxiety#cite_note-147)
