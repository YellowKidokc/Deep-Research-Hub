# Essay 1: OF 10

Resolves 235 of 642 contradictions (36.6%)

## The Skeptic's Toolkit: When Bad Logic Masquerades as Biblical Contradiction

Why 36.6% of alleged contradictions are the critic's own reasoning errors
———

## The Largest Category You've Never Heard Of

Of the 642 alleged contradictions catalogued between the Skeptic's Annotated Bible and various critical compilations, 235 — over a third — are not contradictions in the text at all. They are logical fallacies committed by the person reading the text. This is the single most important finding in this analysis: the largest source of 'biblical contradictions' is not the Bible. It is the critic.
This essay does not address whether the Bible is true. It addresses whether the critic's arguments are valid. These are different questions, and confusing them is itself a logical error. A poorly constructed argument against a proposition does not make the proposition true — but it does make the argument worthless. And 235 of these arguments are worthless.

## The False Dilemma Factory

The most common logical error in this category is the false dilemma: presenting two statements as contradictory when they are not mutually exclusive. Consider the question: 'Was salvation by faith or by works?' The critic presents James 2:24 ('a person is justified by works and not by faith alone') against Romans 3:28 ('a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law') as if these are irreconcilable.
But James and Paul are answering different questions. Paul addresses how a person stands before God — the ground of justification. James addresses how genuine faith manifests — the evidence of justification. Paul says you cannot earn your way in. James says if you're really in, it will show. These are not competing claims about the same thing. They are complementary claims about different aspects of the same reality. The critic manufactures a contradiction by stripping the questions these authors are answering.
This pattern repeats across dozens of entries. 'Is God merciful or wrathful?' Both — to different people in different circumstances, exactly as any just judge would be. 'Does God want people in hell?' God desires repentance (2 Peter 3:9) AND has prepared judgment for the unrepentant (Matthew 25:41) — these are not contradictory unless you believe mercy requires the abolition of justice. 'Is Jesus peaceful or divisive?' He brings peace with God and division among people who disagree about him — which is precisely what every significant truth-claim in history has done.

## The Argument from Silence

The second most prevalent fallacy is the argument from silence: concluding that because one account omits a detail, it contradicts an account that includes it. If Matthew mentions one angel at the tomb and John mentions two, the critic declares contradiction. But Matthew never says 'only one.' Mentioning one does not deny the existence of another. If I say 'I talked to John at the party' and you say 'I talked to John and Sarah at the party,' we have not contradicted each other. I simply mentioned fewer people.
This fallacy accounts for a staggering number of entries in the contradiction databases. Different gospel writers, writing for different audiences with different purposes, select different details from the same events. This is what historians expect from independent witnesses. If four witnesses gave identical testimony in court, the judge would suspect collusion, not be impressed by consistency. The gospels exhibit precisely the pattern of agreement-with-variation that indicates authentic independent testimony.

## Sequential Events Read as Simultaneous

A third major pattern: the critic reads sequential events as if they must be simultaneous. 'Did both thieves revile Jesus, or only one?' Both initially mocked him. One repented during the hours-long crucifixion. Luke records the development; Matthew records the initial state. People change their minds. This is not contradiction — it is narrative.
'Were the disciples frightened or glad when they saw the risen Jesus?' Both — first frightened, then glad, exactly as anyone would be upon encountering someone they saw executed three days prior. The critic snapshots two moments in an emotional progression and presents them as if the text claims both states existed simultaneously. It does not.
How Judas died provides another example: Matthew says he hanged himself; Acts says he fell and burst open. The critic sees contradiction. The historian sees sequence: Judas hanged himself, the rope or branch broke (or his body was cut down after decomposition), and the fall ruptured his remains. This is not creative harmonization — it is the obvious sequential reading that any coroner would consider before declaring two cause-of-death reports contradictory.

## Category Confusion

Many entries confuse different senses of the same word. 'Has there ever been a righteous person?' Romans 3:10 says no one is righteous — in the absolute sense of meriting salvation before a holy God. Yet the Old Testament calls Noah, Job, and Abraham righteous — in the relative sense of covenant faithfulness. These are not competing claims. They operate at different levels of analysis, exactly as 'no one is perfect' and 'she's a perfect employee' use 'perfect' in different registers without contradiction.
Similarly, 'God cannot be tempted' (James 1:13) and accounts of people testing God are not contradictory. The Greek 'apeirastos' means God cannot be enticed by evil — His nature is impervious to moral corruption. Humans can attempt to test God (and are warned not to), but the attempt does not create genuine temptation in God. The difference is between 'cannot be affected by' and 'is not subjected to attempts by.'

## The Dual Agency Problem

A significant subset involves dual agency: 'Who hardened Pharaoh's heart — God or Pharaoh?' The text says both, and the sequence matters. Pharaoh hardened his own heart first (Exodus 7:13, 7:22, 8:15, 8:19, 8:32, 9:7). Only after repeated self-hardening does the text say God hardened Pharaoh's heart (Exodus 9:12). The theological framework is clear: God confirms and solidifies the direction a person has already chosen. This is judgment, not arbitrary manipulation.
'Who tempted David to take the census — God or Satan?' Second Samuel 24:1 says the LORD moved David; First Chronicles 21:1 says Satan incited him. Both are true at different levels of causation. God permitted it as judgment on Israel; Satan was the proximate agent. This is the same framework found throughout Scripture and, for that matter, throughout any sophisticated theology of divine sovereignty and secondary causation.

## The Methodological Conclusion

Two hundred thirty-five alleged contradictions — 36.6% of the total — dissolve not through clever theology but through basic logic. The critic commits false dilemmas, arguments from silence, temporal confusion, category errors, and failures to distinguish levels of causation. These are not obscure fallacies. They are the kind of reasoning errors that would be flagged in an undergraduate logic course.
This does not prove the Bible is true. It proves that more than a third of the case against it is built on faulty reasoning. Any honest skeptic should want these removed from the database — not because they threaten skepticism, but because they embarrass it.
