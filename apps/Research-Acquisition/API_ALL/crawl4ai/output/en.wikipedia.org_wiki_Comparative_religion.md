**Comparative religion** is the branch of [religious studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_studies "Religious studies") that systematically compares the [doctrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrines "Doctrines"), practices, themes and impacts (including [migration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_migration "Human migration")) of the world's [religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion "Religion"). In general, the comparative study of religion yields a deeper understanding of the fundamental philosophical concerns of religion such as [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics "Ethics"), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics "Metaphysics") and the nature and forms of [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation "Salvation"). It also considers and compares the origins and [similarities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_\(philosophy\) "Similarity \(philosophy\)") shared between the various religions of the world. Studying such material facilitates a broadened and more sophisticated understanding of human [beliefs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beliefs "Beliefs") and practices regarding the [sacred](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred "Sacred"), [numinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numinous "Numinous"), [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality "Spirituality") and [divine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity "Divinity").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-1)
In the field of comparative religion, a common geographical classification[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-EB-2) of the [main world religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups "Major religious groups") distinguishes groups such as [Middle Eastern religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Middle_East "Religion in the Middle East") (including [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions "Abrahamic religions") and [Iranian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_religions "Iranian religions")), [Indian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_religions "Indian religions"), [East Asian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_religions "East Asian religions"), [African religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_religions "African religions"), [American religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_American_religion "Native American religion"), [Oceanic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Oceania "Religion in Oceania"), and [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity "Classical antiquity") [Hellenistic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_religion "Hellenistic religion").[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-EB-2)
There also exist various [sociological classifications of religious movements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociological_classifications_of_religious_movements "Sociological classifications of religious movements"). 
## History
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Comparative_religion&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: History")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b0/Monumento_a_Ibn_Hazm.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Monumento_a_Ibn_Hazm.jpg)A statue of [Ibn Hazm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Hazm "Ibn Hazm"), father of modern comparative religious studies, in Córdoba [Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spain "Spain").
[Al-Biruni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Biruni "Al-Biruni") (973 – c. 1050) and [Ibn Hazm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Hazm "Ibn Hazm") (994 – 1064), both of the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age") and considered the "fathers of comparative religion", compared the study of religious pluralism, and their works have been significant in the fields of theology and philosophy.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-Arberry-3)[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-brill-4)[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-gulf-5)[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-hadithsunnah-6) Al-Biruni discussed his idea of history in _[The Remaining Signs of Past Centuries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Remaining_Signs_of_Past_Centuries "The Remaining Signs of Past Centuries")_ (c. 1000 AD) which was translated by [Eduard Sachau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Sachau "Eduard Sachau") in the 19th century. It is a comparative study of calendars of different cultures and civilizations, interlaced with mathematical, astronomical, and historical information, exploring the customs and religions of different peoples.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-Khan-7)
In the seventeenth century, antiquarians such as [Athanasius Kircher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athanasius_Kircher "Athanasius Kircher") along with [Sir Thomas Browne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Thomas_Browne "Sir Thomas Browne") were pioneering scholars of comparative religion. In 1655, [Dara Shikoh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dara_Shikoh "Dara Shikoh") authored the [Majma-ul-Bahrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majma-ul-Bahrain "Majma-ul-Bahrain") a [Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism "Sufism"), text on comparative religion.[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-8)
Social scientists in the 19th century took a strong interest in comparative and "primitive" religion through the work of [Max Müller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_M%C3%BCller "Max Müller"), [Edward Burnett Tylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Burnett_Tylor "Edward Burnett Tylor"), [William Robertson Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Robertson_Smith "William Robertson Smith"), [James George Frazer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_George_Frazer "James George Frazer"), [Émile Durkheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89mile_Durkheim "Émile Durkheim"), [Max Weber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Weber "Max Weber"), and [Rudolf Otto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Otto "Rudolf Otto").[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-10) [Nicholas de Lange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicholas_de_Lange "Nicholas de Lange"), Professor of Hebrew and Jewish Studies at [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University "Cambridge University"), says that 
> The comparative study of religions is an academic discipline which has been developed within Christian theology faculties, and it has a tendency to force widely differing phenomena into a kind of strait-jacket cut to a Christian pattern. The problem is not only that other 'religions' may have little or nothing to say about questions which are of burning importance for Christianity, but that they may not even see themselves as religions in precisely the same way in which Christianity sees itself as a religion.[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-11)
Examples that demonstrate this point are Buddhism and Chinese Folk Religions. These belief systems have not historically been seen as mutually exclusive and have blended over time into different beliefs such as [Pure Land Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_Land_Buddhism "Pure Land Buddhism"). This shows a marked difference from Western conceptions of religions, which see adherence to one religion as precluding membership of another faith.[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-12)
Hinduism and Buddhism provide another insight in the form of [soteriology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soteriology "Soteriology"). Comparative study of religions may approach religions with a base idea of salvation with eternal life after death, but religions like Hinduism or Buddhism don't necessarily share this view. Instead, Hinduism and [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada "Theravada") Buddhism both speak of a falling back into nonexistence and escaping the cycle of [reincarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reincarnation "Reincarnation"), rather than eternal life after death.[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:3-13)[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-14)
## Geographical classification
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Comparative_religion&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: Geographical classification")]
According to [Charles Joseph Adams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Adams "Charles Joseph Adams"), in the field of comparative religion, a common geographical classification discerns[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-EB-2) the [main world religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_religious_groups "Major religious groups") as follows:[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-EB-2)
  1. Middle Eastern religions, including [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"), [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam"), [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism"), [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism"), and a variety of ancient belief systems;
  2. East Asian religions, the religious communities of China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam, and consisting of [Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucianism "Confucianism"), [Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism "Taoism"), the various schools of [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana "Mahayana") ("Greater Vehicle") Buddhism, and [Shintō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shintoism "Shintoism");
  3. Indian religions, including early [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism"), [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism "Hinduism"), [Jainism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism "Jainism"), [Sikhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sikhism "Sikhism"), and sometimes also the [Theravada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theravada "Theravada") ("Way of the Elders") Buddhism and the Hindu- and Buddhist-inspired religions of South and Southeast Asia;
  4. African religions, the ancient belief systems of the various indigenous peoples of Africa, excluding [ancient Egyptian religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_religion "Ancient Egyptian religion"), which is considered to belong to the ancient Middle East;
  5. American religions, the beliefs and practices of the various Indigenous peoples of the two American continents;
  6. Oceanic religions, the religious systems of the peoples of the Pacific islands, Australia, and New Zealand; and
  7. Religions of ancient Greece and Rome and their Hellenistic descendants.


### Middle Eastern religions

#### Abrahamic or Western Asian religions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Comparative_religion&action=edit&section=4 "Edit section: Abrahamic or Western Asian religions")]
Main articles: [Abrahamic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religion "Abrahamic religion"), [Western religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_religion "Western religion"), and [Islamic religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_religion "Islamic religion")
In the study of comparative religion, the category of [Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religion "Abrahamic religion") includes the three [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheistic "Monotheistic") religions, [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity "Christianity"), [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam "Islam") and [Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judaism "Judaism"), which claim [Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham "Abraham") (Hebrew _Avraham_ אַבְרָהָם; Arabic _Ibrahim_ إبراهيم ) as a part of their sacred history. Smaller religions such as the [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith") that fit this description are sometimes included but are often omitted.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-15)
The original belief in the [God of Abraham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Abrahamic_religions "God in Abrahamic religions") eventually became strictly monotheistic present-day [Rabbinic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbinic_Judaism "Rabbinic Judaism"). Religious Jews regard Judaism as the expression of the [covenant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic_covenant "Mosaic covenant") that [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Judaism "God in Judaism") established with the [Children of Israel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Israelites "Israelites"). Jews hold that the [Torah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torah "Torah") forms part of the larger text known as the [Tanakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh "Tanakh") or the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible"). They also look to a supplemental oral tradition represented by later texts such as the [Midrash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Midrash "Midrash") and the [Talmud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talmud "Talmud").[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-16)
Christians believe that Christianity represents the [fulfillment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supersessionism "Supersessionism") and continuation of the Jewish [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tanakh "Tanakh"). Christians believe that [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus") (Hebrew _[Yeshua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yeshua "Yeshua")_ יֵשׁוּעַ) is the awaited [Messiah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Messiah "Messiah") (Christ) foretold in the [Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament "Old Testament") [prophecies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophecy "Prophecy"), and also honor the subsequently produced [New Testament scriptures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament "New Testament").[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-17) Generally, Christians believe that Jesus is both the [incarnation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation_\(Christianity\) "Incarnation \(Christianity\)") of God and the [Son of God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_of_God "Son of God"). Their [creeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creed "Creed") generally hold in common that the incarnation, [ministry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Jesus "Ministry of Jesus"), [suffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_of_Jesus "Passion of Jesus"), [death on the cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_of_Jesus "Crucifixion of Jesus"), and [resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection_of_Jesus "Resurrection of Jesus") of Jesus took place for the [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation_in_Christianity "Salvation in Christianity") of mankind.[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-18)
Islam teaches that the present Christian and Jewish scriptures have been [corrupted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tahrif "Tahrif") over time and are no longer the original divine revelations as given to the [Jewish people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revelation#Mass_revelation "Revelation") and to [Moses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic_authorship "Mosaic authorship"), [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauline_Christianity "Pauline Christianity"), and other prophets. For Muslims, the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran") is the final, complete revelation from [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God "God") (Arabic الله _[Allah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allah "Allah")_); they believe it to have been [revealed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_revelation "Divine revelation") to [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad "Muhammad") alone, whom they characterise as the [final prophet of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prophet_of_Islam "Prophet of Islam") and as the _[Khatam an-Nabiyyin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khatam_an-Nabiyyin "Khatam an-Nabiyyin")_ , meaning the last of the prophets ever sent by Allah ("seal of the prophets"). 
Based on the Muslim figure of the [Mahdī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahd%C4%AB "Mahdī"), the ultimate savior of humankind and the final Imām of the Twelve Imams, [Ali Muhammad Shirazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A1b "Báb"), later known as "the Báb", created the Bábí movement out of the belief that he was the gate to the Twelfth Imām. This signaled a break with Islam and started a new religious system, [Bábism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B%C3%A1bism "Bábism"). However, in the 1860s a split occurred, after which the vast majority of Bábís who considered Mirza Husayn `Ali or [Bahá'u'lláh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%27u%27ll%C3%A1h "Bahá'u'lláh") to be the Báb's spiritual successor founded the Baháʼí Movement, while the minority who followed Subh-i-Azal came to be called [Azalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azalis "Azalis").[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-19) The Baháʼí division eventually became a full-fledged religion in its own right, the [Baháʼí Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bah%C3%A1%CA%BC%C3%AD_Faith "Baháʼí Faith"). In comparison to the other Abrahamic religions - Judaism, Christianity and Islam - the number of adherents for Baháʼí faith and other minor Abrahamic religions are not very numerous. 
Out of the three major Abrahamic faiths, [Christianity and Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_Judaism "Christianity and Judaism") are the two religions that diverge the most in theology and practice. 
The historical interaction between [Islam and Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_and_Judaism "Islam and Judaism") started in the 7th century CE with the origin and [spread of Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam "Spread of Islam"). There are many common aspects between Islam and Judaism, and as Islam [developed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Islam "History of Islam"), it gradually became the major religion closest to Judaism. In contrast to Christianity, which originated from interaction between ancient [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece "Ancient Greece"), [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_empire "Roman empire"), and [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew "Hebrew") cultures, Judaism is very similar to Islam in its fundamental religious outlook, structure, jurisprudence and practice.[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-20) There are many traditions within Islam that originate from traditions which are recorded in the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible "Hebrew Bible") or which stem from post-biblical Jewish traditions. These practices are known collectively as the _[Isra'iliyat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isra%27iliyat "Isra'iliyat")_.[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-21)
The historical interaction between [Christianity and Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_and_Islam "Christianity and Islam") connects fundamental ideas in Christianity with similar ones in Islam. Islam accepts many aspects of Christianity as part of its faith – with some differences in interpretation – and rejects other aspects; for example, it simplifies complex Christian [Christological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christology "Christology") teaching and completely avoids the potential pitfalls of [Trinitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinitarianism "Trinitarianism"). Islam holds that the Quran is the final revelation from [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God "God") and a completion of all previous revelations, including the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible "Bible"). 
[Mandaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeism "Mandaeism"), sometimes also known as Sabianism (after the mysterious [Sabians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabians "Sabians") mentioned in the [Quran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quran "Quran"), a name historically claimed by several religious groups),[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-22) is a [Gnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism#Mandaeism "Gnosticism") and [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism#Mandaeism "Monotheism") religion.[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-Mandaens-23): 4 [[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-24) [John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_the_Baptist "John the Baptist") is their chief prophet, and frequent [baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism#Mandaean_Baptism "Baptism") is part of their core practices. According to most scholars, Mandaeism originated sometime in the first three centuries CE, either in southwestern [Mesopotamia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamia "Mesopotamia") or in the [Syro-Palestinian area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levant "Levant").[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-auto-25) However, some scholars take the view that Mandaeism is older and dates from pre-Christian times.[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-26) Mandaeans assert that their religion predates Judaism, Christianity, and Islam as a monotheistic faith.[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-27) Mandaeans believe that they descend directly from [Shem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shem "Shem"), [Noah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noah "Noah")'s son,[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-auto2-28): 182  and also from John the Baptist's original disciples.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-DrowerHaranGawaita-29)
#### Iranian religions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Comparative_religion&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Iranian religions")]
Main articles: [Proto-Indo-Iranian religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-Iranian_religion "Proto-Indo-Iranian religion") and [Iranian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_religions "Iranian religions")
Several important religions and religious movements originated in [Greater Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Iran "Greater Iran"), that is, among [speakers of various Iranian languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_peoples "Iranian peoples"). They include [Ætsæg Din](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%86ts%C3%A6g_Din "Ætsæg Din"), [Manichaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manichaeism "Manichaeism"), [Mazdakism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazdakism "Mazdakism"), [Mithraism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mithraism "Mithraism"), [Ahl-e Haqq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarsanism "Yarsanism"), [Yazdanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazdanism "Yazdanism"), [Zoroastrianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroastrianism "Zoroastrianism"), and [Zurvanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurvanism "Zurvanism"). 
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f9/Faravahar-Gold.svg/250px-Faravahar-Gold.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Faravahar-Gold.svg)Depiction of the [Faravahar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faravahar "Faravahar"), a popular symbol for Zoroastrianism
Perhaps one of the most important religions that originated in Iran was Zoroastrianism. While not, properly speaking, a world religion, it became widespread in the Iranian cultural sphere, especially through the [Achaemenid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Achaemenid_Empire "Achaemenid Empire") and [Sasanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sasanian_Empire "Sasanian Empire") Empires. It went into decline alongside many Iranian religions with the rise of Islam and the establishment of [Caliphates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate "Caliphate"). The religion still survives today in small numbers, with a particularly notable example being the [Parsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parsis "Parsis") in India and Pakistan.[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-30)[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:0-31)
Scholars have often noted the similarities between Zoroastrianism and the Abrahamic religions, especially Christianity. They've particularly noted this due to the historic relationship between the Jews and the Zoroastrian Persian Empires, as well as the relationship between Greek philosophy, Persia, and Christianity. They've debated whether Zoroastrianism played an influential role in shaping these religions. 
Key areas of concern include a shared sense of duality between the forces of good and evil, or light and darkness. In addition to this, there is a shared belief in the resurrection of the dead as well as an emphasis on free will and the moral responsibility of mankind. These are seen by some as having influenced the three major Abrahamic faiths as well as Gnosticism and the Baháʼí Faith.[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:0-31)
Manichaeism is another Iranian faith that shares many similarities with Zoroastrianism. Notably, Manichaeism shares a belief in the prophethood of [Zoroaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoroaster "Zoroaster") while also acknowledging the prophethood of Gautama Buddha and Jesus Christ. It shares a dualist cosmology that pits good and light against evil and darkness, with an adversary to oppose the benevolent God.[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-32) Furthermore, Manichaeism and [Mandaeism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandaeism "Mandaeism") also share a common belief in many of the figures and stories of the Abrahamic faiths, which has raised questions of influences and origins.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-33)
### Indian religions

In comparative religion, Indian religions refer to all the faiths that originated in [South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asia "South Asia"). It is believed that "the kinship of the religions of India stems from the fact that Jains, Buddhists, and Sikhs look back to Hinduism as their common mother."[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-34)[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:3-13)

[Adi Shankaracharya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adi_Shankaracharya "Adi Shankaracharya") was an early 8th century philosopher and theologian[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-35) who consolidated the doctrine of [Advaita Vedanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advaita_Vedanta "Advaita Vedanta").[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-FOOTNOTESharma1962vi-36)[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-FOOTNOTEComans2000163-37)[[note 1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-38) [Gautama Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha "Gautama Buddha") is mentioned as an [Avatar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avatar "Avatar") of [Vishnu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnu "Vishnu") in the [Puranic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puranic "Puranic") texts of Hinduism. Like Hindus, Gandhi himself did not believe Buddha established a non-Hindu tradition. ### East Asian or Taoic religions
[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Comparative_religion&action=edit&section=7 "Edit section: East Asian or Taoic religions")]
[![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/07/Tao.svg/250px-Tao.svg.png)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Tao.svg)The [Chinese character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_character "Chinese character") depicting _[Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao "Tao")_ , the central concept in Taoism
Main articles: [East Asian religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_religions "East Asian religions") and [East Asian Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_Buddhism "East Asian Buddhism")
See also: [Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao "Tao") and [De (Chinese)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_\(Chinese\) "De \(Chinese\)")
A Taoic religion is a religious philosophy that centers on the [East Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian "East Asian") concept of _[Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao "Tao"), meaning_ "The Way". This forms a large group of eastern religions, including [Chen Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chen_Tao_\(UFO_religion\) "Chen Tao \(UFO religion\)"), [Chondogyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheondoism "Cheondoism"), [Confucianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucianism "Confucianism"), [I-Kuan Tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I-Kuan_Tao "I-Kuan Tao"), [Jeung San Do](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeung_San_Do "Jeung San Do"), [Shintoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shintoism "Shintoism") and [Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism "Taoism"). In large parts of East Asia, [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism "Buddhism") has taken on some taoic features. 
Tao can be roughly defined as the flow of the universe or the force behind the natural order. It is believed to influence the balance and order of the cosmos and is closely associated with nature, as it is thought that nature exemplifies the Tao. 
The flow of _[qi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qi "Qi")_ , as the essential energy of action and existence, is often compared to the universal order represented by the _Tao_. Following the _Tao_ is also associated with a "proper" attitude, morality and lifestyle. This concept is intimately tied to the complex idea of _[De](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_\(Chinese\) "De \(Chinese\)")_ , or literally "virtue" or "power". _De_ is the active expression of _Tao_. 
Taoism and [Chan Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Buddhism "Chan Buddhism") for centuries had a mutual influence on each other in China, Japan, Korea, and Taiwan. These influences were inherited by [Zen Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zen_Buddhism "Zen Buddhism") when Chan Buddhism arrived in Japan and adapted as Zen Buddhism. 
Despite the geographical distance that would seemingly preclude any direct influence, some scholars have historically observed similarities between traditional Chinese religious beliefs and Christianity. This was noted by Jesuit missionaries who became known as [figurists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figurism "Figurism"). Figurists promoted the idea that the ancient Chinese knew the truth of Christian revelation and that many of the figures described in Chinese texts are actually figures and concepts from Christianity. Noted parallels include shared [flood myths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Flood_\(China\) "Great Flood \(China\)"), similarities between [Fuxi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuxi "Fuxi") and [Enoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch "Enoch"), as well as parallels between Christ and the sages.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:1-42) There is also a noted similarity between the Tao being "the Way" as well as Christ claiming to be "the Way".[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-43)
While scholarship rejects this view today, it was a notable view in the history of comparative religion. These beliefs were ultimately opposed and disavowed by the Catholic Church in the 18th century.[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:1-42)
The introduction of Nestorian Christianity to China under the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty "Tang dynasty") also led to increasing similarities between Chinese Buddhism and [Nestorian Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_the_East "Church of the East"). Christians began using Buddhist and Taoist concepts to explain their faith. During this time the [Jingjiao documents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jingjiao_Documents "Jingjiao Documents") were created, sometimes called [sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutra "Sutra") that demonstrated the blending of Christianity with Buddhism.[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-44)[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:2-45) Nestorian Christians and Buddhists formed a monastic tradition that furthered the similarities. This fusion was so pronounced that when [Emperor Wuzong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Wuzong_of_Tang "Emperor Wuzong of Tang") of the Tang dynasty began [persecuting Buddhists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huichang_Persecution_of_Buddhism "Huichang Persecution of Buddhism") in the 9th century, he claimed Christianity was merely a heresy of Buddhism rather than a distinct religion.[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-46) This conflation contributed to the collapse of Nestorian Christianity in China during the Buddhist persecutions.[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_religion#cite_note-:2-45)

### Buddhism
